**THE QUIET VERDICT**

Volume 1: The Classroom

Chapters 1 to 5 (Part 1 of Volume 1)



## Arc 1 Recap: The Classroom

*Aster Institute ranks its students by a hidden point system, and the ones who fall to zero are removed.*

*Ren Aizawa, second in his class, does not show emotion and does not explain himself.*

*Someone, somewhere, has begun to decide who lives.*

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. Each use is recorded in the ledger, in the writer's own handwriting, and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it.*



# Chapter 1: The Blank Line

Those who remember that spring tend to remember the same detail about the boy: he did not look nervous.

Aster Institute stood at the top of a long, curving road, and every first-year who climbed it carried the same expression. Pride, fear, or a mixture of both. Four hundred new students passed through the iron gate that April morning, and the cherry trees along the drive shed their petals onto shoulders that were suddenly very aware of themselves.

Ren Aizawa walked through the gate at an even pace. He did not look up at the clock tower like the others. He did not check his phone. He carried a plain black bag, and his uniform was neat without being careful. A small, polite half-smile sat on his face, as if it had been placed there that morning and he had not thought about it since.

"Class 1-C is on the third floor," said a woman at the registration desk. She did not look up from her list. "Your registration form, please."

He handed it over. She scanned it, frowned, and tapped one line with her pen.

"The name field for your birth record is blank."

"Yes," said Ren.

"It's required."

"I was told it wasn't."

She looked at him then, properly, for the first time. Whatever she expected to find, it wasn't there. A pleasant face. Dark, tidy hair. Eyes that returned her gaze without any weight at all.

Another woman leaned over from the next desk and murmured something. The first one sighed, stamped the form, and slid it back.

"We have it on file," she said. "Go on up."

"Thank you," said Ren.

He climbed the stairs.

---

Class 1-C had forty seats arranged in five rows of eight. By the time the homeroom teacher arrived, thirty-nine of them were filled, and the fortieth was waiting for him by the window.

The boy in the neighboring seat glanced at him and then back at the glass. He was slight and pale, with the kind of stillness that makes a person easy to overlook. He did not introduce himself. Ren sat down, set his bag on the floor, and folded his hands on the desk.

The teacher, Mr. Kurata, entered at exactly 9:00. He was a heavy, tired-looking man with reading glasses pushed into his hair, and he did not greet them. He wrote one word on the board.

POINTS.

"This is the only thing that matters at Aster," he said. "I will explain it once."

He explained it once.

Every student began with one hundred Merit Points. Every class began with a shared pool of four thousand Class Points. Both numbers rose and fell according to a monthly review of performance, behavior, and what Mr. Kurata called "conduct under pressure." Standings were posted on the first of each month. At the end of the year, the four first-year classes were ranked against each other. The top class received priority placement in the school's partner institutions, which was another way of saying their careers. The bottom class lost its pool.

"And any student whose Merit Points reach zero," Mr. Kurata said, "is removed from the school. Not suspended. Removed."

No one spoke. The room had gone very quiet.

"You have been told this institution develops the people who will run the country. That is not a boast. It is a description of what happens to people who are good at this. Some of you will discover that you are not." He picked up the chalk again. "Entrance exam rankings."

He wrote two names at the top of a list.

1. Himeno Saya, 495 / 500 2. Aizawa Ren, 494 / 500

A girl in the front row turned in her seat. She had long, straight hair, a straight spine, and eyes that were looking directly at him. It was not a friendly look. It was the look of a person who has just found a stone in her shoe.

Ren returned the gaze for exactly as long as courtesy required, and then he looked back at the board.

"Take your seats for orientation," said Mr. Kurata. "Your first review is in twenty-six days."

---

The campus tour was led by a second-year named Fujimori, who walked backward through the corridors with the ease of someone who had given the same speech forty times.

"The institute was founded forty years ago," he said. "Nobody remembers by whom. The foundation papers are sealed, which is a very Aster thing to do. The name is from the flower. You'll see it everywhere. The gate, the stationery, the pins you'll be given at graduation."

He pointed at the wall. A small purple flower had been worked into the stone above the stairwell door, its petals radiating from a pale yellow center.

"Fun fact. The founder never explained why he chose it. Every year a first-year asks, and every year the answer is the same, which is that he said it was a promise to someone. He didn't say who."

"That's it?" asked a girl.

"That's it." Fujimori shrugged. "There's a rumor. There's always a rumor. Last year's rumor was that the flower was for a student who never graduated. The year before, it was for a dog."

The group laughed. It moved on down the corridor, toward the north library and the gymnasium and the long glass walkway to the tower.

At the back, one student paused beneath the carved flower for exactly as long as it took to read it. His face did not change. His eyes did not linger. Then he caught up with the others, and the tour continued, and he asked no questions about the founder, the promise, or the dog.

---

The orientation ran until noon. There were forms to fill, locker codes to memorize, a list of forbidden areas, a tour of the north library. Ren did all of it with the same pleasant efficiency. He asked no questions. When the class representative was nominated, a nervous boy named Sakaki Rio was pushed forward by his friends and accepted with the face of a man agreeing to be shot. Ren clapped when the others clapped.

At lunch he sat alone, and he ate quickly, and he left a clean tray. Several students watched him do it. It is a peculiar thing, that unnoticed people draw so many eyes. By the end of the first day, three separate groups had decided independently that he was "scary," "boring," and "probably a genius," in that order.

When the last bell rang, he returned to his desk to collect his bag.

There was a folded piece of notebook paper on the seat.

He did not look around. He did not hesitate. He unfolded it and read it standing, and the whole class, had it been watching, would have seen nothing at all change on his face.

The handwriting was small, neat, and slanted left.

*You missed Question 48 on purpose.*

Ren folded the paper into four. He put it in his breast pocket. Then he picked up his bag, said "Goodbye" to the empty room, and walked out into the corridor.

Behind him, in the last row by the window, a boy who had not yet left his seat kept his eyes on the glass and did not move.

The handwriting was small and neat and slanted to the left. It matched nobody the boy had yet seen write.

It was not until much later that anyone understood how carefully that had been arranged.



# Chapter 2: Second Place

Himeno Saya found him at the vending machines on the second morning.

She did not say hello. She stood a little too close and a little too straight, and she held a carton of tea she was not drinking.

"494," she said.

"Good morning," said Ren.

"You got 494. The exam's only wrong answers were on the physics section, where nobody gets full marks, and the one in the essay, where I lost a point for style. You lost a point on question 48." She lifted her chin. "It's a multiple-choice question about thermal conduction. A child could get it right."

"I misread it."

"You didn't misread it. Your exam sheet had a perfect trail of pencil marks through the answer you eventually rejected." She said it quickly, like someone who had practiced. "You chose correctly and then you changed it."

"Did you look at my exam sheet?"

"Everyone looked at everyone's. Mr. Kurata left them in the staff room."

"That sounds like a mistake on his part," said Ren.

She stared at him. A muscle ticked in her jaw. The vending machine hummed behind them, and a first-year in the corridor laughed at something his friend said.

"Why?" Himeno asked.

"Why what?"

"Why would a person who could finish first choose second?"

Ren considered the question. It looked, to an observer, like genuine consideration. "I don't know," he said. "I may have been tired."

"You weren't tired."

"Then I don't know."

He bought a can of coffee. He said, "Please excuse me," and he walked away down the corridor with his usual pace, and Himeno Saya stood where she was holding a carton of tea she would not finish.

---

The second day of class was a day of small discoveries.

The class learned that Mr. Kurata did not repeat himself. It learned that the cafeteria's cheapest meal cost five Merit Points a day, which was not a lot but was not nothing. It learned that every student carried a school tablet that displayed their own point total and no one else's. And it learned, because a loud boy in the third row insisted on making sure everyone knew, that Tachibana Gen had already found the loophole.

"It's simple," Tachibana said at lunch. He was tall, wide-shouldered, with a smile that was never wasted on anything less than an audience. "The review doesn't just count behavior. It counts *reports.* Anyone who reports something gets credit. And anyone who gets reported on loses points." He leaned back in his chair. "So you've got two kinds of people in this school. The ones who report, and the ones who get reported."

Several students laughed. A few didn't.

"I've been collecting," Tachibana said. "Just things people have said. Just things people have done. It's amazing what's on a phone."

Sakaki Rio, the class representative, was at the next table. He had stopped eating.

"I'm not saying I'll use it," Tachibana said gently. "I'm saying it's an *asset.* And in a place like this, assets are worth money."

At the window seat, the pale boy was watching the whole speech without turning his head. Two tables away, Ren Aizawa ate his lunch at an even rhythm. He did not look at Tachibana once.

---

After lunch, Mr. Kurata sorted the class into groups of five and gave each group an envelope.

"Forty minutes," he said. "One task. The group with the best result earns ten Merit Points each. The group with the worst loses five."

Ren's group was Himeno Saya, Sakaki Rio, Mio Hanazono, and the pale boy from the window seat, who gave his name only when Mio asked.

"Kuze," he said. "Minato."

The task was a logic puzzle printed on both sides of a single sheet. Seven suspects, six statements, one liar. Himeno read it in ninety seconds and began writing at once, in the rapid, slanted way of someone who has never been wrong about a puzzle in her life.

"It's the gardener," she said.

"It can't be the gardener," Sakaki said. "The second statement..."

"It's the gardener."

"But the second statement says..."

"Sakaki-kun," said Ren.

Everyone turned. He had said it softly, in the voice of a man asking someone to pass the salt.

"I think Himeno-san is right. But I think we should check the second statement, because if we're wrong, the group loses points, and if we show our work, Mr. Kurata may award partial credit." He looked at Sakaki with great kindness. "Would you read it aloud?"

Sakaki read it aloud. It took him a minute. In that minute he found the flaw in his own objection, caught it, and said, brightening, "Oh. Oh, it *is* the gardener."

"Good work," said Ren.

"I only..."

"You found the problem. It was a good catch."

Sakaki glowed. Himeno, who had solved the entire puzzle alone and would have been furious to be thanked for it, found that she had been thanked instead for letting someone else finish it. She looked at Ren. He was writing his own name at the top of the sheet in small, even letters.

They finished in twenty-six minutes. They won. Ten points each.

"That was efficient," Himeno said afterward, in a low voice, while the others gathered their things.

"Thank you."

"I solved it."

"You did."

"And then you spent four minutes arranging for Sakaki to feel like he had."

"Did I?" said Ren.

"Yes."

"I'm glad it worked out," he said. "He needed it."

It was true, and that was the worst part. She walked back to her seat unable to name a single thing he had done wrong.

At the window, Kuze Minato had already opened his notebook.

---

That evening, the pale boy approached Ren in the dormitory corridor.

"Kuze Minato," he said. He had a soft voice, one that did not carry. "I sit next to you."

"I know," said Ren. "Hello."

"I wrote the note."

"Which note?"

Kuze studied him for a moment. There was nothing in his own face either, but it was a different kind of nothing. His was the nothing of a person who spent most of his life being politely invisible, and Ren's was something else.

"You said that very smoothly," Kuze said.

"I'm sorry. I didn't mean to."

"I'm not going to tell anyone."

"There's nothing to tell."

"No," Kuze agreed. "There isn't. That's what's interesting about it." He bowed his head slightly. "I wanted to see how you'd respond. I won't ask again."

"Thank you," said Ren. "Is there anything else?"

"No."

"Then good night."

He walked past him toward his room. Kuze watched him go, and he did not go to his own door for a long time.

---

The next morning, the class discovered that Tachibana had posted something to the school's internal messaging board. A single line, timestamped 6:02 a.m.

*Meeting at the north stairwell tomorrow at 7. Come alone, if you want to buy your points back.*

Under it, a list of eleven student numbers.

The list included Sakaki Rio.

It included Himeno Saya.

And at the very bottom, in the position where a man leaves the item he is most pleased with, it included the number of a student who had joined the class two days earlier and had said almost nothing to anyone.

Student 251-0-1. Aizawa Ren.



# Chapter 3: The Crossing

Mio Hanazono noticed the car three seconds before it hit.

It was a gray delivery truck, an ordinary one, driving down the hill road at a speed that was slightly too high for a school zone. She was halfway across the pedestrian crossing outside the Aster gate, and her bag was slipping off her shoulder, and she was thinking about nothing in particular. That is the part she remembered afterward. She had been thinking about nothing, and then the truck was there, and the horn was a long wail that seemed to come from inside her own skull.

She did not scream. She did not move.

Someone caught her by the back of the collar and pulled.

The truck went past so close that the wind of it snapped her hair across her face. It braked hard, fishtailed, and stopped at an angle fifteen meters down the road. The silence afterward was enormous.

Mio found herself sitting on the pavement with her hands scraped raw. A boy was standing over her. He was not breathing hard. His uniform sleeve had a long dark smear of road dirt on it, and he was looking at her with an expression she would try, for months, to describe to herself.

It was courteous. It was attentive. It was the face of someone checking that a package had arrived undamaged.

"You're not hurt," he said. It was not a question.

"I...I'm..." She looked at her hands. "I think I'm fine."

"That's good."

Behind them the students at the gate had begun to shout. Someone was calling for a teacher. Mio's eyes filled, in a long overdue way, and she said, "You saved my life," in a cracked voice.

"The truck stopped," said Ren. "It was probably going to anyway."

"It wasn't."

"Then I'm glad I was nearby."

He extended a hand. She took it, and he pulled her up with exactly the amount of force required and not a gram more.

"Aizawa-kun," she said. She recognized him now: the boy from the second row, the one who got second place. "Thank you."

"You're welcome," he said, and he picked up her bag and handed it to her.

That was all. He turned and walked through the gate, brushing off his sleeve.

---

The school nurse wrapped Mio's palms in gauze and told her, firmly, to rest. Mr. Kurata took the statements himself.

He sat across from Ren in the nurse's office with a notepad on his knee and the weary look of a man who had hoped the term would be quieter.

"Tell me what happened."

"I was walking up the hill road at 8:12," said Ren. "I was about twenty meters behind Hanazono-san. The delivery truck came around the bend at roughly sixty kilometers an hour. The limit in the school zone is thirty. Hanazono-san was in the crosswalk. She stopped. I reached her at 8:13 and pulled her back by the collar."

"8:12."

"Yes."

"You noted the time."

"I check my watch when I walk. It's a habit." He lifted his wrist. A plain black watch. "I'm told it's tedious."

Mr. Kurata wrote it down. He wrote down the speed, the angle, the location. It was the most precise witness statement he had ever received from a sixteen-year-old, and it did not sit quite right with him.

"You didn't hesitate."

"I'm sorry?"

"When you saw the truck. You didn't freeze, like most people do."

"I think I did," said Ren. "I just didn't take very long."

The teacher looked at him over his glasses. Ren looked back, pleasantly. There was not a flicker of anything behind his eyes, not triumph, not fear, not the shaky aftermath of a near-miss.

"Is there anything else, sensei?"

"No." Mr. Kurata closed the notepad. "You may go. And Aizawa."

"Yes?"

"It was well done."

"Thank you," said Ren, and bowed.

When the door closed, Mr. Kurata remained in the chair for a while. He turned to a blank page in his notepad and wrote a single sentence, and then he tore the page out and folded it very small and put it in his breast pocket, the way one hides a thing from oneself.

*He was not breathing hard.*

---

By noon, three versions of the story had circulated through the school.

In the first, Ren Aizawa had thrown himself in front of a truck.

In the second, he had slapped it aside like a mosquito.

In the third, which was the closest to true, he had simply been standing where he happened to be standing, and had reacted. It was this version that Himeno Saya did not believe.

She went to the crossing at lunch.

There was a skid mark on the road. She measured it with her stride. Thirty-one steps from the crosswalk to the final stop. A truck traveling at the speed required to leave a mark that long would have had no time to brake cleanly, and yet the skid began *after* the point where the driver would have first seen Mio.

She stood there for several minutes.

"Excuse me."

It was a police officer, a young one, writing in a notebook near the crosswalk.

"Did you find the driver?" Himeno asked.

"We're looking." He rubbed the back of his neck. "He ran. Just walked out of the cab and left. Rental vehicle, booked three days ago online. Paid in cash at a convenience store, no ID. The name on the contract doesn't match anyone in the system."

"Three days ago."

"Yes."

"The first day of orientation."

He looked at her. "I'm sorry?"

"Nothing," said Himeno.

She walked back to the school. Her expression did not change, but the hand that had held the tea carton two days ago now held her phone, and she typed a single note into it.

*Day 1 entrance ceremony: rental booked. Day 3: truck. Who benefits?*

She stared at it for a long time.

Then she added: *Who was near the crossing?*

And then, after a pause that was longer than she would have admitted, she added a third line.

*Aizawa.*

---

In the dormitory common room that evening, Mio Hanazono found him by the tea machine.

"I wanted to thank you again."

"There's no need."

"I brought something." She held out a small paper bag. "It's from the shop by the station. They're, um. Not great. But I didn't know what else..."

Ren looked at the bag. He accepted it with both hands, in the formal way, and bowed his head very slightly.

"Thank you," he said. "That's kind."

"Do you...do you not like sweets?"

"I like them fine."

"You didn't smile."

"I did," said Ren. "A little."

She laughed in a surprised way, and then she stopped, because she realized that he had not laughed with her. He was only waiting, patiently, until the sound was over. It was not unkind. There was no judgment in it. But for a moment, she felt as if she had been addressing a very polite wall.

"Well," she said. "Good night, Aizawa-kun."

"Good night, Hanazono-san."

She left. In the empty common room, the boy stood holding the paper bag and looking at nothing, and the tea machine beeped, once, and finished.

Somewhere on the third floor, a door closed.

In the north stairwell of the school building, in the dark, a phone screen lit up and displayed a single reply to a single message.

*Come alone.*

*7 a.m.*

*I'll bring the points.*



# Chapter 4: The Price

The evening before, in the dormitory common room, Ren Aizawa had asked Mio Hanazono for a favor.

"I'm sorry to bother you," he said. "I'm not good with the school's board. The notifications don't load on my tablet. Would you mind if I used yours for a moment?"

"Of course," said Mio. She handed it over at once. She was still glowing a little from the crossing, from the sweets, from the novelty of being the person someone relied on.

"Thank you." He tapped through three screens with slow, careful fingers, like a man trying to learn a language. "It seems I can't find the settings. Could you show me?"

"Here. You press the little gear."

"Ah."

"Then there."

"I see." He gave the tablet back. "Thank you very much. That was a great help."

The whole thing took about ninety seconds. Mio would later try to recall it and discover that she could remember only the warmth of it. The tablet had gone back into her bag, and she had gone to bed, and she had not looked at the board again until the next morning.

If she had, she might have noticed that her account had posted a reply at 9:41 p.m.

It was a single line, addressed to Tachibana Gen's public meeting notice, and it consisted of three words.

*I'll come alone.*

---

The north stairwell at Aster Institute was a concrete tube nine floors tall. It was used mostly by cleaning staff and by students who did not want to be found. At seven in the morning, it smelled of floor polish and cold air.

Tachibana Gen arrived first.

He was eight minutes early, which he liked. Tachibana believed in being early the way other people believe in luck. He stood on the fourth-floor landing with his hands in his pockets and watched the stairs below, and he was smiling.

Eleven names on a list. Eleven people who would pay. Five had already answered, and four of those had agreed without arguing. The fifth had written back a single line that he'd have to think about.

*Aizawa Ren.*

He liked that one best.

Footsteps came up the stairs. Not hurried. Not heavy. A single set, at an even pace.

"You're alone," said Tachibana. "Good."

"Good morning," said Ren Aizawa.

He stopped one flight below, as if the distance had been agreed in advance. He held a plain black bag in one hand, and the other hung loose at his side.

"You didn't bring the points," said Tachibana.

"No. That isn't how it works. The system doesn't permit direct transfer."

"Then what are you doing here?"

"I came to listen," said Ren. "You wrote that you'd found something about me."

Tachibana grinned. It was a real grin, the glad kind. He took out his phone, thumbed it open, and held it up so the screen faced the stairs. A video was playing, shaky and dim: a corridor, a figure at the edge of a window, the blur of a face turned toward a camera it did not know was there.

"The first night," Tachibana said. "Dormitory, third floor. You were out of your room at two in the morning. You went to the roof door. I don't know what you were doing up there, but the lock was broken afterward." He tilted his head. "That's a conduct violation. That's worth maybe forty points to the report. To me, I mean."

"I see."

"Or you pay me. Quietly."

"How much?"

"Thirty."

"That's reasonable," said Ren.

Tachibana's smile widened. Then it faltered, very slightly, because the boy on the stairs had not moved. He had not blinked. He had not shifted his weight. He was simply standing there with a courteous expression, as if the conversation had gone exactly as he had anticipated.

"What?" said Tachibana.

"Nothing. I'm checking the time."

"What for?"

"The lock on the roof door," said Ren. "I did break it. I broke it so that the school would have a record of an unlocked roof. It's in the incident log under the cleaning staff's name, and it's been repaired since."

"So?"

"So the video you have shows me opening a door that was already marked as defective. It isn't a violation. It's a witness to the school's own negligence."

Tachibana opened his mouth.

"You should also know," Ren continued, in the same quiet tone, "that your phone has been disconnected from the school's network since 6:45 this morning. Your recordings are saved locally. No one else has them. That was the part I came to confirm."

"What are you talking about?"

"I'm saying you came here alone," said Ren. "Which is what I asked you to do."

For a long moment there was no sound in the stairwell at all.

"You didn't ask me anything," said Tachibana.

"I did. Last night. Through the messaging board. You assumed the account was Hanazono-san's."

The color left Tachibana's face in a slow, even sheet. He took one step back and his heel touched the edge of the landing.

"Aizawa," he said.

"Yes."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing," said Ren. "I told you. I came to listen."

He turned and began to descend the stairs. His footsteps were measured and quiet. Tachibana stood on the fourth-floor landing with his phone in his hand and his back against the concrete wall, and for the first time in his life he was entirely, wordlessly afraid.

He did not know why.

That was the thing about Ren Aizawa. People never knew why.

---

At 7:40, Ren Aizawa was in the north library, in the study cubicle he had reserved the night before. He read from 7:41 to 9:00. Twelve students saw him there. One of them was Himeno Saya, who had arrived at 7:52 and took the cubicle across the aisle.

She looked at him over the top of her book for a long time. He turned a page.

At 9:02, a scream carried across the school.

The east stairwell, not the north. It was a cleaning staff member, a woman in her fifties, who had gone to open a window on the second-floor landing. She found Tachibana Gen at the foot of the steps. He was lying on his side, very neatly, as if he had lain down for a nap.

He did not wake up.

The police arrived within twelve minutes. The school was closed within fifteen.

The official finding was an accident.

The east stairwell camera had been offline for scheduled maintenance since the previous afternoon. The work order, which anyone could read, had been signed by the class representative of Class 1-C as part of a routine facilities form. His signature was perfectly genuine.

Sakaki Rio sat in the office afterward with his face in his hands. "I didn't know," he said, over and over. "I didn't read it. Someone handed it to me with a stack of others. I just signed them all."

"Who handed it to you?" Mr. Kurata asked.

"I don't remember. It was a first-year. I..." His voice cracked. "It was just a form."

And in the corner of the office, in a chair nobody had noticed him take, a pale boy named Kuze Minato wrote a single word in a small notebook.

*Stairwell.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he crossed it out and wrote the other one.

*Eight.*



# Chapter 5: Of Course

Aster Institute held its memorial assembly on the third day after the death.

The gymnasium was filled with four hundred students in black. The headmistress stood on the low stage, a small, upright woman named Aoi Tsukishiro, whose silver hair was pinned so tightly it looked like it had been drawn there. She spoke for six minutes. She did not raise her voice, and the gymnasium became quieter and quieter until it was nearly possible to hear the lights.

"We have lost a student," she said. "This school mourns him. But a school is also a machine, and a machine does not stop. In the interest of fairness, the monthly review will proceed as scheduled."

A ripple passed through the students. It was not outrage. It was a more complicated thing, closer to recognition.

"The Class Points assigned to Tachibana Gen will be redistributed among the remaining thirty-nine members of Class 1-C in equal shares. Thank you."

She bowed. She left the stage.

In the third row of Class 1-C, Sakaki Rio whispered, "She just divided his points like...like he was a bill."

"That's what he is now," said a girl nearby, and she did not sound as though she was joking.

At the end of the row, Ren Aizawa stood with his hands folded in front of him. His expression was solemn, correct, and entirely empty.

---

Sakaki Rio caught up with him in the corridor after the assembly. He was walking quickly, head down, and he almost collided with Ren before he looked up.

"Aizawa-kun! I'm sorry. I didn't see..."

"It's quite all right."

"I wanted to say..." He swallowed. "The teachers asked me about the form again. They think I did something wrong."

"You didn't."

"I know. But it doesn't feel like it." He stared at his shoes. "I signed it. It's my name. If somebody *wanted* the camera off, and I..."

"Sakaki-kun."

He stopped.

"Do you keep a record of what you sign?" asked Ren.

"A...a record?"

"A list. Dates, forms, who handed them to you. It would protect you."

"I don't. I never thought..."

"Then I think you should start." Ren reached into his jacket and took out a small, plain notebook, thin as a wallet. "I have a spare. You can have it."

Sakaki took it in both hands. His eyes were suddenly wet again. "Why are you being so kind to me?"

"You're the class representative," said Ren. "Someone should be."

Sakaki bowed, deeply, and he did not see that the boy he was bowing to had already turned toward the stairs. It had taken less than a minute. It had required no lie. And from that day forward, every form that passed through Sakaki's hands would be copied, dated, and filed with a careful, grateful neatness, in a notebook that Ren Aizawa had chosen and bought and handed over himself.

---

"They're calling it an accident."

Himeno Saya said it to the air, in the school roof garden, at the end of the day. Ren had not exactly been invited. He had come because she had asked him, in the corridor, in a voice that sounded like a verdict.

"Yes," said Ren.

"You don't think so?"

"I think the police are experienced. I think they'd know."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No, it isn't," Ren agreed. He stood with his back to the railing. Beneath them the school grounds spread out in the gold of late afternoon, the cherry petals long fallen, the clock tower casting its long shadow across the lawn. "May I ask what you think?"

Himeno turned to face him. She was very pretty, in a severe way, and she had clearly been awake too long.

"I think someone planned it," she said. "I think the stairwell camera was shut off on purpose, and I think Sakaki was used. I think the truck on the crossing was planned, and the rental, and the cash payment. I think there's a pattern."

"That's a lot of thinking."

"Yes."

"Do you have evidence?"

"No."

"Then it's a theory."

"It's a pattern." She said the word like a flag she had decided to plant. "And the only person who was in both places, or near them, is you."

"I was in the library," said Ren. "Twelve people saw me. You were one of them."

"I know."

"And at the crossing I pulled a girl out of the road."

"I know that too."

"Then what are you accusing me of?"

Himeno's hands tightened on the railing. For a moment it seemed she might say something more. Then she shook her head, once.

"Nothing," she said. "Not yet."

"Then I'd like to give you something," said Ren.

"What?"

"A better theory." He looked out over the grounds. "Suppose that Tachibana was not the only student collecting things. Suppose that someone else, someone who was on his list, had also been collecting. And suppose that person wanted to remove a threat before the first review."

Himeno stared at him. "You're saying a student did it."

"I'm saying it's a possibility. If I were the police, it's the first one I would examine."

"Who?"

"I can't know that." He turned and met her eyes, calmly, with that same small, polite smile. "But I think you will be able to find out. You're very capable."

She opened her mouth and closed it. It was, she realized, an astonishingly generous thing to say. It was also an astonishingly effective one. It had pointed her at an enormous target and left her no reason to look at the boy beside her.

"Is that all?" she said.

"Yes. Thank you for asking me."

He bowed his head and went to the stairs. She watched him go. When the door had closed behind him, she took out her phone and wrote, in the same small note she had made three days ago:

*He suggested I look at the students.*

She stared at that.

Then, slowly, she wrote underneath it:

*Why was he ready with a suggestion?*

That night, Class 1-C received a message on the school board.

It was addressed to everyone. It had no sender, no name, no avatar. It had been posted at 11:48 p.m. and was already being passed around the dormitory in whispers.

*Congratulations on the first review. Your class has dropped to third place.*

*Eleven students have lost points for conduct violations in the last forty-eight hours. The reports were filed anonymously.*

*One of them is Sakaki Rio.*

*You can still recover. The offer is open to anyone who comes to the roof at midnight and asks the right question.*

Mio Hanazono read it in her room with her knees drawn up to her chest. She read it twice. Then she got up, put on her coat, and went out into the corridor.

She knocked on one door.

It opened at once. He was dressed. He had not been asleep.

"Aizawa-kun," she said, "have you seen the board?"

"Yes."

"They want us to go up to the roof."

"I know."

"What do we do?"

Ren considered the question. He seemed, to Mio, to consider it in a way that was very kind.

"You should go back to your room," he said. "Lock the door. Don't answer anyone until morning. I'll handle it."

"You'll *handle* it?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"Of course," said Ren.

It was an odd answer, and she did not understand it. But he said it with such perfect confidence that she found herself nodding, and then she was back in the corridor, and the door was closed, and she had been sent away so gently that she had not noticed.

She walked to her own room. On the way she passed the stairwell, and she paused, and she looked down at the floor.

There was a small silver key lying on the landing.

She picked it up. It was cold and plain, with a number stamped into the handle. *312.*

It was a dormitory key.

She turned it over in her palm. Then she looked back at the door she had just left.

Room 312.

It was Ren's.

And it had been lying, she realized, on the landing outside his door, as if it had been dropped there for her to find.
