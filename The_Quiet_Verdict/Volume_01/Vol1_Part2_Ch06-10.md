## Arc 1 Recap: The Classroom

*Aster Institute ranks its students by a hidden point system, and the ones who fall to zero are removed.*

*Ren Aizawa, second in his class, does not show emotion and does not explain himself.*

*Someone, somewhere, has begun to decide who lives.*

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. Each use is recorded in the ledger, in the writer's own handwriting, and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it.*



# Chapter 6: Midnight on the Roof

At 11:20 p.m., a night guard named Mr. Taguchi was drinking tea in the east wing's security booth when a first-year student knocked softly on the window.

"Excuse me. I'm sorry to disturb you."

"Shouldn't you be in bed?" said Taguchi, but not unkindly.

"I couldn't sleep. I wanted to ask if the library is open." The boy glanced at the monitors, apparently by accident. "Oh. Do those cover the whole campus? That's impressive."

Taguchi, who was proud of his monitors, said they did, more or less.

"Even the roof?"

"There's a broken camera on the east stairs. Been waiting on a repair since Tuesday." He took a sip. "The rest are fine. I do a full loop at midnight, and again at two."

"That's very thorough."

"It's the job." He peered at the boy. "You're from 1-C, aren't you? The one who saved that girl?"

"I was nearby."

"Good lad." The old man smiled. "My wife says kids these days have no manners. I'll tell her about you."

"Please give her my regards. I hope she's well."

"She makes the best curry in the district," said Taguchi, warming. They talked for four minutes about curry, and the district, and a daughter who had gone to university in the city. When the boy left, bowing, the guard was in an excellent mood and had entirely forgotten that he had said *midnight.*

The boy walked down the corridor. At the junction he turned away from the library, toward the east stairs.

It was 11:27. He had twenty-five minutes before the guard's loop.

He knew it was twenty-five, because he had been told.

---

At 11:52 p.m., Ren Aizawa climbed the roof stairs of the school's east wing.

He had not been invited. He had not been asked. He had read an anonymous message on the school board, which promised recovered points to whoever came and asked the right question, and then he had put on a gray jacket and left his room.

The door at the top of the stairs was already open.

The roof of Aster Institute was a wide, flat expanse of concrete, edged with a low rail and lit only by the clock tower's glow. At the far side, with his back to the city lights, a tall young man in a third-year uniform was waiting. He held a tablet in one hand. He did not turn when the door opened.

"You're the only one who came," he said.

"Good evening," said Ren.

"Ogami Tetsu. Class 3-A." The older student finally faced him. He was good-looking in a hard-edged way, with a smile that was friendly the way a bank is friendly. "You've heard of me?"

"No."

"You will." Ogami tapped the tablet. "I run the market. A student with points to spare sells them, a student who needs them buys them, and I take a small fee for the privilege of making it safe. Every class has someone like me. Most of them are less polite."

"I see."

"The message tonight was a test. I wanted to see who was curious. You are the first first-year in three years to come up here without a friend."

"That seems unwise of the others."

Ogami laughed. It was a good laugh, a practiced one. "Now. The right question."

"I don't have one."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because someone is using your channel to threaten my class," said Ren. "And I assumed that you would want to know."

The laugh stopped.

It stopped slowly, like a machine winding down. Ogami's smile held, but something behind it moved, and Ren watched it happen with the same polite attention he gave to everything else.

"That's an interesting claim."

"The message was posted at 11:48. The account was created at 9:15. It's registered to a device in the dormitory, not the third-year building." Ren spoke quietly. "Someone borrowed your name. I think they want the market to look like it's targeting first-years, so that the headmistress will shut it down."

"Who?"

"I don't know. I thought you might."

Ogami was quiet for a long time. The wind lifted the edge of his jacket. Below them, the lawns lay silver under the tower light, and across the dark a single window in the girls' dormitory flickered and went out.

"There's a third-year in 3-B," Ogami said slowly. "Honda. He's been trying to break the market for a year."

"That sounds plausible."

"It's him."

"I can't be sure."

"I can." Ogami's grip on the tablet tightened. "Thank you for the warning. I won't forget it."

"Please don't mention my name."

"I don't think I need to," said Ogami. He studied Ren. "You're a strange one."

"I've been told."

"Go to bed, first-year."

"Good night," said Ren, and he left.

He descended the east stairs at an even pace. At the landing he stopped for exactly four seconds, looking at nothing, and then he continued down. Nobody was watching. The camera at that landing had been broken since Tuesday.

On the roof, Ogami Tetsu stood alone for another minute. Then he made one call.

He did not know it was the last call he would make with a clear conscience, or with any conscience at all.

---

In the staff office the next morning, Honda Ryuu stood with his fists clenched at his sides and his face the color of old paper.

"I didn't take them," he said. "I swear. I've never seen that bag."

"It was outside your locker," said the vice principal.

"Someone put it there!"

"Who?"

"I don't *know*! Ogami, maybe, it's the kind of thing he'd do! He hated me! I tried to shut down his market!"

"That's a very specific accusation," said the vice principal, without warmth, "for someone who claims to know nothing."

Honda opened his mouth. Closed it. He looked at the faces around him, the teachers, the clerk, the police officer in the corner. He saw nothing but a case that had already closed.

"I want a lawyer," he whispered.

Nobody answered. In the corridor outside, a first-year in a tidy uniform had paused to tie his shoe, and he did not look up as the door shut. When he straightened, he went about his day exactly as he had before, with a small, polite half-smile that had not changed since April.

---

In the morning, the class discovered that a first-year named Honda Ryuu from the neighboring dormitory had been caught by three teachers carrying forty-seven stolen tablets.

The tablets belonged to third-years. They had all been sold to him, the investigation concluded, by a student who needed the money.

That student had been Ogami Tetsu.

Everyone agreed it was a very tidy case. And the person who had arranged for forty-seven tablets to be left in a duffel bag outside Honda's locker, where a teacher could find them, was nowhere on any list.

---

That evening, in the north library, Himeno Saya reread what she had written that day.

*Honda: staff office, 8:50. Denied everything. Said it was Ogami.*

She had been in the corridor outside. She had heard it through the half-open door, the cracked, hollow, helpless voice of a boy who knew that nobody was listening. She had heard a hundred liars in her life, in a hundred classrooms, and every one of them had had a rhythm. They spoke too smoothly. They checked their audience. They rehearsed.

Honda Ryuu had done none of these things. He had simply said that he had never seen the bag, again and again, with the dull despair of a man repeating a true sentence to a wall.

She underlined the line twice.

*He sounded like he was telling the truth.*

Then, below it, in smaller letters, a thing she did not yet know how to ask.

*Who benefits from a boy nobody believes?*



# Chapter 7: Ogami's List

Ogami Tetsu was found in the old equipment room behind the gymnasium three days later.

The police were careful this time. They came in plainclothes, in twos, and they spoke to the headmistress in her office with the door closed. The official statement, read by a teacher at the morning assembly, took less than a minute. A third-year student had been found deceased. A note had been left. The school was cooperating fully, and counseling services were available.

The note, the whispers said, was two lines long.

*I have been used. I am sorry.*

By lunchtime it was all anyone talked about.

"He had a lot of enemies," someone said.

"He had a lot of debt," said someone else.

"The market's finished," said a third, in the hushed tone of a person reciting a death sentence for a business.

In the cafeteria, Ren Aizawa ate a small bowl of rice and a piece of grilled fish. He took each bite at the same pace. When the girl across from him, Mio Hanazono, said that she couldn't finish her lunch, he nodded and said he understood.

"Aizawa-kun," she said, very quietly. "Were you the one who talked to him? On the roof?"

He put down his chopsticks.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody told me. I just..." She fidgeted. "I saw you go out that night. I couldn't sleep. I saw you leave."

"You were awake at midnight."

"Yes."

"You should sleep more," said Ren gently.

"That's not an answer."

"No. I know." He looked at her for a moment. There was nothing in his face that she could name. It was courteous and it was complete. "I did go up to the roof. He asked to meet a first-year, and I was curious. We spoke for a few minutes. I told him I thought someone was impersonating him. He thanked me."

"Did he seem..."

"He seemed like a man who had a lot on his mind."

She nodded. Her eyes were shining, and she looked down quickly.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I shouldn't have asked."

"You're allowed to ask me things."

"Really?"

"Yes."

It was a kind thing to say, and it had the effect of closing the conversation. She did not ask anything else. When she left the table, her shoulders were a little looser than when she had come, and she did not notice that she had said nothing at all about what she had actually wanted to know.

---

The police retrieved Ogami's phone on the second day.

Detective Sato, a broad, patient man in a wrinkled suit, sat in the headmistress's side office with the device on the table and a cup of cold coffee at his elbow. He read the call log twice.

"Thirty-one calls in the last forty-eight hours," he said. "Twenty-four to creditors. Five to his mother. Two to numbers I can't match."

"Can you trace them?" asked the headmistress.

"One is a prepaid. Dead since the night he died." He scrolled. "The other is a school extension. The north library's front desk."

"The library?"

"At 12:19 on the night of the roof meeting. Duration, forty-one seconds."

"Who was at the desk?"

"Nobody. It closes at ten." Sato tapped the screen. "Someone forwarded the line. Probably from a dormitory landline. Which means that someone, at twenty past midnight, was talking to Ogami for forty-one seconds, in the dark, and chose a number that would show up as the school."

"What was said?"

"I'd love to know." He sat back. "You want my opinion? Most suicides, there's a pattern. Withdrawal. Giving things away. This boy was in debt up to his collar and had a case against him, sure. But he also called his mother at 4 a.m. and asked what she was making for dinner on Sunday."

The headmistress said nothing.

"He wasn't planning to die," said Sato. "He was planning dinner."

He lifted the note, sealed in its plastic sleeve, and held it to the light. The handwriting was Ogami's. The wording was a man's. But the second line sat slightly crooked, as if written by someone who had been told what to write and had taken his time with it.

*I have been used.*

"Who uses a boy and then asks him to sign it?" the detective murmured.

Nobody had an answer.

---

That afternoon, Himeno Saya made her move.

She found Sakaki Rio in the class representative's office, flipping through a folder of forms, and she shut the door behind her.

"Who gave you the maintenance order?"

"I told you, I don't remember."

"You told the teachers that." She sat across from him. "Now tell me."

Sakaki's hands shook. He was a gentle boy, round-faced, with the permanent look of someone expecting to be blamed. "It was a first-year," he said. "I'm almost sure. I saw the uniform."

"Which class?"

"I don't know."

"What did they look like?"

"Ordinary." He rubbed his eyes. "That's the thing. They looked ordinary. Short hair. Dark. They said, 'Representative Sakaki, I'm sorry to bother you, just a routine form.' And I said it's fine. And they bowed."

"They bowed."

"Very politely."

Himeno was silent for a moment.

"Sakaki-kun," she said, "do you know anyone in the class who bows very politely?"

"Half the class bows politely. That's how we were raised."

"Anyone who bows a little too correctly?"

He frowned. His brow creased, and for a second a thought almost surfaced. Then it sank again, the way a stone sinks into a pond, and he shook his head.

"I'm sorry. I can't."

Himeno stood up.

"It's all right," she said. "Thank you."

In the corridor, she stopped and breathed out. She hadn't expected to be so frightened, and she was angry at herself for the fear. She took out her phone and opened her notes.

Under *Day 3: truck* and *He suggested I look at the students*, she added a new line.

*Ogami: suicide. Sakaki: used. Tachibana: used. Ogami: "I have been used."*

And below that, in smaller letters:

*Everyone who dies was already being used by someone else first.*

She looked at it. A chill went down her back.

*Who is being used by whom?*

---

On the other side of the school, in the library, a pale boy at a corner desk opened a notebook with a cracked spine.

Kuze Minato had been keeping a list since the second day of class. The first page held a single word, underlined twice, and underneath it eight short entries. He had written them in a hand that slanted slightly to the left.

*1. Question 48.* *2. The truck.* *3. The rental, three days early.* *4. The camera on the east stairs.* *5. The form Sakaki signed.* *6. The tablets in Honda's bag.* *7. The roof. Camera broken.* *8. The note.*

He turned to a new page. He sat for a long time with his pen above the paper.

Then he wrote the ninth.

*9. Every one of these was arranged by someone who has never raised his voice.*

He closed the book.

He did not know yet that the man he was writing about had already read the first page.



# Chapter 8: The Third

The third death at Aster Institute was a girl.

Her name was Aoyagi Hina, a first-year in Class 1-C, and nobody had ever described her as anything but nice. She had been the kind of student who brought extra pencils. She had a small laugh and a shy way of speaking, and she sat three seats behind Ren Aizawa in the fourth row. She had recently started to eat lunch alone.

She was found on a Thursday morning at the base of the bell tower.

The school closed for two days. Parents came. A grief counselor was brought in. The newspapers began to use the word *pattern*, and then, a day later, a different word.

*Curse.*

Class 1-C returned on Monday to find that someone had left a single white flower on her empty desk.

Nobody spoke. Forty students, thirty-eight now, sat very straight and avoided each other's eyes.

Mr. Kurata entered at exactly 9:00 and did not write on the board.

"I will say this once," he said. His voice was rough. "The school's first-year classes will continue. The monthly review will continue. Counseling is mandatory for everyone in this room, and the school will schedule it."

He paused. For the first time, he looked every one of his forty-four years.

"What happened to her," he said, "was a tragedy."

He did not say whose.

---

The counselor's office smelled of lavender and photocopier toner.

Dr. Ishizaki had been at Aster for six years and had seen every kind of fear a teenager could produce. She kept a bowl of mints on the desk, a box of tissues on the left, and a clock on the wall behind the client's head, so that she could check it without being seen to.

"Please sit, Aizawa-kun."

"Thank you."

"This is just a conversation. You can say anything you like." She opened a folder. "How have you been sleeping?"

"Seven hours."

"Appetite?"

"Normal."

"How do you feel about Hina's death?"

"I'm very sorry that it happened," said Ren. "She was kind. She lent me a pencil in the first week."

Dr. Ishizaki wrote that down. It was a reasonable answer. It was an appropriate answer. In six years she had never heard a more appropriate one.

"Do you feel sad?"

"I think so."

"Angry?"

"No."

"Afraid?"

"No."

"That's unusual," she said gently. "Most of your classmates are."

"I'm told I'm calm by nature."

"Is there anything you do when you're upset? A habit, or a place you go?"

"I walk." He considered. "I find it helps to be somewhere high."

She wrote *high places* and then circled it, and then, with a faint sense of dislike for herself, crossed it out.

"Aizawa-kun, I want to ask you something honestly. You don't have to answer." She put down the pen. "Is there anything you're not telling me?"

He looked at her. It was an open look, patient and unhurried, the look of a boy who would wait as long as she needed. She felt the clock tick behind her and realized that she had been holding her breath.

"I'm sure there are many things I haven't told you," said Ren. "But nothing that would help."

"Help whom?"

"Anyone."

She laughed, a short, startled sound. He smiled with her, a little, in the right place, for the right length of time.

When he had gone she sat for ten minutes without moving. Then she opened the folder and wrote one line at the bottom of his page, in a hand that did not feel like her own.

*No signs of distress. Cannot say whether this is reassuring.*

---

At 10:15 that morning, Sakaki Rio stood up in the middle of a lesson.

"Excuse me, sensei," he said, in a voice thin as paper. "I think I should say something."

"Sit down, Sakaki."

"No. I'm the class representative. I think it's my job." His hands were clenched on the desk. "Two people from this class have died in the last month. If you add the third-year, three. They keep saying it's a coincidence. It isn't."

"Sakaki..."

"It isn't, and everyone knows it." He turned. His eyes were wet. "We're all scared. Nobody wants to say so, because saying so makes it real. But I'm saying it. I'm scared."

The room was very still.

And then, from the fifth seat in the second row, a quiet voice said, "So am I."

It was Mio Hanazono. She had stood up too. Her fingers shook against her skirt.

"So am I," said another student. Then another.

It spread. Within thirty seconds, almost the entire class was on its feet, a ragged line of frightened teenagers, some crying, some furious, some simply holding on to the person beside them. Mr. Kurata watched it with an expression that was hard to read.

In the second row, one student had not stood.

"Aizawa-kun," said Sakaki, wiping his face. "You too. Please."

Ren looked up.

He rose slowly. He smoothed the front of his jacket. He faced the class and said, in an even, gentle voice that carried to the back of the room:

"I'm not afraid."

A pause.

"But I'll stay with you."

It was such a plain sentence. There was no flourish in it, no promise. And the effect, in that silent room, was extraordinary. A girl in the third row let out a shaky breath. Sakaki's knees nearly gave out. Somebody started to laugh, a wet, helpless laugh, and was joined by another.

"We should stay together," said Ren. "The rest of the school treats us like a problem. We should treat each other like a class."

"Yes," said Sakaki. "Yes, that's..." He wiped his face again. "That's right."

"I would like to propose something," said Ren. He glanced at the teacher, who had not stopped him. "A rotation. No student walks alone after dark. We trade information. We look out for each other."

The class voted. It was unanimous.

At the back of the room, in the seat by the window, Kuze Minato said nothing. He watched the faces turned toward the boy at the front. They were wide and wet and utterly trusting.

Kuze did not vote.

He wrote one line, and he did not need to look at the notebook to know what it would be.

*He has just become the person they cannot imagine being wrong.*

---

On the first of the month, the standings were posted.

CLASS 1-A: 4,412 CLASS 1-C: 4,198 CLASS 1-B: 3,841 CLASS 1-D: 3,205

A week earlier, 1-C had been third.

Himeno Saya read the list standing in the main corridor. People moved around her in a current. She read it again, and she did not blink, and she did not breathe.

The class had gained points for "cooperative conduct." It had gained them for the rotation, which the school's evaluators found "exemplary." It had gained them for the reduced reports and the lower number of conduct violations. It had gained them, in other words, because two members of the class were dead, and the remaining thirty-eight had been so frightened that they had finally learned to behave.

*Every death raised our standing.*

She walked the length of the corridor. She stopped at the glass doors. In the reflection, she could see behind her a student at the far end of the hall, an unremarkable boy in a tidy uniform, who had stopped to read the rankings.

He looked up at her in the reflection. He bowed his head slightly.

Himeno did not return the bow.

Her phone buzzed. She looked at it.

It was a message from the headmistress's office. Four short lines.

*Please come to my office at four o'clock.*

*Bring no one.*

*We have a visitor you should meet.*

*It concerns the person you suspect.*



# Chapter 9: The Voice

The first broadcast from Ø aired at 9:00 p.m. on a Friday, on every news channel in the country at once.

The screen was black. A white letter appeared in the center, simple and clean.

Ø

Then a voice. It was synthetic, a low, even tone that had been scrubbed of age and gender and accent. It sounded patient. It sounded, strangely, amused.

"Good evening," it said. "I am called Ø. You may have heard the name. I am here because someone in this country has found a way to kill people without leaving a trace. I intend to find that person."

In the dormitory common room at Aster Institute, a hundred students had gathered around a single screen. Nobody spoke.

"To those who know something: please come forward. To those who are afraid: I understand. And to the one responsible," said the voice, "I would like you to know a few things."

A pause. A faint, almost musical hum.

"First. I will find you. Second. I do not think you are a monster. I think you are a person who has been given something extraordinary and has decided to use it. I would like to understand why."

Someone near the back whispered, "It's talking to him."

"Third," said Ø. "You are close to me. I do not mean that you are in this room. I mean that you are in a place I have already looked."

The screen went dark for one second. Then a line of text appeared, white on black.

*Of course.*

The broadcast ended.

In the hour that followed, it was replayed on four hundred screens. It was watched in the cafeteria of a hospital, where a nurse set down her tray. It was watched in a train carriage, over the shoulder of a stranger. It was watched by a retired detective in a flat in the north, who said aloud to an empty room that he had heard that voice before, and then, after a long pause, that he had not. Within the day, it had acquired a name on the internet, and a thousand theories, and a fan page.

No one noticed that, of the four hundred students who watched it at Aster Institute, one had been looking not at the screen but at the faces turned toward it.

---

The common room erupted. Students shouted over one another. A boy near the window began to argue loudly that it was fake, and a girl told him to shut up, and somewhere a tablet crashed to the floor.

In the second row, Mio Hanazono turned to the boy beside her and whispered, "What do you think?"

"It's very well produced," said Ren Aizawa.

"Aizawa-kun!"

"I'm sorry." He seemed to consider. "I think it's real. I think the person behind it has done this before. I think we should be careful."

"They said they're close. That they've already looked."

"Yes."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that they're telling us something and not telling us something else." He rose from his seat, smoothing his jacket. "Excuse me. I'd like to get some air."

"Of course," said Mio, automatically.

He paused at the door. He looked at her.

"Of course," he said, very quietly.

She would remember that later. It was a strange, soft echo, a phrase that belonged to someone else. At the time, she only smiled uncertainly, and he left.

---

Kuze Minato found Mio in the corridor outside the common room. She was standing against the wall with her arms wrapped around herself.

"Hanazono-san."

She startled. "Oh. Kuze-kun. I didn't see you."

"People don't." He said it without self-pity, like a fact about weather. "Can I ask you something?"

"I suppose."

"When Aizawa left. What did he say?"

"Nothing. He just said he wanted air." She frowned. "Why?"

"Did he say anything else?"

"He said 'of course.' After I said it." She shook her head. "It was funny. It's what the voice said. At the end."

Kuze was silent for so long that she began to feel uncomfortable.

"It's a common phrase," she said, a little defensively.

"Yes."

"He wouldn't copy something like that."

"No," Kuze agreed. "He wouldn't copy anything."

"Then what are you saying?"

"I'm not saying anything." He took the notebook from his pocket and held it against his chest like a hymn book. "I only collect things. I don't know what they mean yet."

"You collect things about Aizawa-kun?"

"About everyone."

"Why?"

He looked at her, and for the first time she saw his eyes properly. They were gray, and very tired, and much older than the rest of him.

"Because someone should be keeping the record," said Kuze. "And nobody else seems to want to."

He bowed and walked away down the corridor. Mio stayed where she was for a long time, hearing the two words in her head, in a voice that was not quite one voice but two, saying the same thing, in the same tone, from different ends of the same room.

---

At the top of the north tower, in the empty observatory that no one used, Ren Aizawa stood at the dark window and watched the city.

He was alone. He did not speak. He did not pace, or fidget, or look at his hands. The lights of the city lay below him, a spill of gold on black, and a thin wind came through a gap in the glass.

He stood there for eleven minutes.

Then he took out his phone. There was a single contact in it, with no name, only a number. He typed two lines.

*Ø exists. We were right to assume it.*

*Proceed with the first schedule.*

He sent it. He deleted it. He put the phone away.

In the reflection on the glass, for a moment, the outline of his face was visible. It was not a cruel face. It was not a joyful one. It was the face of a person reading a very long document and arriving, at last, at a sentence he had expected.

He descended the stairs.

---

In the headmistress's office, forty minutes earlier, Himeno Saya had met the visitor.

He was sitting in the visitor's chair with his legs crossed and his shoes off. He was a thin, hunched man in a rumpled shirt, with unruly black hair and wide, dark-ringed eyes. He held a spoon in one hand. A cup of tea stood on the desk beside him, and he had put an unreasonable quantity of sugar in it.

"This is Mr. Wataru," said the headmistress. "He represents the Task Force."

"Hello," said the man, without looking up.

"The Task Force is led by Ø," said Aoi Tsukishiro. "Mr. Wataru is the closest thing to a face that Ø has ever had."

"I'm only the liaison," Wataru said. "I'm not at all interesting."

Himeno sat down slowly. "Why am I here?"

"Because you've been keeping notes," said Wataru. He stirred the tea. "Because your notes are, frankly, very good. And because the person Ø is looking for is probably someone who read the same notes you did and drew the opposite conclusion."

"I don't understand."

"You will." He smiled, a thin, bright smile. "Ø would like to meet your class, Miss Himeno. Everyone. By video, next week."

"Why?"

"Because Ø has a theory about the person responsible," said Wataru. "And the theory is that he is very polite."

Himeno did not move.

"I haven't told you a name," said Wataru, mildly.

"No," said Himeno.

"Good." He sipped his tea. "That means we're both being careful."



# Chapter 10: Task Force Ø

The Task Force came to Aster Institute on a gray Tuesday in May.

There were nine of them, plus a van full of equipment, and they took over the old staff conference room on the second floor. Mr. Wataru gave the headmistress a list of requirements. Fourteen desks. Six monitors. A secure line, a shredder, and a kettle.

"A kettle," said Aoi Tsukishiro, in a flat voice.

"For the tea," said Wataru.

She arranged it. She was very good at arranging things.

By Wednesday the conference room had become a command post. Cables ran across the floor in fat black ropes. A wall had been covered with photographs, student IDs, floor plans, timelines. Nine people, six of them veteran detectives from three different agencies, bent over laptops and murmured into headsets. In the center of the table stood a single laptop, closed, with a white sticker on its lid.

Ø.

Nobody touched it. It was opened only twice a day, at nine in the morning and nine at night, and when it was, the room went silent, and a flat white line appeared on the screen and spoke.

Himeno Saya was allowed to attend one session. She sat in a corner, in a plain folding chair, and did not speak.

"Report," said the voice.

"The third victim, Aoyagi Hina, had been a witness to something," said a detective with gray hair. "She told two classmates she'd seen a student on the roof the night of the first meeting. She didn't name him."

"Why not?"

"We think she wasn't certain."

"What do you think?"

"I think she was certain and afraid."

A pause. The voice hummed softly, a rising, falling chord.

"Of course," said Ø.

The room relaxed by a degree. It was the habit of the thing. When Ø said those two words, it meant that the reasoning had landed, and the room could move on.

"I have a question," said the voice.

"Yes."

"The girl said 'roof.' Who has roof access at Aster?"

"Cleaning staff," said the detective. "Maintenance. Anyone with a broken lock."

"Who has a broken lock?"

A silence. Then the detective turned to Wataru, who was lying across a bench with a cushion over his face.

"The roof lock was reported broken on the first night of the term," Wataru murmured. "By a student. In the incident log."

"Which student?"

"Nobody. Anonymous. A cleaning staff member's name is on the entry." He lifted the cushion. "But there's a pattern in the log. Every time something is reported broken, there's a corresponding entry that closes the loop. The roof lock. The east camera. The stairwell door. They're all in the same handwriting."

"Whose?"

"The school's. Whoever manages the incident system. It's an automated form, but you can see the touch of the hand."

"Find the hand," said Ø.

Himeno's heart began to pound. She did not move. She did not breathe. In her lap, her fingers dug into the fabric of her skirt.

She had known exactly whose hand it was since the third week of class.

She said nothing.

She did not know why.

---

That afternoon, in the corridor outside the conference room, Wataru handed Himeno a paper cup of tea with five sugars already in it.

"I don't take sugar."

"Everybody takes sugar. They just don't know it." He sat down on a window ledge, crossed his legs, and stared at the ceiling. "Ask your question."

"I haven't got one."

"You've got forty. Pick one."

She hesitated. "How does Ø do it? Find people?"

"Ø doesn't find people. Ø finds *patterns.* People are what you find when you've stopped looking for them." He took a sip from his own cup. "Do you know how many cases Ø has solved?"

"No."

"Neither do I. But I know the rule. Ø never meets a suspect face to face. Never. Not once." He grinned. "The voice and the screen and the laptop. That's all anyone has ever seen. A bit of text. A color. A habit."

"Why?"

"Because the moment you give a clever person your face, you've given them a handle." He shrugged. "Ø says there are two kinds of enemies. The ones who fear you and the ones who study you. The second kind are more dangerous. You don't let them study you."

"And the question? The one about names?"

"What question?"

"At the end of the session, you said Ø always asks it."

"Did I?" Wataru looked at her with great innocence. "I'd have to check."

She did not believe him. She was beginning to suspect that Wataru lied about small things so that nobody would notice when he told the truth about large ones.

"Ø asks everyone who comes close to the case the same question," he said, abruptly. "Always the same, always at the end. *What is your name?* Most people answer it. It's a harmless question, so they say who they are, and Ø listens to how they say it." He tilted his head. "A name is a very revealing thing, Miss Himeno. Not what it is. What a person does when you ask for it."

"And if they don't answer?"

"Then Ø asks again."

"And again?"

"Until they do," said Wataru, "or until something happens to one of them."

He said it lightly. He did not look at her. After a moment, he stood, smoothed his rumpled shirt, and went back into the conference room, leaving her in the empty corridor with a cup of tea she did not want and a growing, cold certainty.

Ren Aizawa had not been asked for his name.

He had been asked whether he had *another.*

It was a different question entirely.

---

At 4:10 that afternoon, Ren Aizawa was summoned to the conference room.

He walked in with his usual pace and bowed to the room. He did not look at the wall of photographs. He did not look at the laptop. He stood, hands at his sides, and waited to be told where to sit.

"Aizawa Ren," said Wataru, from his bench. "Second in the class. Thank you for coming."

"Of course," said Ren.

Nobody noticed. Nobody but one.

In the corner, Himeno Saya's head came up sharply.

"Please sit," said Wataru. "This is a routine conversation. We're speaking to every student with a certain level of..." He waved a hand. "Proximity."

"I understand."

"You saved a girl at the crossing."

"Yes."

"You were in the library when Tachibana died."

"Yes."

"You spoke to Ogami Tetsu on the roof."

"Yes."

"Is there anything else you would like to tell us?"

"No," said Ren. "Is there anything else that you would like to ask me?"

Wataru smiled. It was a slow, delighted smile, like a child's at a magic trick.

"Not me," he said. "But Ø might."

The laptop was opened.

The screen lit. A single white line appeared. For a long moment, nothing happened. The room was silent. A fan hummed. A pipe ticked behind the wall.

Then the voice, low and measured, said, "Good afternoon, Aizawa."

"Good afternoon," said Ren.

"You don't seem surprised."

"I'm not."

"Why not?"

"Because I watched your broadcast," said Ren. "And because you wouldn't have asked to meet this class if you had no reason to think someone in it might be the one."

A beat.

"That's very reasonable," said the voice.

"Thank you."

"I will not ask you whether you are the one," said Ø. "That would be a waste of both our time. I would like to ask you something else."

"Please."

Another pause. The room seemed to lean toward the screen. Even Wataru had sat up.

"Do you have a name that is not Aizawa Ren?"

Himeno stopped breathing.

The question hung in the air for what felt like a very long time. It was not menacing. It was not clever. It was a plain question, asked in a plain voice, and every person in the room felt the temperature drop by a few degrees.

Ren Aizawa tilted his head. He looked, for a moment, like a boy who was thinking about the weather.

"I'm afraid I don't understand the question," he said.

"I think you do."

"I'm sorry."

"Think about it," said Ø. "I'll ask again."

The screen went dark.

Wataru exhaled. He lay back on his bench and put the cushion over his face again.

"Well," he said, in a muffled voice. "That went well."

"May I go?" said Ren.

"Please."

He bowed. He turned. He walked to the door at an even pace, and with each step he took, a girl in a folding chair in the corner felt her throat close a little more.

She was sure of one thing now.

Ø had asked the question because Ø already knew the answer.

And Ren Aizawa had not blinked.
