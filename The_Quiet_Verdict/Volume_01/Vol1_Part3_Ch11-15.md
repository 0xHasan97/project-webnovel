# Chapter 11: The Hand

The Task Force found the hand on a Wednesday.

It had taken them nine days. The incident system at Aster Institute was a sprawling, badly maintained database that nobody had updated since the previous decade, and its records had been entered by sixty-one different people through four different terminals. Mr. Wataru had lain on a bench for most of those nine days, staring at the ceiling, with a cushion on his face and a printout of every entry balanced on his stomach.

"There," he said at last.

"There what?" asked the gray-haired detective.

"A habit." He sat up. His hair stood out in all directions. "Every report in this system closes the same way. Not with the standard phrase, which is 'resolved.' With the word 'noted.' Roof lock, east camera, stairwell door. 'Noted.' Nobody else uses it."

"Who entered them?"

"That's the interesting part. The entries came from four terminals. But the user ID is always the same. A student number that doesn't exist." Wataru picked up his tea. "Someone made a ghost."

"A ghost can be traced."

"A ghost can be traced to a terminal. I traced it. Terminal three." He smiled his thin, bright smile. "The dormitory common room."

There was a short silence.

"Which has a hundred and twelve users," said the detective.

"Yes."

"Who all pass through it daily."

"Yes. It's very well chosen." Wataru sipped his tea. "Whoever he is, he has made himself a perfect crowd."

The laptop was opened, and the flat white line spoke.

"Good," said Ø. "Now watch the terminal. Not the user. The terminal. The ghost will use it again, because the ghost is a habit, and habits are the one thing intelligent people forget to disguise."

"Understood."

"One more thing. Has anyone examined the guest wing?"

"The guest wing?" The detective frowned. "It's empty."

"Exactly. It is the only wing of the school that nobody enters. I would like the entry log." There was the faint, musical hum. "Please do not touch the locks. Only the log."

---

Himeno Saya was sitting in the north library, six feet from a boy who was reading a book about thermodynamics, when she made the decision.

She had been circling it for days. She had written it in her notes and crossed it out, and written it again. *Tell Wataru.* The hand in the system, the word *noted,* the dormitory terminal. She knew whose habit it was. She had read the word in a hundred little margin notes on a hundred pieces of homework, in neat blue ink.

She had seen him write it on a worksheet in the first week. *Noted.*

She looked up.

Ren Aizawa turned a page.

If she told Wataru now, Ø would watch the terminal, and the terminal would be used, and she would be right. And that would be the end of it. She believed that. She believed it so hard that her hands were cold.

And yet.

*Why was he ready with a suggestion?* She had asked it in her notes. She had never answered it. She did not trust the clean, convenient answer that came to her now, because she had begun to suspect that every clean, convenient answer was one that had been made for her.

She closed her notebook.

She did not tell Wataru.

She told herself she needed more proof. She told herself it was caution. It was the first lie she had ever told herself, and it was very difficult to hear.

---

Mr. Kurata's third-period lecture, that Tuesday, was on decisions under uncertainty.

"Suppose," he said, chalk in hand, "that you are given a sealed envelope. Inside it is either a thousand points or a penalty of five hundred. You may open it, or you may sell it to the person beside you for two hundred. Which do you choose?"

Hands went up. The class argued, loudly, for ten minutes. Some said they would open it; some said two hundred was two hundred. A boy in the third row announced that the envelope was clearly a trap.

"Aizawa," said Mr. Kurata.

Ren stood. "I would ask who is selling it."

"Why does that matter?"

"If the person offering to buy it is willing to pay two hundred, they believe the contents are worth more. If the person who made the envelope is generous, the odds are better than they appear. If they are not, the penalty is more likely." He paused. "The question is not what is in the envelope. It is who wants it."

"And if you have no information about either?"

"Then I would watch what the buyer does when I hesitate."

Mr. Kurata studied him. The class had gone quiet. It was a strange answer for a sixteen-year-old, and in the front row Himeno Saya wrote it down without being asked.

"That's a very cold way to look at it," said the teacher.

"I'm sorry, sensei."

"Don't be. It is also correct." He set down the chalk. "Sit."

At the back of the room, by the window, Kuze Minato put down his pen and did not pick it up again for the rest of the class.

---

At 6:40 that evening, Sakaki Rio sat at a corner table in the common room with a notebook open in front of him.

It was a plain notebook, thin as a wallet. For ten days he had carried it everywhere. In it he had written, in a neat, grateful, schoolboy hand, every form that had passed through his fingers. Date. Subject. Who delivered it. *Maintenance, east camera. A first-year, uniform, bowed.* *Dormitory fire drill schedule.* *Sports festival permits.*

"Sakaki-kun," said a gentle voice. "May I?"

"Aizawa-kun! Of course." Sakaki scooted over. "Are you checking my handwriting?"

"I'm checking my own memory." Ren sat. "I think I forgot to return a form to you last week. A guest wing access request. I handled it for Mr. Kurata, and I think it may have been countersigned under your name."

"Oh." Sakaki turned back a page. "Here. May 14th, guest wing, one night, reserved for the Task Force's principal. You were just the courier." He tapped the line beneath it. "And the signature on the delivery slip. I copy those, too. I copy everything."

"May I see?"

"Please."

Ren took the notebook in both hands. He read down the page with the same level attention he gave a newspaper or a menu. He read one line for eleven seconds. Then he turned the page, read the next, and the next, and returned it with a small bow.

"Thank you. I was worried it was misfiled."

"It wasn't. Everything's here." Sakaki beamed. "I never would have thought of doing this. It's *so* useful."

"I'm glad."

"I owe you."

"You don't," said Ren.

He rose. At the end of the table, over the rim of a cup of cold tea, a pale boy by the window had stopped writing. Kuze Minato had seen the notebook change hands. He had seen Ren read the line. And, because he had spent a month learning where to look, he had seen the one thing nobody else had.

For eleven seconds, the second hand on the common room clock had been the only thing in the room that moved.

Ren Aizawa had not blinked.

---

That night, at 11:48, a message arrived on the secure channel that Ø used to speak with the Task Force.

It was a single line, and it was addressed to one person.

*Ren Aizawa. The north tower observatory. Midnight. Come alone.*

*I would like to show you my face.*


# Chapter 12: What Is Your Name?

At 11:31 p.m., Mio Hanazono opened her dormitory door to find Ren in the corridor with a thin black notebook in his hand.

"Aizawa-kun? It's late."

"I know. I'm sorry." He did not seem hurried. "I'm going to the library to finish some work. I wanted to tell someone, in case the guard asks."

"The library closes at ten."

"There's a reading room that doesn't. Sakaki-kun showed me." He smiled, a little. "It's quiet."

"Are you all right?"

"Yes."

"You look..." She hesitated, and the word she had meant to use slid away from her. "The same."

"Do I?"

"You always do." She laughed, uncertainly. "It's a compliment. I think."

"Thank you." He inclined his head. "Good night, Hanazono-san."

She watched him walk down the corridor toward the stairs. At the turn, he paused, and half turned, and for one moment she thought he would say something else.

"You've been very kind to me," he said.

"I haven't done anything."

"That's why," said Ren, and went down.

---

The observatory at the top of Aster's north tower was a round room with a domed ceiling and a brass telescope that no one had used in thirty years.

There was a door at the back, behind a rack of old star charts. Ren Aizawa had known about it since the night of the first broadcast, when he had stood alone at the dark window and counted the sounds. A hum. A fan. A faint, regular tick, like a small machine thinking.

He opened the door.

Beyond it was a narrow room lined with shelves. A long desk. Three monitors. A laptop with a white sticker on its lid. An electric kettle, plugged into a surge protector, and a mug with a faded star on it.

A man was sitting at the desk, with his back to the door.

"You came," he said. It was the voice from the broadcast, but without the filter. It was low, mild, a little hoarse, the voice of a man who had spent most of his life talking to screens. "Please close the door. The draft is bad for the equipment."

Ren closed it.

"Sit."

There was a folding chair. He sat.

The man swiveled slowly in his own chair. He was perhaps thirty. He wore a gray cardigan with a button missing, round glasses, and a pair of old leather slippers. His hair was neat. His face was long and pale and tired and unexpectedly kind. If you had passed him in a library, you would have thought he was a teacher, or a translator, or someone who tended his mother's garden.

"I told you I would show you my face," said Ø. "I am a man of my word. It is one of my few virtues."

"Good evening," said Ren.

"Good evening." Ø's smile was small and a little sad. "I have been in this room for twenty-three days. I chose it because nobody comes here. I thought I had hidden very well." He gestured at the ceiling. "And yet, on the ninth day, I heard someone standing on the other side of that door for eleven minutes."

"I didn't knock."

"No. That is what I found so interesting." He folded his hands. "I should like to say a few things, and then I should like to ask you a question. May I?"

"Please."

"I know who you are. Not your name. I know *what* you are. I have known since the second week." He did not raise his voice. "You are a person who has been handed an extraordinary power and decided that the right use of it is patience. You do not kill because you enjoy it. You kill the way a gardener prunes. You think of it as a duty."

"That's a very generous reading."

"I do not mean it generously. I mean it with respect." He leaned back. "But you are also a child, Aizawa-kun. You are sixteen. And you have made the mistake that all clever children make. You have assumed that the world is a puzzle with a solution."

"And it isn't?"

"It is a conversation. And conversations are won by the person who is willing to be seen." He took off his glasses. "So I am letting you see me."

He looked at Ren with plain, uncovered eyes.

For a long moment, neither of them spoke.

The fan hummed. The brass telescope ticked in the cold. The boy in the folding chair held his gaze with the same polite, level attention he gave to everything, and nothing at all moved in his face. Not his eyes. Not his mouth. Not his hands, which lay loose in his lap, one holding the plain black notebook he carried everywhere.

"I think there is a rule," said Ø softly. "I think a name is not enough. I think you must see a face. And I think, in your arrogance, you have never considered that I might have worked out the second half and kept the first."

"You mean your name."

"My name is something that nobody in this school knows. Nobody in the Task Force. Not even Mr. Wataru. I have been so careful." He put his glasses back on. "So you see, Aizawa-kun, you may look at me as long as you like. It will not help you."

Ren did not answer.

"Now my question," said Ø. He tilted his head. "I ask it of everyone who comes this close. It is a harmless question. People answer it all the time."

"Please."

"What is your name?"

The silence that followed was the longest of the night.

Ren Aizawa rose. He smoothed his jacket. He bowed, a correct, courteous bow, the bow of a student leaving a teacher's office. His notebook was open in his left hand. In his right was a pen. He turned to a page near the back, a page that no teacher would have recognized as notes, and wrote a single short line in a very small hand, in the time it takes a person to adjust a sleeve. The pen did not hesitate. It took less than two seconds.

"Good night," he said.

"You are not going to answer."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I'd rather not," said Ren, "be someone you can think about."

He closed the notebook. He walked to the door. He did not look back.

Behind him, Ø sat for a moment, quite still. Then he stood, with a small, puzzled frown, and crossed the room to the balcony door, as he did every night at this hour, to look at the lights of the city.

He had been standing there for two minutes when the first strange thing happened.

---

It was reconstructed later from three sources.

The first was a night guard, Mr. Taguchi, who heard nothing, because there was nothing to hear.

The second was a gardener, who found the man at the foot of the north tower at 5:20 the next morning, in the cold, among the rocks.

The third was a single line in the school's own incident log, entered at 1:04 a.m., from terminal three.

*Balcony rail, north tower. Noted.*

The police called it an accident. A trespasser, they said, a drifter, a man with no wallet and no phone, who had wandered onto campus and climbed a forbidden stair. He wore a gray cardigan with a button missing. He had no name.

There was one more item in the file, which nobody read.

It was an inventory of what the dead man had owned. A gray cardigan with a missing button. A pair of leather slippers. A pair of round glasses, cracked at the hinge. And a small brass key, stamped with the number *G-1.*

Nobody knew what it opened.


# Chapter 13: The Typist

On Thursday, the white line did not speak at nine in the morning, or at nine at night.

This was not unusual. Ø sometimes went silent. He had been known to disappear for days when working a problem, and the nine detectives in the old conference room had learned to wait.

On Friday at 9:00 p.m., it spoke.

"Report," said the voice.

The room straightened as one. The gray-haired detective, who had been dozing in his chair, shot upright and almost knocked over a cup. Mr. Wataru, lying on the bench, did not move, but a corner of his cushion twitched.

"The guest wing entry log," said the detective. "Nobody's used it. Not in six weeks."

"Good. Keep it that way."

"And the terminal?"

"The ghost has not returned," said Ø. "Leave the surveillance in place. I will inform you if I want it moved."

The detectives exchanged glances. It was the right answer, and the right pace, and the right voice. But there had been something, a small and untraceable thing, in the rhythm of the sentences. A fraction of a second too slow between *good* and *keep.* A pause that Ø did not usually make.

"Is something wrong?" asked the detective, before he could stop himself.

"No."

"Forgive me. It's only..."

"I have a cold," said the voice. "It affects the processor."

It was such a human thing to say that the room relaxed. Someone laughed, a little. The session moved on to the next topic, and the next, and ended at 9:22.

When the screen went dark, Mr. Wataru took the cushion off his face and looked at the ceiling for a long time.

"Ø has never had a cold," he said.

Nobody heard him.

---

Detective Sato had called Mr. Wataru on Thursday afternoon, in a voice that was careful the way a man is careful carrying a full bowl.

"I know you're busy with the school case. But I thought you'd want to see this."

The county morgue was cold and smelled of bleach. Sato led him down a corridor to a steel drawer, and Wataru, in his rumpled shirt and his bare feet pushed into borrowed shoes, looked at the face on the table for a long time.

"Male," said Sato. "Thirty, thirty-five. No wallet, no phone, no ID. He fell from the north tower of Aster Institute some time around midnight on Wednesday. The cardigan's handmade. The slippers, too. And there's this."

He held up a plastic bag. Inside was a small brass key. *G-1.*

"The guest wing at Aster," said Wataru slowly.

"That's the one."

"Did you try it?"

"Room G-1 is empty, as far as the school knows. The reservation's under a code. 'Principal, Task Force.' No name." Sato rubbed his neck. "I wasn't going to say anything. But the way he's dressed. The ink on his fingers. He's *comfortable.* He looks like a man who lives in front of a screen. And your people are the only ones who've been living in front of a screen in that building for six weeks."

Wataru looked at the face again. It was long, pale, quiet, a little puzzled. He had never seen it before.

He had never seen Ø's face, either. Nobody had. That was the point.

"I can't identify him," he said, at last.

"You mean you won't."

"I mean I *can't.* I've spoken to the person who runs my case every day for two months. By voice. By text. I wouldn't know him from a stranger in a train."

Sato exhaled. "Then he's a drifter."

"Yes," said Wataru. "He's a drifter."

He went out into the cold. He stood in the parking lot for several minutes, hands in his pockets, shoulders hunched, looking at nothing. Then he took out his phone and opened a message he had received that morning from a private channel, at 8:59 a.m., and read the single line again.

*I have a cold.*

He put the phone away. He did not go back to the school until nightfall.

---

In the dormitory, at 9:30 that night, Sakaki Rio received a message.

It arrived on his school tablet, in a private channel he had never seen before. The screen was black. A single white letter glowed in the center.

Ø

Sakaki stared at it for a full minute. Then, very slowly, he put down the cup of instant noodles he had been eating and pressed his thumb to the glass.

*Sakaki Rio. Class 1-C. Representative.*

*You do not know me, but I know you. I have read the incident log. I have seen the notebook you keep. You are meticulous, honest, and you document everything. These are rare qualities.*

*I need someone inside Aster. Someone the students trust, who sees every form that passes through the school, and who can relay my messages to the Task Force without drawing attention. I cannot be seen at this stage. I need a typist.*

*It will not be easy. It may be dangerous.*

*Will you help me?*

His hands were shaking. He pressed them flat to his knees. He did not tell anyone. He did not even breathe, for several seconds, as if the message might evaporate. Then he typed, with two clumsy fingers:

*Yes. Of course. Thank you for choosing me.*

The reply came in four seconds.

*Good. You will receive instructions each evening at 8:00. You will tell no one, not even the Task Force. Not even Mr. Wataru.*

*You will not ask me who I am. You will not ask me why.*

*And Sakaki-kun?*

*Thank you.*

The screen went black.

Sakaki sat for a long time, staring at his own reflection. Then he got up, walked to the window, and looked out at the campus lights. For the first time in his life, he felt chosen.

---

Ren Aizawa was waiting for him in the hall.

"You look pale," he said.

"I...I'm fine." Sakaki laughed shakily. "I think I just ate something strange."

"You should rest."

"I will. I...Aizawa-kun." He hesitated. His face was flushed and bright and terrified. "If someone asked you to do something important. Something secret. Something that might help a lot of people. And they said you couldn't tell anybody. Would you do it?"

Ren considered. He considered it with great kindness, the way he considered everything.

"I think it would depend on who was asking," he said.

"Suppose it was someone you trusted."

"Then yes."

"Even if you were scared?"

"Especially then," said Ren. "That's what trust is for."

Sakaki's eyes shone. He bowed, quickly, awkwardly, and went back to his room. The door closed behind him.

Ren stood in the corridor for a while. Then he turned and walked to the stairwell, and as he did, a faint sound followed him from the shadows at the far end of the hall.

A small, slow movement. The turning of a page.

Kuze Minato, in the dark, had written one more line.

*10. Ø did not have a cold.*


# Chapter 14: Two Notebooks

On Saturday morning, Ren Aizawa stopped beside the window desk in the empty classroom, where Kuze Minato sat alone, writing.

"Good morning."

Kuze did not look up. "Good morning."

"You are always writing."

"Yes."

"May I ask what?"

"No."

Ren nodded, as if this were the most natural answer in the world. He did not move away. He stood with his hands loosely at his sides and his plain black notebook tucked under one arm, and he looked down at the top of Kuze's head with pleasant interest.

"I was only thinking that it must be a great deal of work," he said. "Keeping a record."

"It is."

"Is it for yourself?"

"Partly."

"And the rest?"

"For whoever comes after." Kuze finally lifted his eyes. They were very steady. "Someone should know what happened. Even if no one can say who did it."

"That is a generous thing to do."

"It is not generosity."

"No?"

"It is inconvenient," said Kuze. "I would prefer not to. But I notice things, and I cannot make myself stop."

Ren studied him. There was no threat in it, and no warmth. It was the gaze a man gives to a clock he has not seen before, wondering how it was wound.

"I should like to be in it," he said, "when you've finished."

"You already are."

"Then I hope it is accurate."

"It will be," said Kuze. "That's the problem."

Ren smiled, a very small, polite smile, and bowed his head, and left.

Kuze sat motionless for nearly five minutes. When he picked up his pen again, he found that he had pressed so hard on the last word he had written that it had torn the page.

---

Himeno Saya found Kuze Minato in the north library on a Sunday afternoon, in the one corner no one ever used.

He was sitting with his back to the wall, and his notebook was open on his knee. He did not look up when she approached.

"You've been following me," she said.

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Eleven days." He turned a page. "You walk quickly. It's difficult."

"Why?"

"Because I thought you might be useful." He closed the notebook, finally, and looked at her with his tired gray eyes. "And because you have been lying to the Task Force."

She stiffened. "I haven't lied."

"You've withheld. It's the same thing, only quieter." He did not say it unkindly. "On Wednesday you read the word *noted* in the incident log and recognized the handwriting. You did not report it. You came to the library instead and watched Aizawa read a book."

"How could you possibly..."

"I was in the next aisle."

She stared at him. Then, to her own surprise, she sat down on the floor across from him and let her bag slide from her shoulder.

"Show me yours," said Kuze.

"My what?"

"Your notes. I've seen you writing in your phone. I assume you have more than I do."

It took her a long minute. In that minute, she realized how long it had been since anyone had asked her to share anything. She took out the phone. She opened the notes. She handed it across without a word.

He read for a long time. When he reached the bottom, he nodded slowly.

"This is very good," he said. "You have the stairwell, the truck, the form. You've connected Sakaki and Ogami and Hina. You didn't have the pattern of the rankings until the eighth, but you got there." He handed back the phone. "You are missing four things."

"What?"

"Question 48. You mentioned it. But you did not weigh it." He opened his own notebook and turned it around. "It is the first thing he did. Before the truck, before the roof, before Tachibana. He deliberately made himself second."

"To avoid attention."

"To *plan* his attention. He was never going to be first, because first is watched. He wanted to be the one the class looked at when they needed someone to be calm."

Himeno was silent.

"The second thing is the pencil," Kuze said. "Aoyagi Hina lent him a pencil. He told the counselor. He mentioned it because it was true and affectionate, and the counselor wrote it down as evidence of empathy. But Hina was dead for six days before the counselor asked."

"He used her death as a kindness."

"He used her death as a *credential.*" Kuze turned the page. "The third thing is that every one of these events took place in places without cameras, but every one has a witness who can place Aizawa somewhere else. The library. The common room. The nurse's office. It is not an alibi. It is an architecture."

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth is the one I cannot explain."

He pushed the notebook toward her. At the bottom, in small, left-slanting letters, was a line she had not seen.

*On the night the stranger fell from the tower, Aizawa left his dormitory at 11:31 and the corridor camera shows him returning at 12:58, in the same clothes, the same shoes. He had been out for an hour and twenty-seven minutes. And he did not look like a boy who had hurried.*

"What stranger?"

"The one in the paper." Kuze said it softly. "A man who died on the rocks below the north tower. No name. No ID. A gray cardigan."

"I read about it. A drifter."

"Yes," said Kuze. "A drifter who happened to fall from the one building that houses the sealed archive."

She felt the floor tilt.

"There is a fifth thing," Kuze said, before she could ask. "On Wednesday afternoon, in the common room, Aizawa borrowed Sakaki's notebook. He turned to a page about the guest wing and read one line for eleven seconds. I counted. Everything else he read in two."

"What was on the line?"

"I could not see. But the Task Force has since asked for the guest wing entry log, and they are looking in the wrong place. They want to know who goes in. Nobody has asked who reserved the room."

---

"You think that was Ø."

"I think I do not know." He took the notebook back. "I think that the voice on the laptop did not have a cold. I think that Sakaki Rio is walking around like a man who has been given a medal. And I think that something happened in that tower, and that Aizawa Ren is the only person in this school who knows what."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you are the only one who is as afraid as I am," said Kuze.

She looked at him for a long time. Then she took her own notebook from her bag, the real one, the one she had never shown anyone, and put it on the floor between them.

"We need rules," she said.

"Yes."

"First rule. We don't say his name aloud. Not in the dorm, not in class."

"Agreed."

"Second. We don't confront him. Not until we have something that can't be explained."

"Agreed."

"Third." Her voice was very small. "If either of us stops showing up, the other one doesn't ask why."

"No," said Kuze. "The other one finishes the notebook."

He held out his hand. She took it.

Neither of them knew that at that exact moment, on the roof above them, a boy in a tidy uniform was standing quietly at the rail with a plain black notebook in his hand, listening to the wind, and that the angle of the sun on the glass of the library skylight was such that a shadow had fallen across the page.

It was a very small shadow.

It did not move.


# Chapter 15: A Perfect Week

It was, by every measure the school had, a perfect week for Class 1-C.

The monthly review came on a Friday. The standings were posted on the main corridor board at 8:00 a.m. By 8:05, there was a crowd. By 8:10, a few students were crying, and not all of them were sad.

CLASS 1-C: 4,901 CLASS 1-A: 4,744 CLASS 1-B: 3,902 CLASS 1-D: 3,311

"We're first," whispered Mio Hanazono. She had both hands pressed to her mouth. "We're first. We've never..."

"Don't say it too loudly," said Ren Aizawa, at her side. "It makes the others feel worse."

"But we did it."

"You did it. All of you."

"You did," said Mio. "You led us."

"I stayed with you," said Ren. "That was all."

There was a roar down the corridor as the class found each other. Sakaki Rio was being thrown into the air by three classmates. Himeno Saya stood very straight at the back of the crowd, staring at the board with a face the color of chalk. And at the window, in the last row, a boy named Kuze Minato wrote a single number in his notebook and did not look up.

*Eleven.*

---

The same morning, the Task Force made its first arrest.

It happened quietly, in the staff office, at 10:20. A tall detective and two uniformed officers took Honda Ryuu by the arms and told him that he was being held in connection with the deaths of Tachibana Gen, Ogami Tetsu, and Aoyagi Hina.

He did not fight. He seemed to have lost the energy.

"I didn't do it," he said, over and over, in a hollow voice, as they led him down the corridor. "I didn't. I didn't."

"The evidence is convincing," said the detective.

"What evidence?"

"A message was received this morning from the lead investigator. It identified you as the most probable suspect. A search of your room produced a notebook with the names of all three victims."

"That's not mine!"

"It has your handwriting."

"It's *forged!*"

"Please come quietly, Honda-kun."

In the doorway of the conference room, Mr. Wataru stood with his hands in his pockets and watched the procession pass. He said nothing. He looked tired, and not at all like a man who had won.

When it was over, he returned to the bench and lay down. The gray-haired detective came to stand beside him.

"It's closed," he said. "Ø gave the order. The evidence fits."

"It fits," Wataru agreed.

"Then why do you look like that?"

"Because it fits too well."

"You think the boy is innocent?"

"I think the boy is a *convenient* boy." Wataru put the cushion over his face. "I think someone gave Ø a case that Ø wanted. And I think Ø took it."

"Ø is never wrong."

"No," said Wataru, in a muffled voice. "That's what bothers me."

---

At 8:00 p.m., in his room, Sakaki Rio received his daily instructions.

*The arrest of Honda was premature but necessary. The public needs to believe the matter is closed. This will give us room to work.*

*You will send the following to the Task Force at 9:00. Do not alter it.*

He copied the paragraph. He read it twice. It was brief, calm, and elegant, and it said that Ø would be unavailable for several days and that the Task Force should proceed on the assumption that the case was largely resolved.

He sent it at 9:00.

Then he sat for a long time with his hands in his lap. He could not have said why his stomach hurt. He had done a good thing. He had helped. He told himself so, over and over, and the more he said it, the less it sounded like a sentence and the more it sounded like a prayer.

---

At four o'clock, Headmistress Aoi Tsukishiro received Ren Aizawa in her office.

It was a plain room. A single window, a desk of dark wood, a vase containing one branch of white blossom. She did not offer him tea. She sat with her hands folded and studied him for a very long time, the way a person reads a letter she has already read twice.

"Congratulations," she said. "Your class stands first."

"Thank you, Headmistress."

"I did not ask you here to praise you."

"No."

"I asked you here because a gardener found a man on the rocks below the north tower. He wore a gray cardigan. He had a key to the guest wing." She did not blink. "My staff entered the sealed archive on Thursday morning. It had been cleaned. Someone had wiped the desk, unplugged the equipment, taken the laptop. But they left a kettle."

"I see."

"Do you know why they left it?"

"I don't."

"Because a kettle looks innocent." Her voice was perfectly level. "I find, Aizawa, that the things which look most innocent in this school are the things that have been placed there on purpose."

"That's a very perceptive observation."

"I have been the headmistress of this institution for twenty years. I have watched forty classes pass through it. I have seen clever children and cruel ones and a very few who were both. I am not an easy woman to impress."

"I understand."

"I do not think you do." She unfolded her hands. "Aster was not built to produce winners. It was built to find out what the winners will do when nobody is watching. I would like you to remember that."

"Yes, Headmistress."

"You may go."

He bowed. At the door he stopped, and without turning around, said in the mild, courteous voice that he used for everything, "Headmistress. If I may. Who built it?"

"I'm sorry?"

"The school. The foundation papers are sealed. The guide said that no one remembers who founded it."

For the first time, Aoi Tsukishiro's expression changed. It was a tiny, precise thing, a narrowing at the corner of one eye. If he had been looking, he would have seen it.

"That," she said, "is not your concern."

"Of course," said Ren.

He closed the door. She sat for a long moment in the silence. Then she opened her desk drawer, took out a small gray folder, and looked at the name on the tab for the first time in twenty years.

It was blank.

---

At 10:15, there was a knock at Ren Aizawa's door.

It was Mio Hanazono. She was holding a small, cream-colored envelope.

"This came for you," she said. "The mail clerk left it at the front desk. It wasn't stamped. I think someone brought it by hand."

"Thank you."

"It's from..." She frowned. "It's very strange. There's no return address."

He took it. He turned it over. The paper was heavy, expensive, with a faint watermark, a small, five-petaled flower.

"Aizawa-kun?"

"It's nothing," said Ren. "Please go to bed."

She went. He closed the door, and he stood in the dark for a moment with the envelope in his hand. Then he slid a finger under the flap.

Inside was a single, very old pressed flower, flat and brown and perfect. A purple aster.

And beneath it, a card.

The handwriting was gentle and unhurried, the writing of a man who has never had to rush anything in his life.

*My quiet one.*

*I read about your friend in the north tower. Congratulations. You have done in a month what some of the others took years to learn.*

*I have missed you very much.*

*Come home when you are ready.*

*— E.*

For a long time, nothing happened.

He read it once. He turned the card over, and examined the back, and then the watermark, and then the thickness of the paper, in that order and at that speed. Then he placed the card and the flower in the envelope, and the envelope in the drawer of his desk, and he locked the drawer.

He stood at the window for eleven minutes. The campus lay dark below him, the clock tower pale against the stars.

In the morning he ate breakfast, and he was the first student in the classroom. The only thing anyone could say had changed was that he arrived four minutes early.
