## Arc 1 Recap: The Classroom

*Aster Institute ranks its students by a hidden point system, and the ones who fall to zero are removed.*

*Ren Aizawa, second in his class, does not show emotion and does not explain himself.*

*Someone, somewhere, has begun to decide who lives.*

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. Each use is recorded in the ledger, in the writer's own handwriting, and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it.*



# Chapter 16: The Woman in Gray

The envelope had arrived by hand, and in a school with four hundred students and sixty staff, a hand leaves a mark.

The mail clerk at the front desk was a cheerful, round woman named Mrs. Ota, who kept a jar of boiled sweets beside her stamp pad and knew every parent in the district by their handwriting. On Saturday morning, Ren Aizawa approached her counter and bowed.

"Excuse me. I'm sorry to bother you. On Friday, an envelope was left for me. Cream-colored. Do you remember who brought it?"

"Oh, the pretty one." Mrs. Ota beamed. "Of course. A lady. Tall, gray coat, hair in a bun. About forty, I'd say. She had a lovely voice."

"Did she give a name?"

"No, dear. Very polite, though. She said she was delivering something on behalf of a friend and that you would understand." She pushed the jar toward him. "Sweet?"

"Thank you." He took one. "Did she say where she was from?"

"Not as such. But she had a little pin on her collar. A lantern, I think. Brass. I noticed because my mother had one like it, from the old charity." Mrs. Ota tapped her chin. "A children's charity. Orphans, you know. They used to sell the pins at the train station for the Christmas drive."

"Do you recall the name?"

"It's gone. Something with a house in it."

"That's very helpful," said Ren. "Thank you."

He bowed. He turned from the counter and walked to the door. Behind him Mrs. Ota popped a sweet into her own mouth, content, and did not notice that the boy had left the sweet she had given him on the counter, unopened, in exactly the spot she had pushed it from.

---

The stationer's was on the second street from the station, a narrow shop with a bell over the door and a smell of ink and old paper. The man behind the counter was bald and bespectacled and held the envelope under a lamp for a long time.

"Laid paper," he said. "Cotton, I'd say, hundred-gram. And the watermark's a five-petal flower, hand-pressed. A single mill. They stopped making it in..." He frowned. "The eighties?"

"Do you know who it was made for?"

"I know exactly. It was a special order, one hundred reams a year, for a foundation." He turned the paper. "Lantern House. It was an orphanage on the coast. Closed down some time ago. A great shame. They had lovely stationery."

"Closed down why?"

"I don't know. There was a fire, I think. Or a scandal." He shrugged and handed back the envelope. "People said the head of the house was a doctor. A very gentle man. Wouldn't hurt a fly."

"Thank you," said Ren.

He paid for a notebook he did not need. He walked out under the bell and stood on the pavement for a moment in the thin June sunshine, with the envelope held in one hand and his gaze on the traffic, and nothing in his posture or his face suggested anything but a student with an errand.

Across the street, half hidden by a news kiosk, a pale boy lowered his notebook.

*Lantern House,* wrote Kuze Minato, in tiny, left-slanting letters. *A doctor. A fire.*

He looked up. The pavement was empty.

---

In the north library that evening, Kuze Minato slid a folded page across the table to Himeno Saya.

"Lantern House," he said.

She read the line. "An orphanage."

"On the coast. Closed after a fire, or a scandal. Run by a doctor. I found two newspaper articles in the archive, both short. The second is dated nineteen years ago and does not say how the fire began." He tapped the page. "It says forty children were transferred to other institutions. It does not say where."

"Forty."

"And one hundred and eleven were enrolled in the preceding decade."

She looked up. "Seventy-one are unaccounted for."

"I did not say that."

"You didn't have to." Her voice was very quiet. "Why would Aizawa receive a card from a closed orphanage?"

"I do not know." Kuze capped his pen. "But I observed that he did not hurry to the stationer's. He went directly. As though he already knew which shop made the paper."

"You think he's from there."

"I think," said Kuze, "that nobody in this school knows where he came from. His file says *transferred from a private academy,* and the academy has no record of him. I called. I said I was a relative."

Himeno shut her eyes. When she opened them again, she looked, for a moment, very young.

"Kuze-kun. What are we doing?"

"Collecting," he said. "Until it is enough."

---

On Monday, Mio Hanazono was late for class.

She arrived at 8:19, breathless, her hair untidy and her bag slung crooked on her shoulder, and she slid into her seat with an apologetic bow. The teacher let it pass. She stared at her desk for the entire first period.

At the break, Himeno Saya stopped beside her.

"Are you all right?"

"What? Oh. Yes. I'm fine." Mio smiled too quickly. "I overslept."

"You came through the front gate at 8:07."

"Did I?"

"I was in the library window." Himeno kept her voice low. "You were talking to a woman."

"Oh. That. She was asking for directions."

"For twelve minutes?"

Mio's smile flickered and held. "She was very lost."

Himeno studied her. It was not a cruel look. She had never been cruel to Mio, who laughed too easily and cried too easily and had once spent an entire lunch period apologizing to a vending machine. But Himeno had learned in six weeks to hear the small gap between a person's words and their face, and in Mio's, right now, the gap was wide enough to fall through.

"Mio," she said. It was the first time she had used her first name. "If anything is wrong..."

"Nothing's wrong."

"You can tell me."

"Himeno-san." Mio's eyes were suddenly bright. "I would. Honestly. But I don't know how to explain it to myself, so I don't know how I'd explain it to you."

That was all. She turned away. At the far end of the room, a pale boy at a window desk was writing in his notebook, and a boy in the second row, who had not looked up from his textbook all morning, turned a page.

---

At 4:40 that afternoon, the woman in the gray coat was waiting outside the main gate.

She was as Mrs. Ota had described: tall, composed, perhaps forty, with her hair in a neat bun and a small brass lantern on her collar. She held a paper bag from a bakery. She stood perfectly still beneath the cherry trees, heavy with green summer leaves, like a person who had been trained to wait.

When Mio came down the hill road, she did not go to her. She simply held out the bag.

Mio took it. Her hands were shaking.

"You look well, Mio-chan," the woman said, in a lovely, low voice. "Have you been eating?"

"Yes, Matron."

"Have you been sleeping?"

"Yes."

"Have you been good?"

A silence. The wind stirred the leaves.

"Yes, Matron," said Mio, very quietly.

"That is all I wanted to know." The woman smiled, and it was a gentle smile, infinitely patient. "Eat the pastry. It's the kind you like."

She turned and walked away down the hill road, her gray coat swinging at her knees. Mio stood with the bag in her hands and did not move for a very long time.

She did not see the figure on the roof of the south dormitory, a small dark shape against the evening sky, which had watched the whole exchange without moving at all.

And she did not see that the shape lifted one hand, very slightly, and wrote something in a plain black notebook that had nothing to do with homework.



# Chapter 17: The Vote

The special exam was announced on a Tuesday, with no warning, in the cold clear voice of Mr. Kurata.

"This is a conduct examination," he said. "It is worth four hundred Class Points. You will not be told in advance what it measures."

He wrote four lines on the board.

1. The class will nominate one student for reassignment. 2. Reassignment will occur at the end of the term. 3. The class must be unanimous in its choice. 4. If the class fails to reach a unanimous decision within seventy-two hours, it will forfeit the four hundred points.

A cold silence settled over the room.

"Reassignment," said a girl. "What does that mean?"

"It means the student will be transferred to another institution." Mr. Kurata's face did not change. "It is a good institution. It is a *different* institution."

"That's expulsion," someone whispered.

"It is reassignment."

"But why would we choose someone?"

"Because the alternative is four hundred points," said Mr. Kurata. "Seventy-two hours. Good luck."

He left. The classroom exploded.

---

By midnight, three factions had formed.

The first, led by a loud boy named Nakamura, argued that the class should choose the weakest student, whoever had contributed the least. The second, led by a girl named Sugiura, argued that this was cruel and refused to name anyone. The third, which was the largest, simply wanted somebody else to make the decision.

"We need a vote," said Sakaki Rio, standing on a chair in the common room. His voice cracked. "A fair one. Anonymous. Written ballots."

"And if no name gets all forty?"

"Thirty-eight," said someone. "There are thirty-eight of us."

"Then we lose the points," said Nakamura. "And I'm not losing four hundred points because you can't decide."

"It's *someone's life!*"

"It's a school!"

The shouting went on for an hour. At the end of it, Himeno Saya stood on the edge of the crowd with her arms folded and her chin up. She had a plan, and she hated it. She could win the exam. She could organize the ballot, steer the discussion, manufacture a consensus that fell, by clean logic, on the one student whose absence would harm the class least. It would work. It would be efficient and fair.

She would not be able to look at herself afterward.

"Himeno-san."

She turned. Ren Aizawa was standing at her elbow, hands in his pockets, perfectly calm.

"You have a plan," he said.

"I have a bad one."

"May I hear it?"

She told him, in a low, clipped voice. He listened to the end without interrupting. When she had finished, he nodded slowly.

"It would work," he said.

"I know."

"But I think there is a better one."

"Which is?"

"We don't nominate anyone."

She stared at him.

"Rule three says the nomination must be unanimous," said Ren. "Rule four says that if we fail to reach a unanimous decision, we forfeit four hundred points. It does not say that the nomination must be of someone *else.*"

"You mean..."

"I mean that the class can unanimously nominate any student it likes. Including the one proposing it." He smiled, faintly. "If every member writes their *own* name on the ballot, the nomination is unanimous. There is no loser. The exam is satisfied. And the class has a binding result."

"That's not a nomination. That's a technicality."

"Yes. And the rules do not forbid technicalities." He looked at her steadily. "It is also the only result that does not require anyone to hurt anyone else. I thought you might prefer that."

Himeno opened her mouth and closed it.

It was, she realized, brilliant. It was also something else. It was the first time since April that Ren Aizawa had offered her an idea that cost nothing. No hidden hook, no second layer. Or if there was one, she could not find it, and that frightened her far more.

"You'd put your own name on the ballot," she said slowly.

"I would."

"Even though you don't know what *reassignment* means."

"I know exactly what it means," said Ren. "That's why I'm sure the school will not carry it out."

"Why?"

"Because a school that expels a first-place class for obeying its own rules would have to explain itself." He turned to look at the noisy room. "And Aster does not like to explain itself."

---

On the second night, Nakamura Taichi cornered Ren in the stairwell.

He was a big, loud boy with a square jaw and a scholarship he was terrified of losing. He had spent two days arguing for the weakest-student plan, and he did not like that the class was drifting away from him.

"You," he said. "You've been whispering to people."

"I've been talking," said Ren.

"You're going to get them all to write their own name. I heard. It's a trick."

"It is a technicality."

"It's a *trick,* and when the school finds out, we'll be punished."

"Possibly." Ren stood at the foot of the stairs with his hands loosely folded. "May I ask you something? Why are you so certain someone has to be chosen?"

"Because that's the *exam!*"

"Is it? Or is it what you assumed the exam was?" He tilted his head. "You have the highest score on the practical tests, Nakamura-kun. In the bottom half on essays. If the class chose the weakest student, it would be someone with a poor essay average. You would be fourth."

Nakamura's mouth opened. Closed.

"I'm not saying it to frighten you. I'm saying it because you are a person who is good at things, and I would hate to see you spend two days defending a rule that might one day be used on you." Ren's voice was mild and warm. "If I'm wrong, I'm very sorry. Perhaps you should think it over."

"I..."

"Thank you for talking with me."

He walked up the stairs. Nakamura stood where he was for a long time, his big hands opening and closing at his sides. Then he went to find Sugiura Aya and, in a mumbling, embarrassed voice, told her he'd changed his mind.

---

The vote was held at 9:00 a.m. on the third day.

Thirty-eight ballots went into the box. Sakaki read them aloud, one at a time, his voice growing steadier with each. *Hanazono Mio. Sugiura Aya. Nakamura Taichi. Himeno Saya.* Thirty-eight different names. Thirty-eight students who had each, quietly, written their own.

At the end there was a long silence.

"The ballots are unanimous," said Mr. Kurata at last. For the first time that term, his mouth twitched. "Each student has been nominated by the entire class."

"Is that allowed?" Nakamura demanded.

"It is not forbidden."

"What happens now?"

"The exam is concluded." He glanced at the clock. "Class 1-C has passed with full marks. You are awarded four hundred points, and an additional two hundred for what I believe the committee will call 'novel cooperation.'" He cleared his throat. "Dismissed."

The class cheered. Sakaki burst into tears. Himeno stood at the back with her arms still folded, looking at the boy at the front of the room who had accepted the applause with a small bow and no change in expression at all.

*He didn't win,* she thought. *He made it so nobody could lose.*

She wrote that in her notes later, in the library, at the bottom of a long page. Then she stared at it for a very long time.

*Which is the same thing,* she added, *as making sure that everyone owes you.*

---

In the corridor outside, Mr. Kurata encountered the headmistress.

"Four hundred points," she said.

"Six hundred."

"Six." Aoi Tsukishiro's expression was unreadable. "I told the committee it would be a good exam."

"It was."

"And the solution?"

"Unanimous." He pushed his glasses up into his hair. "Whose idea was it?"

"The class's."

"Whose, Kurata?"

He hesitated. He looked through the glass of the classroom door at the boy who had been the second to leave his seat, who was now carefully stacking another student's dropped papers into a neat pile.

"Mine," said Mr. Kurata. "I think. It must have been."

The headmistress said nothing. She looked at him for a moment, and her eyes were very dark, and then she turned and walked away down the corridor with the soft, even steps of a woman who has remembered something.



# Chapter 18: A Question for Ø

Mr. Wataru had never been good at being ignored.

He had been ignored for most of his life, by teachers, by employers, by a series of increasingly patient girlfriends, and he had developed, as a defense, a talent for being so unimportant that nobody noticed when he paid attention. It was the only reason he had lasted so long in the Task Force. He was the man you forgot to hide things from.

On Tuesday, at 9:00 p.m., he lay on his bench with the cushion on his face and listened to the voice of Ø conclude the evening session.

"That is all."

"Understood," said the gray-haired detective.

"One thing more." The voice was low, level, impeccable. "I would like each of you to review your protocols for the safe handling of evidence. I have noticed some laxity."

"Of course."

"Good night."

The white line vanished. The detectives began to pack up. Wataru lay still, and for a long moment he did not breathe, and then, in a muffled voice from beneath the cushion, he said:

"It said *laxity.*"

"What?" said the detective.

"The word. *Laxity.*" He took the cushion off his face. "Ø has never used that word. In two years. Not once."

"Perhaps it's a new vocabulary."

"Ø does not have new vocabulary." Wataru sat up. "Ø has the same eight hundred words, in the same order, and uses them like a man who is saving money. The man I know does not say *laxity.* He says 'carelessness.' He says it with a little hum on the first syllable."

"You have been listening to a machine for two years. Don't you think you might be reading too much into..."

"I have been listening to a *person.*"

He stood. He crossed the room and picked up the laptop with the white sticker. The detective moved to stop him, then stopped. Nobody had ever touched it before.

"What are you doing?"

"Asking a question," said Wataru. He opened the lid, and typed, slowly, with two fingers:

*You told me on the first day to avoid sugar. Do you remember why?*

A pause. Then the reply, in the same flat white font:

*I would not recommend it. It dulls the mind.*

"Ø never said that," Wataru murmured.

"Then who..."

"Ø *never told me to avoid sugar,*" said Wataru. "I made that up. Ø's rule is that a man should do exactly as he likes, so long as it doesn't interfere with the work. The real Ø would say *I would not presume.* The real Ø would say *you are very fond of it.*"

The detective's face had gone gray.

"Wataru..."

"Whoever is answering me has the voice, and the rhythm, and the logs. They have read two years of conversation." He closed the lid. "But they have not *lived* it."

"Then Ø is..."

"I don't know." He put the laptop back on the table and stood looking at it for a long moment, hunched and rumpled, a small man in a creased shirt. "I know that the man at the morgue could be anyone. I know that the voice has a cold, and I know that it knows words it has never said. And I know one other thing."

"What?"

"If I tell you what I think, and you tell the Task Force, and the Task Force tells the world, then whoever is running this puppet will simply stop. And I will never find out who."

The detective stared at him.

"So you want me to say nothing."

"I want you," said Wataru, "to go back to your hotel, eat a large meal, and sleep. And tomorrow I want you to come into this room and do your job exactly as before. Say *good evening* to the laptop. Write down what it tells you. And *obey.*"

"Obey a fake."

"Obey a fake that thinks it's winning." Wataru's thin smile appeared. "People are so much easier to watch when they believe they are not being watched."

---

At 11:00 that night, Wataru dialed a number on a phone he rarely used.

"Sato."

"It's late."

"The man in the cardigan. What happened to the body?"

"Unclaimed. We hold them for sixty days." A pause. "Why?"

"I'd like it kept. Fingerprints, dental, a hair sample. Anything. Quietly."

"On whose authority?"

"Mine." Wataru stared at the ceiling. "I'm aware that it's irregular."

"You're aware," said the detective, "that the man in the cardigan was a drifter."

"Detective Sato. When a drifter dies in a tower, he does not usually have a key to a room that officially does not exist. He does not have hand-sewn slippers. And he does not, in my experience, have a mug with a star on it in a sealed room above his head." He closed his eyes. "I'd like him kept."

A long silence on the line.

"Fine," said Sato. "I'll put him in the cold room. Nobody'll look."

"Thank you."

"Wataru." The detective's voice dropped. "Are you telling me something I don't want to hear?"

"I don't know yet," said Wataru. "But I think the dead are more honest than the living, and I think it's going to be a very long winter."

---

On Wednesday evening, Himeno Saya was walking back to the dormitory when she saw Sakaki Rio on the lawn, sitting alone on a bench with his tablet on his knee.

He was smiling. It was a small, fragile, unfamiliar smile, the smile of a boy who has been entrusted with something enormous.

"Sakaki-kun."

"Himeno-san!" He fumbled with the tablet. "I was just, ah, doing homework."

"On a bench."

"It's a nice night."

She sat beside him. For a moment neither of them spoke. The campus lights glowed in the warm dark, and somewhere a bell was ringing.

"I wanted to say," Himeno said slowly, "that you've been wonderful this month. With the class. With the vote."

"I didn't do anything."

"You read the ballots aloud. Every single one. I don't think I could have."

He blushed to his ears. "It was just names."

"It was thirty-eight people trusting you."

"Yes," said Sakaki. His smile faded. "Yes, I suppose it was."

"Is something bothering you?"

"No."

"Sakaki-kun."

"I'm *fine,* Himeno-san."

She waited. After a long time, he spoke in a very small voice.

"Do you ever feel like you've been given a job by someone who doesn't tell you what it's for? And you're good at it. And you like being good at it. But you're a little afraid of what it's *for?*"

She went very still.

"What is the job?"

"I can't say."

"Can you tell me who gave it?"

"I can't say that either." His eyes were brimming. "I promised. I promised I wouldn't ask questions."

"Sakaki-kun..."

"Please don't ask me again."

He gathered his tablet, bowed, and walked away quickly across the lawn. She watched him go. She did not call after him. Her hands were cold, and her chest ached, and she realized with a kind of slow horror that she had never in her life wanted so badly to save someone and had so little idea how.

On the far side of the lawn, a boy in a tidy uniform stood in the shadow of a tree, watching them both.

He had been there for a long time.

He did not follow Sakaki. He did not approach Himeno. He turned, in his own time, and walked toward the dormitory with an even, patient stride, and in the glow of a lamppost, for a single moment, his shadow stretched across the grass and touched hers.

Then it moved on.



# Chapter 19: Sugar

The invitation arrived on a plain white card slid under Sakaki Rio's door.

*Tea. Conference room, 3:10. Bring the notebook. —W.*

It was written in a hurried scrawl, and it smelled faintly of sugar. Sakaki read it four times, and then, because he had no one to ask, he put it in his pocket and went to class and sat through three periods of mathematics without absorbing a single line.

At 3:09, he stood outside the old staff conference room with his hand raised to knock.

"It's open," called a voice.

Inside, the room was empty except for a bench, a kettle, and a man in a rumpled shirt with his feet on the table. The wall of photographs had been taken down. The cables were gone. The laptop with the white sticker had been moved to a shelf, closed.

"Sakaki-kun," said Wataru. "Please sit. Do you take sugar?"

"I...no. Thank you."

"Nobody does. They just don't know it." He poured a cup anyway and put in five cubes. "Don't be nervous. You are not in trouble."

"I'm not nervous."

"Of course you are. Your hands are shaking."

Sakaki looked down. They were.

"I have a question," said Wataru. "A very simple one. I want you to answer it honestly, and I want you to understand that nothing you say will go beyond this room." He sipped. "Who is Ø?"

The color left Sakaki's face.

"I don't know."

"That's a true answer. I can tell. Most liars say 'I don't know' too quickly." He tilted his head. "Do you know who sends you your instructions?"

"I...I can't..."

"You have a private channel. Eight o'clock every evening. You relay what you're told to the Task Force at nine. You have been doing it for a month."

"How did you..."

"Because I was the one who noticed that the laptop answered at 9:00 every evening, and that the messages were pasted, not typed." He smiled gently. "You paste very neatly, Sakaki-kun. It's the best thing about you."

Sakaki's eyes filled. "I promised."

"I know."

"I promised I wouldn't tell anybody. I promised I wouldn't ask who it was."

"And you haven't. You have kept every promise. That is exactly why I trust you." Wataru put down his cup. "I am not going to ask you to stop. I would like you to continue exactly as you have been. Take the instructions. Paste them. Send them."

"But..."

"And I would like you to do one more thing. Each evening, before you paste, I would like you to read me the message first. Privately. In this room. Without telling the person who sends them."

"That's..." Sakaki's voice cracked. "That would be lying to them."

"Yes."

"To someone who trusts me."

"Does he?" asked Wataru, very softly.

The question hung in the air.

Sakaki looked at his hands. A single tear slid down his cheek and dropped, with a tiny sound, onto the cover of the notebook in his lap.

"I don't know," he whispered.

"Then perhaps we should find out." Wataru leaned forward. His eyes, so often half-closed, were wide and dark and terribly gentle. "I will not hurt you. I will not let anyone hurt you. But I need you to help me understand what this person wants. And to do that, I need you to be braver than you have ever been."

For a long, long minute, Sakaki did not speak.

"I'll do it," he said, at last.

"Thank you."

"Please don't tell Aizawa-kun."

Wataru's expression did not change. "Why would I tell Aizawa-kun?"

"I don't know." Sakaki wiped his face with his sleeve. "I just. He's been so kind. If he knew I was keeping something from him, he'd..." He shook his head. "He'd be *understanding.* That's the worst part. He'd be so understanding I wouldn't be able to stand it."

"I see," said Wataru.

He did not write it down. He did not need to.

---

In the common room that afternoon, Mio Hanazono sat across from Ren with a cup of tea she had not touched.

"Aizawa-kun," she said. "May I tell you something?"

"Of course."

"There's a woman. She's been coming to the gate." Her fingers tightened on the cup. "She says she's from the place I grew up. I lived in a children's home when I was little. She was in charge of one of the floors."

"I see."

"I don't know why she's here. I haven't seen her in years. I thought..." She laughed, thinly. "I thought I'd left it behind."

"What does she want?"

"I don't know. She only asks if I've been good."

"And have you?"

"I've always been good," said Mio, with a trace of bitterness. "It's the thing I'm best at."

Ren listened without interrupting. When she finished, he waited a moment, with the look of a man considering whether to speak.

"I'm sorry that she frightens you," he said.

"She doesn't frighten me. Not exactly." Mio hesitated. "It's more that when I see her, I feel like someone's holding a door open that I don't want to walk through."

"That's a good description."

"Do you think I should tell her to stay away?"

"I think you should do what feels safest," said Ren. "And I think you should tell me if she comes again."

"You wouldn't mind?"

"I'd prefer it."

Her eyes lifted to his. They were wet, and grateful, and entirely unsuspecting, and she did not notice that he had not asked the woman's name, or the name of the home, or anything at all that a person who did not already know the answer would have wanted to ask.

---

At 8:00 p.m., the first of the new instructions arrived.

Sakaki read it twice. Then, hands trembling, he walked down the corridor to the old conference room, knocked once, and let himself in.

"It's here," he said.

Wataru was lying on the bench with the cushion over his face. He lifted one corner. "Read it."

He read it aloud.

*The Task Force should shift its focus to the dormitory network. A student there may be using a proxy server to mask his activity. I want a detailed audit of every login on terminal three over the last six weeks. Do not alert anyone.*

"Terminal three," said Wataru. "Again."

"Is that bad?"

"It's very interesting." He sat up, hair on end. "Whoever is running the puppet wants us to look at terminal three. Because it's where the ghost lived. He wants us to find the ghost."

"Why?"

"Because he wants us to find him *and nobody else.*" Wataru stared at the ceiling. "Someone set that terminal up as a decoy. Someone is about to hand us a victim."

"Who?"

"I don't know. But I will tell you what I think." He smiled his thin, bright smile. "I think the person who is about to be framed is going to be somebody who does not exist."

Sakaki frowned. "How can you frame someone who doesn't exist?"

"By inventing him," said Wataru, "very carefully, one login at a time."

---

At 10:40 p.m., in the dormitory, Ren Aizawa sat at his desk with a plain black notebook open before him.

He was not writing in it. He was looking at a page of Sakaki's handwriting, a photograph he had taken that evening, in the four minutes Sakaki had left his notebook on the common room table, of every entry in the class representative's log. Date. Subject. Who delivered it.

*June 3, 3:10 p.m., conference room, W.*

He studied the line for exactly eleven seconds.

Then he opened his drawer, and took out a small cream envelope with a lantern pin clipped to its flap, and set it beside the notebook, and looked at them both.

He did not reach for a pen. He did not touch his phone. He sat motionless in the light of the desk lamp, a boy in a pressed school uniform with two things in front of him that he had not yet decided what to do with.

For a very long time, there was no sound but the clock.

Then he closed the notebook, and put out the lamp.



# Chapter 20: Of Course

On Thursday morning, Ren Aizawa passed Sakaki Rio in the corridor.

"Good morning, Sakaki-kun."

"Good morning!" Sakaki said, a little too brightly. He had dark circles under his eyes. "How are you?"

"Well. And you?"

"Fine. Fine. Busy."

"I saw Mr. Wataru in the corridor yesterday. He seemed tired."

"Did he?" Sakaki's grip tightened on his tablet. "I wouldn't know. I haven't seen him."

"No?"

"No." He laughed. It was a small, high, miserable sound. "I've been in the library, mostly."

"That's wise," said Ren. He smiled. It was the kindest smile Sakaki had ever seen. "You should look after yourself. If anyone asks something difficult of you, you can always come to me."

"I know," said Sakaki, and his eyes shone, and he fled.

Ren stood in the corridor for four seconds. Then he went on his way, and when he reached the Task Force's door, he knocked on it twice, politely, as if he had been summoned.

He had not been. That was the point.

---

Mr. Wataru liked to say that the most dangerous moment in any investigation is the moment you stop being afraid of the suspect.

He did not say it aloud. He said it, instead, to a cup of tea, in the staff conference room, on the first Thursday of June, with rain ticking against the glass and the sky outside the window the color of dishwater.

"You're very quiet today," said a voice from the doorway.

"I'm always quiet."

"No. Usually you're lying on the bench."

"I'm *thinking* on the bench. It's a different activity." He lifted his head. "Come in, Aizawa-kun."

Ren Aizawa crossed the threshold with his usual even pace. He wore his uniform neatly buttoned, and carried a folder of homework under one arm, and he bowed at the correct angle before he sat in the visitor's chair.

"You asked to see me, sir."

"I did. I want to thank you." Wataru poured a second cup. "For your help with the Honda case. Your observations about the incident log were very useful."

"I'm glad."

"You also saved a girl's life. You kept a frightened class together. You solved a puzzle that three adults couldn't." He added sugar. "I wonder if you realize how unusual that is."

"I think I was lucky."

"Do you?"

"I think I was in the right place."

"Hm." Wataru stirred. "I have a theory about luck. It's that it is the name people give to preparation they didn't witness."

Ren said nothing. He took the offered cup in both hands and held it without drinking.

"I have a confession," said Wataru. "I have been rude to you. For six weeks I have been watching you from over the top of a cushion. I apologize."

"There's no need."

"I was looking for a murderer. And you were the most obvious candidate in the school." He smiled, an apologetic, crooked smile. "Calm. Careful. Always nearby. Always helpful. I've been trained to distrust helpful people."

"That seems reasonable."

"It is. And I was wrong." Wataru spread his hands. "I've reviewed everything. The alibis. The timeline. The twelve witnesses in the library. It's an impossible schedule for one student to keep. Whoever's doing this is operating from the shadows, and you are, I'm afraid, merely a good boy in a bad place."

Ren inclined his head. "Thank you, sir."

"So I wanted to tell you. I'm closing your file." He lifted the cup. "A very *good* student, Aizawa-kun. Truly. You've been an enormous help to the Task Force."

There was a pause. In the tick of the rain, in the faint pale light, in the steam rising from two cups, something passed across the room, a very small thing, so brief that if you had not been looking for it you would have called it nothing.

Ren's eyes met Wataru's, and held them, and the corner of his mouth moved in the smallest, most courteous way.

"Of course," he said.

Wataru did not move.

He did not blink. He did not breathe. He sat with the cup halfway to his lips and the cushion beside him and his rumpled hair standing up in the gray light, and in the space of one second his entire face went still, the way a pond goes still when a stone has already sunk.

It was the third time he had heard it.

The first had been the voice on the broadcast, on the first night. *Of course.*

The second, in this very room, from Ø. *Of course.*

And now, from a boy of sixteen who had spoken it as a person speaks his own name.

"You say that quite often," said Wataru.

"Do I?"

"I've noticed. A habit."

"I'm told I'm very polite."

"Yes." Wataru set down his cup. His hand did not shake. "You are the most polite person I have ever met."

"Thank you, sir."

"May I give you some advice?"

"Please."

"Do not be too good at it," said Wataru. "The people who notice are usually the ones you can least afford."

Ren rose. He set his untouched cup on the table, in the exact center of the saucer, and bowed.

"I'll remember that," he said. "Thank you for the tea."

He left.

For a full minute the room was silent. Then the door at the back opened, and Himeno Saya stepped in from the adjoining closet, where she had been sitting on a stack of boxes for forty minutes with her hands pressed over her own mouth.

"You said you were closing his file," she whispered.

"I did."

"You *lied* to him."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because he lied to me first." Wataru lay back on the bench and put the cushion over his face. His voice came through it, muffled and tired and oddly steady. "And because there is a rule in this business, Miss Himeno, which Ø taught me. The man you trust least is the man you must let believe he is unobserved."

"You *know.*"

"I know," said Wataru. "I know the voice on the laptop is a puppet. I know the man at the morgue was Ø, or someone who loved him. I know that the most polite boy in your class has just said two words to me that he has said to no one else in this school, and that he has said them *on purpose.*"

"On purpose?"

"He wanted me to hear it." Wataru took the cushion away. His eyes, wide and dark, were fixed on the ceiling. "He wanted me to know that he knows that I know. And he wanted me to know that he's not afraid."

Himeno's knees weakened. She sat down hard on a box.

"So what do we do?"

"Nothing," said Wataru. "For now. We let him feel safe. We let him believe he has won. And we find out who is standing behind the person who's standing behind the puppet."

"There's someone else?"

"There is always someone else." He smiled, thin and bright and terribly tired. "Ø taught me that, too."

Outside the window, in the courtyard, a boy in a tidy uniform crossed the gravel at an even pace. He did not look up at the window. He did not look back. In his left hand he carried a plain black notebook, and the wind lifted the corner of the cover once, and let it fall.

He had not been surprised at all.
