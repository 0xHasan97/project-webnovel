# Chapter 21: The Ghost Has a Name

The audit of terminal three took the Task Force nine days.

It was grinding, tedious work. Four detectives sat in the old conference room with a printout of six weeks of logins, a pot of strong tea, and a growing dislike of one another. They cross-referenced every session against dormitory access records, library reservations, and the meal-swipe system. Each evening at nine they reported to the laptop with the white sticker, and each evening the flat white line replied with a calm, precise instruction.

And each evening, in the small hours, Mr. Wataru lay on his bench with the cushion over his face and watched them walk into it.

"Here," said the gray-haired detective, on the ninth day.

He laid a sheet of paper on the table. It was a column of timestamps, forty-one of them, highlighted in yellow.

"The ghost used terminal three forty-one times. Every one of those sessions was opened using a single student ID. Not the fake one. A real one."

"Whose?"

"Kisaki Haru. Class 1-D." The detective sat back. "First-year. Bottom of his class. Seventeen Merit Points."

"Seventeen," said Wataru, from under the cushion.

"One more bad week and he's expelled."

"Hm." Wataru lifted the cushion by an inch. "That's very convenient."

"It's evidence."

"It's *wonderful* evidence. It has a name and a face. It has a motive, because a boy with seventeen points will do almost anything for a hundred." He sat up slowly. "It's exactly what a clever person would leave for us."

"You don't think he's guilty?"

"I think Kisaki Haru is a boy who is frightened of being expelled," said Wataru. "And I think that somebody who understands fear very well has made use of it."

The detective frowned. "Should we arrest him?"

"No."

"But the laptop said..."

"The laptop says what it's told." Wataru smiled his thin, bright smile. "We will detain him for questioning. We will be kind. We will give him tea and a blanket. And then we will let a few days go by and see who comes to visit."

---

Mr. Kurata kept a small office on the third floor, with a window that faced the sea and a shelf of books he never lent to anyone.

On Thursday afternoon, he asked Ren Aizawa to stay behind.

"I want to ask you something," he said. He did not sit. He stood by the window with his glasses pushed into his hair and his hands in his pockets. "It will sound strange."

"Please."

"The vote. The unanimous ballots. I have been trying to recall who suggested it."

"I believe the class arrived at it together."

"Yes. That is what I told the headmistress." Mr. Kurata turned. "But I remember sitting at my desk on the Monday before the exam. I remember writing the four rules. And I remember, very clearly, writing *the nomination must be unanimous* and thinking, *there is a way around this.*"

"That's very perceptive of you, sensei."

"It is not perceptive. It is a memory." He rubbed his eyes. "I have taught for twenty-one years. I have set that exam three times. Twice, the class tore itself apart. I wanted to see what would happen. I think I wanted someone to find the loophole."

"Then you were fortunate."

"Was I?"

Ren looked at him with courteous interest. "I'm not sure what you're asking me, sensei."

"Nor am I." Mr. Kurata let out a short, tired laugh. "I only wished to say that if there is a thing a teacher is permitted to be afraid of, it is that he has begun to do someone else's thinking. And not to know whose."

"I don't think you need to be afraid."

"No?"

"I think you are a good teacher," said Ren. "I think the class is lucky."

Mr. Kurata studied him for a long moment. Then, quietly, he said, "Thank you, Aizawa. You may go."

When the door had closed, the teacher stood for a long time at the window. Then he took the folded scrap of paper from his breast pocket, the one he had written months ago, and he held it in his hand and did not unfold it.

---

At 6:00 that evening, Himeno Saya waited outside the door of Class 1-D.

She had never been inside the room. In the school's unspoken geography, 1-D was a place you did not go, like the east end of a hospital. The class stood last in the standings, and everyone in it knew it, and the knowledge had settled into the posture of every student who came out through the door.

Kisaki Haru was the last to leave.

He was small and narrow-shouldered, with a pinched face and hair that stood up at the back. He carried a bag too big for him. He saw Himeno and stopped, and a look crossed his face of such complete, helpless fear that she felt it like a physical blow.

"I'm not going to hurt you," she said.

"I didn't do anything."

"I know."

"Everyone says I did." His voice cracked. "They've been saying it since this morning. The Task Force came and asked for my tablet. They were *nice.* They gave me a juice box. I don't know what I..."

"Kisaki-kun." She held up both hands. "Please. Let's sit down."

They sat on the bench by the stairwell. She waited until his breathing slowed. Then she said, as gently as she knew how, "Did someone ask you to lend them your login?"

He stared at her.

"I can see that someone did," she said.

"It was a game," he whispered.

"Tell me."

"I...I got a message. On the board. A while ago. In April, after the first review." His hands twisted in his lap. "It said they knew I was in trouble. That I only had seventeen points. They said if I let a friend use my account sometimes, for a computer thing, they'd put a hundred points on it every month. No one would know."

"A hundred a month."

"I thought it was a prank. But then the points came. And I thought, it's just a login. What harm can it do?" He covered his face. "I never saw who it was. I never met them. They only wrote. They were so *kind.*"

"What did the messages say?"

"Things like..." He swallowed. "Things like, *You're doing so well. I'm sure the next review will be better. Don't worry, I'll look after you.*"

Himeno closed her eyes.

She knew that voice. She had heard it in the corridor, in the class, on the stairs. She had heard it say, *I'll stay with you.*

"Kisaki-kun," she said. "Did they ever use the word *noted?*"

His head came up. His red eyes were wide.

"How did you know?"

---

At 9:00 that night, in the north library, Kuze Minato read Himeno's account in complete silence.

When she finished, he laid down his pen, took off his glasses, and rubbed the bridge of his nose. He looked very tired.

"I have been wondering," he said, "why he has stopped making mistakes."

"He didn't make one."

"He did. It is a small one." He put the glasses back on. "The word *noted.* Eight weeks ago he used it carelessly in a school form. Now he has used it in a private message to a frightened boy, who repeated it to you."

"That's not a mistake. That's evidence."

"No. It is a *habit,* Himeno-san. It is something he cannot help." He tapped the table. "Which means that the man we are watching is not perfect. He has weaknesses. He has things that he does without noticing. And somebody else, sooner or later, is going to notice them too."

"Wataru."

"Perhaps." He looked at her for a moment. "Or perhaps someone cleverer."

She shivered.

"You think there's someone cleverer than Wataru?"

"I think there is someone who has *been watching Wataru.* And I think it is the same person who sent the card to Aizawa." His voice dropped. "I think we have misunderstood the shape of the game. We believed there were two players. Perhaps there are three."

A long silence followed.

"Who is the third?" Himeno whispered.

Kuze looked down at the notebook, at the two words he had written on a page a few days ago and had not been able to cross out.

*Lantern House.*

"The one who taught him to be like this," he said. "In the first place."


# Chapter 22: Seventeen Points

Kisaki Haru was detained on a Friday and released on a Monday.

They had not charged him. The Task Force simply asked, over three days, a series of gentle, repetitive questions. They brought him meals. They let him call his mother. On Monday afternoon, a detective escorted him to the gate and said, in a kind, flat voice, "We may need to speak to you again."

"Am I...am I free?"

"For now."

He walked across the lawn alone with his oversized bag, shoulders hunched. Nobody spoke to him. A small circle of students formed around him as he moved and parted as he passed, the way water parts around a stone that it has decided is poisonous.

By the time he reached the dormitory, his Merit Points had dropped to nine.

It was the school's doing. The review committee, as a matter of policy, deducted points from any student named in a formal investigation. He sat on his bed with his tablet in his hands and stared at the number.

*Nine.*

Below it, a notification.

*Your Merit Points are below the threshold of survival. At zero, you will be removed from the institution.*

He did not cry. He had run out of that. He sat for an hour with his back against the wall, thinking about his mother in a flat in a town three hours away who worked double shifts to pay for the uniform he wore.

At 8:00, his tablet chimed.

It was a private message. No sender, no avatar. A single line.

*I know it's been a hard week. Would you like to talk?*

---

In the north library, Kuze Minato looked up from his notebook as Himeno Saya dropped into the seat across from him.

"He's talking to Kisaki again," she said. Her face was white with fury.

"How do you know?"

"I just left him. Kisaki let me read the message." She slammed her hand on the table. "He's going to do it again. He's going to take a boy who has nothing left and put one more weight on him."

"Yes."

"We have to warn him."

"We cannot." Kuze did not raise his voice. "Not without revealing that we know. It would tell Aizawa that you spoke to Kisaki. It would end whatever advantage we have."

"Then we let him be *used?*"

"We watch," said Kuze. "We cannot do anything else."

"That isn't good enough."

"No." His gray eyes were steady. "It is not. But it is what we have."

Himeno stood. She stood with her hands curled into fists at her sides, trembling, and for a moment he thought she might walk out and do something she would regret. Then, slowly, her shoulders dropped. She sat back down.

"Tell me what we're watching for."

"The message will ask him for something." Kuze picked up his pen. "And whatever it asks, it will be small. It will be the smallest thing in the world. It will cost him nothing. And it will be the last step before something else."

---

At 8:20, Kisaki Haru read the second message on his tablet.

*I can help. I have a connection who can arrange for your points to be reviewed. A clerical error, perhaps. An appeal. But I need to be careful, because the Task Force is watching.*

*Would you mind if I borrowed your account one last time?*

*It would only be for a single evening. And when it's done, I'll make sure everything is put right.*

He stared at it. He thought of his mother. He thought of the juice box. He thought, with sudden, desperate clarity, that the person who had written these words had never once been unkind to him, and that everyone else in the school had.

*What would you do with it?* he typed.

*Nothing that could hurt you. I give you my word.*

*Why are you helping me?*

The answer took four seconds.

*Because no one else did.*

His hands shook as he typed.

*Ok.*

*Thank you,* said the voice. *Go to bed, Kisaki-kun. Everything will be all right.*

He closed the tablet. He lay down. For the first time in nine days, he slept.

---

In the morning, the Task Force received an anonymous tip.

It was a short, clean message, typed in a neutral voice and routed through the same school channel as every message before. It said that a student named Kisaki Haru would attempt to use terminal three that evening at 9:15 p.m. to delete evidence of his crimes.

"Do we act?" asked the gray-haired detective.

"Of course," said the laptop.

"Should we apprehend him at the terminal?"

"Yes. Take two men. Be discreet."

Mr. Wataru, on his bench, did not speak. He lay with his cushion over his face and his hands folded on his chest like a man being measured for a coffin. When the session ended, and the detectives left to prepare, he sat up very slowly and looked at Sakaki Rio, who was standing in the doorway with a stricken face.

"It's a frame," Sakaki whispered.

"Yes."

"You said we'd stop him."

"I said we would *understand* him." Wataru got to his feet. "Come. We have until 9:15."

"What are we going to do?"

"Something very rude," said Wataru, and for the first time in many weeks, he was not smiling.

---

At 8:31 p.m., someone knocked on the door of Room 114 in the first-year dormitory.

Kisaki Haru opened it a crack. A thin man in a rumpled shirt stood in the corridor, holding a paper cup.

"Hello," said Wataru. "You'll remember me. The juice box."

"I...yes."

"May I come in?"

"I'm not allowed visitors."

"No, you aren't." Wataru did not move. "I'm sorry. I'm going to say something unkind, and I'd like to do it quickly."

Kisaki said nothing. He stood in the narrow gap with his hand on the door.

"At 9:15 tonight, two detectives will be waiting at terminal three. They have been told you will come and delete evidence. They will arrest you. You will be formally charged by morning." Wataru's voice was low and quite gentle. "The person who has been so kind to you has arranged this."

"That's not true."

"I think you know it is."

"He *helped* me."

"He gave you a hundred points a month so that you would be tired enough to say yes." Wataru lowered his cup. "I'm sorry. I truly am. I have been watching him use a great many people. You are the one I like best, because you are the only one who has not been frightened into silence."

Kisaki's face crumpled. He pressed the heel of his hand to his eyes.

"Why would you tell me?"

"Because I gave you a juice box," said Wataru, "and I felt terrible about it afterward."

It was such an absurd thing to say that Kisaki laughed, once, helplessly, wetly, and Wataru smiled.

"Don't go to the terminal. Go to the common room. Study. Be seen by as many people as you can, for as long as you can." He turned to leave. "And if someone asks why, tell them the truth. Tell them you changed your mind."

He walked away down the corridor. Kisaki closed the door and slid to the floor with his back against it, and for a long time he sat in the dark, listening to the sound of his own heart, thinking that he had never in his life been told something so frightening that made him feel so much less alone.

---

At 9:12 that night, Kisaki Haru walked into the dormitory common room.

He did not go to terminal three. He sat down at the long table near the window, opened his math textbook, and began, with enormous concentration, to work through a page of equations.

At 9:15, two detectives in plain clothes took up positions behind terminal three. They waited. The minutes crawled.

At 9:30, a tablet chimed at the long table.

Kisaki Haru looked at it. It was a short message from the voice that was always so kind.

*Why aren't you at the terminal?*

He swallowed. He typed, with trembling fingers:

*I changed my mind.*

*Why?*

*Because a man gave me a juice box.* He stared at the screen. *And I thought, if I am going to be punished, I want it to be for something I did.*

There was no reply for a long time.

Then, at 9:41, a single word appeared.

*Noted.*

And the tablet went dark.


# Chapter 23: The Matron

On the morning of the last day of June, Himeno Saya found Ren Aizawa in the north library.

He was sitting at a window desk with a book on number theory open before him. She stood opposite him until he looked up.

"Good morning, Himeno-san."

"Kisaki Haru did not go to the terminal."

"I heard."

"You sent him a message. Twice."

"I don't know what you mean."

"He showed me. *Noted.*" She said the word like an accusation. "It was you."

He closed the book on one finger. "I think someone may have wished to make it appear that way."

"Don't."

"Forgive me?"

"*Don't,*" said Himeno, and her voice shook. "Don't do the voice. Don't be gentle. I have watched you do that to every person in this school for three months. I know what it is." She leaned forward over the desk. "You are using them."

For a moment he regarded her in silence. It was a courteous, level regard, and there was nothing behind it that she could read, not anger, not guilt, not the slightest flicker of fear.

"I am sorry that you feel that way," said Ren.

"That's all you have to say?"

"I think it is the only honest thing I could say."

"It isn't honest. It's *nothing.*"

"Yes," he agreed. "That is why."

He rose, tucked the book under his arm, and bowed. She stood with her fists clenched on the desk as he walked out of the library, and it was a full minute before she noticed that she was crying.

---

On the last day of June, a woman in a gray coat came to the gates of Aster Institute and asked for Ren Aizawa by name.

It was a hot afternoon. The cherry trees hung heavy with leaves, and the air above the hill road shimmered, and the guard at the gate, Mr. Taguchi, mopped his neck with a handkerchief and looked at her with something close to alarm. She was too composed for the heat. She stood in the sun in a heavy wool coat as if she had never felt weather in her life.

"I'm sorry, ma'am. Visitors have to be registered."

"I understand." She smiled. "I am a friend of the family."

"Which family?"

"His." She said it gently, as one might correct a child. "Would you please let him know I am here? I will wait."

Mr. Taguchi sent for him.

Ren Aizawa came down the hill road at an even pace. He had the same plain bag over his shoulder and the same small, polite half-smile. He stopped at the gate and bowed to the woman, correctly, the angle of a student greeting an elder.

"Good afternoon," he said.

"Good afternoon." Her eyes moved over him slowly, from his hair to his shoes, with the unhurried attention of someone checking a work she had completed long ago. "You have grown."

"Yes."

"You are taller than I expected."

"I'm told so."

"Walk with me."

It was not a request. She turned and began to walk along the outer wall, under the shade of the trees, and after a moment he fell into step beside her. Mr. Taguchi watched them go. At a window in the third-floor corridor, a pale boy with a notebook watched them too.

---

They walked for six minutes without speaking.

The path ran along the cliff edge above the sea. The wind carried salt and the distant call of gulls. Far below, waves broke against rocks the color of old iron, and the woman in gray stopped at the railing and looked down.

"The doctor is well," she said.

"I'm glad."

"He asks about you. Every day. He has kept your room as it was."

"That's kind of him."

"He wishes to see you."

"I'm busy."

"I told him you would say so." She smiled, a little. "He said to tell you that it is not a matter of urgency. He said that you will come when you are ready, because you are a child who always completes what he begins."

Ren said nothing. The wind pulled at his collar.

"I have been told to ask you a question," the woman said. "And then I will go."

"Please."

"Do you still count the stairs?"

For the first time that anyone could later recall, there was a pause before he answered.

It was a short one. It lasted perhaps one second, and in it nothing moved, not his eyes, not his hands, not his breath. A gull cried overhead. The sea went on striking the rocks.

"Yes," said Ren.

"How many at the school?"

"Two hundred and sixty-four to the roof. Two hundred and nine to the north tower."

"And at Lantern House?"

"Eighty-eight."

"Good." She turned from the rail. "He will be pleased. He always said you had the most beautiful memory."

"Matron."

"Yes?"

"Mio Hanazono has done nothing you asked."

For a moment the woman in the gray coat studied him. Her face did not change, but something deep in it altered, like a lamp turned down.

"I know," she said. "That is why she is still here."

"She isn't part of this."

"Isn't she?"

"No."

"Child." Her voice was almost tender. "Everyone is part of this. You taught me that. You taught the doctor, too. It is the only thing you have ever taught anybody." She lifted a gloved hand and, very lightly, touched his cheek. "Eat. Sleep. Be good. We will speak again soon."

She walked away along the cliff path. Her coat did not stir in the wind.

Ren stood at the railing for a long time, looking at the sea.

---

Kuze Minato found him there at dusk.

He approached slowly, with the notebook closed in his hand. He stopped five paces away, because it felt like the right distance, and stood in the long light with his shadow stretched out behind him.

"Aizawa-kun."

"Kuze-kun."

"I was watching from the window."

"I know."

"I do not know what she said."

"No."

"But I know what you said back." He took a breath. "Two hundred and sixty-four."

Ren turned his head.

"I counted them," Kuze said. "On the first day. I thought I was the only one who did. I thought it was a private habit." His voice was very quiet. "I did not think anyone else in the school climbed the roof stairs and *knew.*"

The wind hummed in the cliff rail.

"There is something about you," said Kuze, "that I recognize. I do not mean the calm. I do not mean the cleverness. I mean a thing underneath. A habit of being so careful that you have forgotten what it was you were afraid of."

"You are very observant."

"It is all I am."

"Then you must be very tired."

Kuze smiled. It was a small, surprised smile, and it was the first one anybody had seen on his face. "Yes," he said. "I am."

They stood for a moment longer. The light turned red on the sea.

"Aizawa-kun. Whoever she is, whatever she wants. If you were to ask me for help..."

"I would not."

"No," said Kuze. "I do not suppose you would."

He bowed, and went back along the cliff path toward the school. When he was out of sight, Ren Aizawa took a plain black notebook from his pocket, opened it, and wrote two short lines in a very small hand.

Then he closed it and put it away, and walked back the way he had come, counting.


# Chapter 24: The Closing Statement

On the first Monday in July, the National Police Agency convened a press conference.

It was held in a gray conference hall, in front of a gray curtain, under cold white light. A row of men in dark suits sat behind a table with microphones. At the center, with a printed statement in his hand, sat a mild, tired official with thinning hair.

"Good morning," he said. "I am here to announce the conclusion of the Task Force's investigation into the deaths at Aster Institute."

Flashbulbs. A murmur from the benches.

"After a thorough review, the Task Force has determined that the deaths of three students and one third-year were the result of a series of accidents and one confirmed suicide, compounded by the concealment efforts of a student named Honda Ryuu, who has confessed to the theft of forty-seven school tablets and has been remanded to a juvenile facility."

"What about the broadcasts from Ø?" shouted a reporter.

"The consultant known as Ø has concluded his advisory role. He has asked me to thank the public for its patience." The official looked up. "He considers the matter closed."

"Is the killer still at large?"

"There is no evidence of a killer."

"And the murders?"

"There were no murders." His voice was steady. "There was a tragic pattern. The Task Force has found that pattern to be coincidental."

A stunned silence. Then pandemonium.

---

In the dormitory common room at Aster Institute, a hundred students watched the screen in silence.

It lasted four minutes. When the footage cut to a reporter on the lawn outside, Sakaki Rio, who had been sitting on the arm of a couch with his hands clasped, let out a long, shaking breath.

"It's over," said someone.

"They said it was coincidence."

"They said *Ø* said so."

"Does that mean we're safe?"

Nobody answered. A girl began to cry softly. A boy in the corner laughed, high and wild, and was shushed. In the middle of the room, in the second row of sofas, Mio Hanazono sat with her knees pulled to her chest and her eyes fixed on the dark screen.

"Aizawa-kun," she whispered.

"Yes."

"Is it really over?"

He was standing at her shoulder, hands folded behind his back, looking at the screen without blinking.

"The Task Force has said so," said Ren.

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." His voice was low and kind. "I think it is over for now. I think things will be quieter."

"That sounds like you're not sure."

"I am never sure," he said, and she laughed, a little, because it was such an odd and honest thing to say.

At the back of the room, by the window, Himeno Saya stood with her arms folded and watched his face. She was looking for something. A crack. A flicker. A small, pleased, private satisfaction at the shape of an outcome that he had so carefully arranged.

She found nothing.

*He is not even relieved,* she thought. And, for the first time, it frightened her more than anything else about him.

---

In the county morgue, Detective Sato switched off the television in the break room and lit a cigarette.

"Coincidence," he said, to nobody. "A tragic pattern."

At his elbow, a young technician in a white coat held out a folder. "The fingerprint results came back on the cardigan man, sir. I thought you'd want to see."

Sato opened it. He read for a long time. Then he stubbed out the cigarette and dialed a number he had written on the back of his hand.

"Wataru."

"It's very late, Detective."

"You wanted his name."

A silence. A rustle, as if someone had sat up quickly.

"Mikado Soichi," said Sato. "Thirty-one. A translator and part-time lecturer. Linguistics. Reported missing in April by a sister in Sendai. Last known contact was a freelance contract with an unlisted client." He turned a page. "There's a note in the file. The sister said that he had been very happy about the contract. He said it was the first time in his life anyone had asked him to be quiet for a living."

Wataru said nothing.

"He was a translator," said Sato. "He wasn't Ø. He couldn't have been. Ø has been running cases for twelve years. This man was a hired voice."

"Yes."

"Which means the man you've been talking to..."

"I know," said Wataru. "Thank you, Detective."

"What will you do?"

"I will go on," said Wataru. "I always do."

He hung up. In the dark of the conference room, with the television still blaring the closing statement of the case, he sat with the phone in his hand and the name in his head and the dead man's smile in his memory, the smile of a man who had been hired to be a face and had decided, at the end, to be a person.

*I'm sorry,* he thought. *I should have asked your name in May.*

---

At 8:00 that evening, Sakaki Rio read the instructions on his tablet and went straight to the conference room.

"It's here," he said.

Wataru lay on the bench with his hands behind his head. "Read it."

"*The Task Force has served its purpose. The matter should be considered closed. I am grateful for your service. I will be in touch.*" Sakaki swallowed. "That's all. It's...it's goodbye."

"No."

"It isn't?"

"It is *the first line of a letter.*" Wataru sat up. He looked at the laptop on its shelf for a long time. "Whoever is writing this does not want us to stop. He wants us to *stop looking.* He wants us to go home and let him put the pieces back in the box."

"He's finished?"

"He is *nearly* finished." Wataru's eyes narrowed. "That is when a clever person is most careful, and most proud. He has an elegant ending. And he cannot resist watching to see if we believed it."

"What do we do?"

"We believe it. Loudly." Wataru rose, brushing crumbs from his shirt. "Tell the Task Force to pack. Tell them to be seen leaving. Tomorrow morning there will be vans on the lawn and boxes in the corridor, and the whole school will see it."

"And then?"

"And then," said Wataru, "I will stay."

"Alone?"

"I am the only man who has not been noticed since April." He smiled his old thin smile, and for a moment the weariness fell away from it, and what was beneath was something sharp and bright and almost gleeful. "It would be a shame to waste it."

---

On the roof of the north tower, after midnight, a boy in a tidy uniform stood at the rail with his notebook in his hand.

The city lay spread below him, a thousand small lights. A thin wind came from the sea. He watched the dark streets for a long time, and then, as he had done once before, he counted aloud, in a voice so soft it did not carry past his own sleeve.

"Two hundred and sixty-four."

He closed the notebook.

"Two hundred and sixty-five."

It was the sound of a boy adding one more stair to a staircase that had never been there.


# Chapter 25: Good Evening, Aizawa

On Tuesday morning, from the north library window, Himeno Saya and Kuze Minato watched the vans pull out through the gate.

"He's staying," said Himeno.

"Yes."

"Alone."

"He told Sakaki he would be the last man in the room." Kuze did not look up from the notebook. "It is a very dangerous thing to say aloud."

"We should do something."

"We are." He turned a page. "We are making a list of every person who enters the second floor. Sakaki has agreed to let us see the corridor log each night. It is not much."

"It's something."

"It is exactly as much as Wataru would allow." Kuze closed the notebook. "I do not think he wants help. I think he wants witnesses."

Himeno was quiet for a moment. Then, in a small voice: "Do you think he's afraid?"

"I think he is the only one of us who knows what he is afraid *of.*" Kuze rose. "That is its own kind of courage."

They watched the last van disappear. The gate swung shut behind it with a long, hollow clang that echoed across the empty lawn.

---

The vans left on a Tuesday.

They rolled out through the iron gate at 10:00 in the morning, three black vehicles in a convoy, with their windows tinted and their license plates covered. The students lined the road to watch. A few waved. Most stood quite still, in the heat, in the green shade of the cherry trees, and said nothing at all.

By noon, the old conference room was empty.

The wall of photographs was gone. The long table was bare. In the corner, an electric kettle sat unplugged, and on a shelf by the window, closed, with a white sticker on its lid, rested a single laptop.

It was a Tuesday like any other. At 4:00, Class 1-C attended a lecture on comparative economics. At 5:30, the dormitory cafeteria served curry. At 7:00, a nervous first-year with a clipboard conducted a fire drill. By 9:00, the school was quiet.

And at 9:00 exactly, in a small, dim office on the second floor, a man in a rumpled shirt sat down in a chair that was far too large for him and opened the laptop.

He did not lie on the bench. He did not have a cushion. He sat very straight, with his hands flat on the desk and his hair standing out in all directions, and he looked at the screen.

A white line.

*Good evening,* it said.

He typed.

*Good evening.*

*It is quiet,* said the line. *The Task Force has gone. You are the last.*

*Yes,* said Wataru.

*I am pleased. I wanted to thank you for your service.*

*You are welcome.*

*Is there anything you would like to ask me before I close the file?*

Wataru looked at the question for some time. Outside the window, the lawn was dark, and the lights of the dormitories glowed in their long rows. He put his hands on the keys.

*Just one,* he typed.

*Please.*

*What is your name?*

A long pause.

It was so long that he began to wonder whether the line had cut out. The cursor blinked. A fan whirred. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, softly, with the sound of a person trying not to be heard.

Then the screen changed.

*I think you already know the answer, Mr. Wataru.*

*You have been circling it for weeks. Do you wish to say it first?*

His hands hovered over the keys. Then, slowly, deliberately, he typed:

*Mikado Soichi. Thirty-one. A translator from Sendai. He was kind to me, in his way. He said he'd like to meet me someday.*

*He was very kind,* the line replied. *It is a great pity.*

*Who are you?*

*I think you already know the answer, Mr. Wataru.*

His fingers stopped.

*I think you have known for some time. I think you have been very patient. And I think it is fair, now that we are alone, for me to say the same thing back.*

*Good evening.*

*Good evening, Wataru.*

---

At that moment, on the first floor of the south dormitory, in a room at the end of the corridor, Ren Aizawa sat at his desk in the glow of a single lamp.

He was not looking at a laptop. He was not looking at a screen. He had no device before him but a plain black notebook, and a pen, and a small cream envelope with a lantern pin clipped to its flap.

On the wall above his desk hung a clock. It ticked quietly.

He did not move.

The lamp's glow lay on his face, on the neat dark hair and the pleasant, forgettable features, and on a faint, steady expression that had been present, without change, since the day he arrived at Aster Institute. It had never shifted. It did not shift now.

And yet, if anyone had been watching, they would have seen that he was waiting.

At 9:07, a message arrived on his phone.

It had no sender. It came through no channel he had set up. It was a single line of text, and it had been written, he understood, by someone he had never met, who had been watching him, with patience, for far longer than he had known.

*Congratulations on your closing statement. It was very elegant.*

*I would like to offer you a trade.*

*Shall we speak?*

He read it once.

He did not answer at once. He sat with the pen in his fingers, looking at the screen, and the clock above his desk ticked eleven times. Then he wrote, in his small, even hand, on the first clean line of the notebook, and typed the same words into the phone.

*What are the terms?*

The reply came in four seconds.

*One question. One answer. Face to face. You may choose the place.*

*Why?*

*Because I have been very patient, and I would like to hear your voice before the winter.*

*The north tower observatory,* he typed. *Midnight.*

A pause. The phone's small light glowed in the dark of the room.

*Of course,* said the message.

It was the same phrase. It was the same, to the letter, and the same in rhythm, in weight, in the faint, courteous finality of its ending. Ren Aizawa had heard it said, in four months, by a mild man in a cardigan, by a voice on a broadcast, by a rumpled detective with a cushion on his face. He had said it himself, more often than he knew.

He had never before thought to ask where he had learned it.

He set down the phone. He picked up the pen.

---

It would be recorded later, in the notebook of a pale boy who had not been present, that at 9:08 p.m. on the second Tuesday of July, two things happened at Aster Institute at the same time.

In the second-floor office, a rumpled man lifted his hands from the keys of a laptop and said, aloud, to nobody, "He is not alone."

And in the south dormitory, a boy of sixteen set down his pen, and looked for the first time in four months at the envelope on his desk, and said to the empty room, in a voice so soft and so level that it might have been a line of poetry or a prayer:

"Of course."
