**THE QUIET VERDICT**

Volume 2: The Third Player

Chapters 26 to 30 (Part 1 of Volume 2)



# Chapter 26: The Doctor

The observatory at midnight smelled of cold brass and chamomile.

Ren Aizawa climbed the last stair at 11:58. Two hundred and nine, to the north tower. He did not slow on the final step. He pushed open the door and stood on the threshold of the round, domed room, with the telescope pointing at the dark and the star charts rustling on their rack, and he looked at the man who was already there.

He was sitting on the low step at the base of the telescope, a thermos beside him and a tin cup balanced on one knee. He was old, though not frail. His hair was white and thick and brushed straight back from a broad forehead, and he wore a tweed waistcoat over a soft gray shirt, and a pair of half-moon spectacles hung from a cord around his neck. A cane lay across his lap. He looked like a retired country physician who had wandered in out of the rain.

"Ah," he said, and his smile was warm, entirely, uncomplicatedly warm. "There you are."

"Good evening," said Ren.

"Please, come in. Close the door. I am too old for drafts." He lifted the thermos. "Chamomile. I took the liberty of bringing two cups. I know you do not care for coffee."

"Thank you."

"Sit. Sit." He patted the cold stone beside him. "I will not bite. I have not had the teeth for it in years."

Ren crossed the room. He sat, at a courteous distance, on the second step, with his plain black bag at his feet. The old man poured a second cup and handed it over with both hands, and Ren accepted it the same way, and for a moment they sat side by side in the lamplight like a grandfather and a grandson sharing a bench.

"You are taller than I expected," said the doctor.

"So I'm told."

"And thinner. Do they feed you here?"

"Yes."

"Good." He sipped. "I will say it plainly, because I am too old to be anything else. I am very proud of you."

Ren said nothing.

"The tower. The translator. The Task Force. The way you let that charming little man think he had you cornered." His eyes crinkled. "You did it all without once raising your voice. I could not have designed it better myself. And I *did* design it."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"You sent the card."

"I did." The doctor smiled. "But I do not mean the card. I mean the *test.*"

---

He told it simply, with the gentle, unhurried air of a man describing a hobby.

Twelve children had passed through the Quiet Program at Lantern House. Eleven had been placed in schools, in ministries, in boardrooms, wherever a mind of that kind might be useful or contained. The twelfth had been placed at Aster, in April, and told only to be himself.

"Aster was never a school," said the doctor. "I mean that fondly. It is an excellent school. But it was built, with the quiet help of a few foundations, as a place to watch a particular kind of child. A sealed room, so to speak, with a very large window."

"And Ø?"

"Ah." He set down his cup. "Ø was my little joke."

"Mikado Soichi."

"Poor Soichi. He translated for me for six years. A gentle man, entirely without ambition. When I asked him to be a voice, he said he would be delighted." The old man shook his head. "I wrote every line. The cardigan, the slippers, the little ritual with the glasses. He was only to sit in a room and read. I told him that he must ask each visitor one question at the end, and that it must always be the same."

"My name."

"Yes. It is my favorite question. It tells me so much." He patted his knee. "You did not answer. I was so pleased. I told Soichi, *If the boy answers, we have failed.*"

"He is dead."

"He is. And I am sorry. He was a kind man, and I think that is the one thing in you that I could not teach." There was no accusation in it. It was said with infinite tenderness. "You see, I wished to know whether you would kill a stranger who had done you no harm, simply because he was in your way."

"He asked."

"He asked what I told him to ask."

"He asked, and I did not wish him to know."

"Yes. That is the answer." The doctor sighed, deeply, with a kind of satisfaction. "It is the *only* answer. You were not angry. You were not afraid. You were only tidy." He sipped his tea. "You found his name, of course. I let him book the guest room under his own. A little carelessness, to see whether you would read it. Eleven seconds, I am told."

Ren said nothing.

"Eleven. You always wait eleven. Do you know why?" The old man's eyes crinkled. "Eleven children went before you. You were taught to give each of them a moment. I do not think you have ever stopped."

The boy's hand lay quite still on his knee, on the plain black notebook that he carried everywhere.

"And you keep that close, still," said the doctor, gently.

"It is only a notebook."

"Of course."

Ren set his cup on the stone. He did it quietly, exactly, without a ripple in the tea.

"What do you want?" he said.

"I want you to come home."

"No."

"I am old. I will not live forever. I have a house on the coast with a library and a kitchen and a very patient housekeeper, and I have a room for you. I would like you to finish your education where it began." He lifted a hand, as if to forestall objection. "You need not leave Aster at once. Finish the year. Graduate if you wish. But when you have done what you came here to do, come to me."

"And if I do not?"

"Then I will go on watching you." The doctor smiled. "And I will keep sending cards."

"And the others?"

"Which others?"

"The Task Force. The class. Himeno Saya. Kuze Minato." Ren's voice was exactly as it had always been, polite, level, unhurried. "The people you will not hurt, if I come home."

For a moment, the old man was quite still.

"I never said I would hurt them," he said. "I would not. I have never, in my life, hurt anyone."

"No."

"I have only *arranged* things."

"Yes," said Ren. "That is why I will not come."

The doctor looked at him for a long moment, and in that look there was no anger, no disappointment, nothing but a vast and gentle curiosity, as of a man looking at a clock that has begun, unexpectedly, to strike the wrong hour.

"Everyone is part of this," he said softly. "You taught me that."

"I did not."

"You were nine years old and it was Tuesday." He smiled. "I wrote it in the margin of my notebook. I have never forgotten it."

He stood, with the care of an old man rising from cold stone. He took his cane. He tucked the thermos under his arm and crossed to the door, and at the threshold he turned, and took from the pocket of his waistcoat a small, glossy photograph, and laid it on the lowest step of the telescope.

"For you," he said. "Something to remember me by."

He went out. His footsteps went down the stairs, slowly, one at a time, with the soft tap of the cane. Ren counted them. He counted all two hundred and nine.

When the last echo had faded, he rose and went to the step and picked up the photograph.

It showed two children on a flight of stone stairs, in the thin light of a winter morning. They stood side by side. The girl was seven, perhaps, small, with a round face and hair in two short braids, and she was laughing, with her head thrown back, in a way that Ren Aizawa had seen once, at a crossing, on a hill road, in April.

The boy beside her was a little older. He had no face.

It had been scratched away, very carefully, with the point of a pin, leaving a small, pale, perfect oval in the dark of the print.

On the back, in a gentle, unhurried hand, was written a single line.

*Nobody kept yours for long.*



# Chapter 27: Three Notebooks

Mr. Wataru held the meeting in the cellar of the old gymnasium, at midnight on Friday, because it was the only place in the school that did not have a camera, a window, or a vent.

It was a low brick room that smelled of rubber mats and floor wax. A single bulb hung from a cord. A stack of folding chairs stood against one wall, and someone had set three of them in a circle on the cold concrete floor, with a battered thermos in the middle.

Himeno Saya arrived first. She stood at the foot of the stairs with her arms folded, looking at the chairs as if they might bite.

"You said it was important."

"It is. Please sit." Wataru was already there, perched on the edge of one chair, with his rumpled hair and his bare feet in an old pair of sneakers. "Kuze-kun will be a moment. He insists on taking a different route every time."

"That's very sensible."

"It is. It's also very irritating."

A faint tap came from the stairs, and Kuze Minato appeared with his notebook under his arm. He nodded to both of them, sat on the third chair, and opened his notebook to a clean page.

"We should establish what each of us knows," he said.

"Yes." Wataru poured tea from the thermos into three paper cups. "I will begin, because I have been lying to you for the longest."

"You haven't been lying," said Himeno.

"I have been *selectively informative,* which is the same, only more flattering." He handed out the cups. "Two weeks ago, the dead man in the cardigan was identified as Mikado Soichi, a translator. The sister reported him missing in April. Before he died, he was paid to be a voice. I do not know by whom."

"I do," said Kuze.

Wataru looked at him.

"On the night of the meeting," said Kuze, "I followed Aizawa to the north tower. I did not go in. I waited at the foot of the stairs. At 12:41, a man came down. White hair. A cane. A tweed waistcoat." He looked at his notebook. "He walked past me in the dark. He did not see me. But he was humming."

"Humming what?"

"I do not know. It was not a melody. It was a rhythm." Kuze lifted his eyes. "Two hundred and nine beats. One for every stair."

The cellar was very quiet.

"That's the third player," said Himeno, in a small voice.

"Yes."

"He's old."

"He is *very* old," said Wataru. He had stopped smiling. "I did not say it aloud because I did not want to be right. But I will say it now. I think there is a man behind Ø. I think he built the puppet that I have been talking to for two years. And I think that Aizawa Ren is not his enemy. I think he is something much worse."

"What?"

"His *child.*"

---

They talked until two in the morning.

It was a strange conversation, halting, careful, full of long silences. Wataru told them what he knew about the funding of the Task Force: an anonymous grant, administered by a foundation with a very short name and a very long board. Kuze told them about the stairs, about the number, about the way Ren had paused for a full second when the woman in the gray coat had asked him whether he still counted. Himeno told them about Mio.

"She's his," she said. "I mean, she's part of it. Part of the program. I don't think she knows what it is. I think they just told her to be good, and she was, and now they're using it."

"We do not know that," said Kuze.

"I know it in here." She pressed a hand to her ribs. "I've seen her face when the woman comes. It isn't fear. It's *obedience.* It's the face of someone who is trying to remember what she's been told to forget."

Wataru did not interrupt. He sat with his tea untouched and his shoulders hunched, listening, with the patience of a man who had spent years listening to a machine and had finally, belatedly, begun to enjoy the company of people.

"We need a name for ourselves," said Himeno suddenly.

"Why?"

"Because we're going to be doing this for a while."

"We already have one," said Kuze. "Three notebooks."

"That is not a name. That is an inventory."

"It is the most accurate thing I could think of."

Wataru laughed. It was a surprising sound, short and breathy and genuinely delighted, and it seemed to startle him as much as the others. He put a hand over his mouth.

"I'm sorry. It's only that Ø used to say the same sort of thing. He said that a good team is just a list of what each person is afraid of." He lowered his hand. "I don't suppose that matters now."

"It matters," said Himeno.

"Does it?"

"If he was only a puppet, he was a very well-written one." She hesitated. "Somebody taught him what to say. Somebody who understood what you needed to hear."

Wataru stared at her.

"That is," he said slowly, "the first sensible thing anyone has said about Ø in two weeks."

"What is?"

"That the man at the morgue was only half of a person. And that the other half is still out there." He rose, and went to the stack of folding chairs, and stood with his back to them. "Somebody wrote the lines. Somebody understood me *well enough to say what I needed to hear.*"

He turned.

"Do you know what that means?"

"That the doctor knew you," said Kuze.

"That the doctor *chose* me," said Wataru. "Out of the whole police force. A very small man, with a very low profile, who is easily forgotten, who will do exactly as he is told by a voice he trusts." He smiled, crookedly. "I was *hired,* my friends. I was not recruited for my talents. I was recruited for my invisibility."

Nobody spoke.

"It is rather humbling," he said. "And rather useful. Because there is one thing that a man who has designed everyone else's role will never think to plan for."

"What?"

"That the invisible one might start paying attention."

He picked up his cup. He lifted it a little, toward the two students, a small, crooked toast in the light of the bare bulb.

"To three notebooks," said Wataru.

After a moment, Himeno raised her cup. Kuze did not speak, but he lifted his pen, which was perhaps the same.

They drank.

The thermos was empty. The bulb swung a little on its cord. And somewhere above them, on the third floor of the south dormitory, a light went out in a room at the end of a corridor, and a boy lay down in the dark, with his eyes open, and began to count the ceiling tiles.



# Chapter 28: The Reporter

The café was called the Blue Hour, and it was the only place in the town of Aster where a first-year student could sit for three hours on a single cup of tea without anyone asking him to leave.

Kuze Minato had chosen it for that reason. It was a narrow, low-ceilinged room with a dozen wooden tables, a window that looked onto a rainy street, and a waitress who had learned long ago to leave quiet customers alone. He sat at the back, with his notebook closed and a cup of barley tea cooling by his elbow, and waited.

She arrived at 3:07.

She was perhaps thirty-five, thin, with a wool beret pulled down over short dark hair and a canvas bag slung across her chest. Her coat was soaked. She shook the rain from it at the door, scanned the room with sharp, tired eyes, and made her way straight to his table.

"Kuze Minato."

"Yes."

"Hoshina Aya." She sat. "You emailed me."

"I did."

"You said you had a question about Lantern House."

"I have several."

She laid her bag on the floor and looked at him for a long moment. "You're younger than I expected."

"People say that."

"What do you want to know?"

"Everything," said Kuze.

She laughed, a short, humorless sound. "That's what I said, six years ago."

---

She had been a junior reporter at a regional paper when the fire broke out. It was a small story: a charitable home on the coast, a basement blaze at three in the morning, no casualties. The staff had been evacuated. The children had been moved to nearby facilities the same night. The head of the home, a physician named Dr. Emil Kreutz, had given a short statement and then gone, she was told, into retirement.

"It was too tidy," said Hoshina. "Forty children. A hundred-year-old building. A basement fire, and not one injury." She took a cup from the waitress without looking at it. "I asked the fire department for the report. They said it was classified. I asked the charity commission for the records. They said they had been transferred. I asked the children's services office where the forty children had gone."

"And?"

"They said it was *confidential.*" She said the word with a flat, bitter relish. "Forty children. Confidential."

"And you stopped."

"No. My editor stopped me." She turned the cup in her hands. "He called me in and told me that the story was sensitive. He said a very senior person had called the owner of the paper. He said he was sorry. He said it in the voice you use when you tell a child that the dog has gone to live on a farm."

Kuze listened without moving.

"I kept going on my own," said Hoshina. "Nights, weekends. I've been doing it for six years. I've got a storage unit full of paper. And I'll tell you what I've found."

She reached into her bag and drew out a thin, battered folder. She did not open it. She laid it on the table between them with the care of someone setting down a live thing.

"Lantern House wasn't an orphanage," she said. "Not really. It was a *program.* It took in children who tested above a certain threshold, children with extraordinary memory, or pattern recognition, or a certain kind of stillness, and it kept them for a decade. Not the way a school keeps them. The way a lab does."

"For what purpose?"

"I don't know. But I know that every year, for twelve years, a small number of children were *graduated.* Quietly. Placed in schools, ministries, trading firms, wherever. They were called the Quiet Ones." Her voice had dropped. "There were twelve of them. I know ten of the names."

She opened the folder.

Inside was a single sheet, photocopied so many times that its edges had blurred. A list, typed, in a column. Twelve lines, numbered, each with a name, a date, a destination.

Ten had been circled in red.

*1. Tsukishiro, Aoi. 1989. Placed: Aster Institute (administration).*

Kuze did not breathe.

*2. Honjo, Takeshi. 1990. Placed: Ministry of Finance.*

*3. Arai, Sayuri. 1991. Placed: Nomura Securities.*

He read all the way down, past the names, past the cold little dates. Ten lines circled, two not. The eleventh, uncircled, said only *Withheld.*

The twelfth was the shortest of all. It said:

*12. R. Placed: Aster Institute. April.*

There was no year.

He did not move. He did not blink. In the rain-streaked window the gray street glided past, a bus, a bicycle, a woman with an umbrella, and behind them all the faint, steady hum of the Blue Hour's refrigerator.

"Number one," said Hoshina, quietly, "is the headmistress of the school you attend."

"Yes."

"I don't know whether she knows that I know. But I thought you ought to."

"Thank you."

"The eleventh is a mystery. And the twelfth..." She shook her head. "I only learned of him last week. A woman at the charity commission, a clerk who retired this spring, found a note in a drawer. It had *R.* on it, and *Aster,* and *April.* She called me."

"Do you know what the R stands for?"

"No."

"Do you know why it was kept off the list?"

"I think," said Hoshina, "that the doctor did not finish him until this year."

Kuze closed the folder.

"Ms. Hoshina. If I were to tell you that there is a boy at my school who counts the stairs, who never raises his voice, who has the most beautiful manners I have ever seen, and who may have killed three people to protect a secret he has not even been asked to keep..." He looked up. "What would you say?"

She was silent for a long time. Outside, the rain was easing. The light through the window had turned the color of weak tea.

"I would say that I am too old and too tired to be surprised," said Hoshina Aya. "And I would say that I would very much like to meet him."

"No."

"No?"

"I do not think you would survive it," said Kuze.

She looked at him sharply. Then, slowly, she nodded.

"Fair enough," she said. "Then tell me what you need."

He thought about it. He thought about the cellar, and the bulb, and the thin, crooked toast.

"A way in," he said. "Past the headmistress."

"I can try."

"And a name for the eleventh."

"That one," said Hoshina, "I'll have to dig for." She rose, shouldered her bag, and held out a small, chapped hand. "But I'll find it. I've been looking for six years. I can wait one more week."

He took her hand. It was warm, and dry, and surprisingly strong.

"Thank you," said Kuze.

"Don't thank me yet," said Hoshina. "I haven't done anything but tell you the world is worse than you thought."

She left. He sat for a while longer, alone, with the folder closed before him and the cold tea at his elbow.

Then he opened his notebook and wrote one line, in tiny, left-slanting letters, at the top of a fresh page.

*The headmistress was one of them.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then, below it, he wrote the other thing he had known since the moment he read the list.

*So was Mio.*



# Chapter 29: Number One

Aoi Tsukishiro did not take visitors after six.

It was a rule she had kept for twenty years, a small, fixed, private thing, like the vase of white blossom on her desk or the way she folded her gloves. At six, the lamp on her desk was lit. At 6:05, the door was locked. At 6:10, she poured one cup of green tea and sat with it in silence until seven.

On Wednesday at 6:12, someone knocked.

She did not look up. "I am not available."

"Forgive me, Headmistress." The voice was low, tired, apologetic. "It's Wataru. From the Task Force. I only need one minute."

"The Task Force has been dismissed."

"Yes. It has." A pause. "I have stayed behind."

She set down her cup. She sat very still. Then, slowly, with a small dry click, she turned the key on her desk and called, "Come in."

He entered the room the way he did everything, hunched, rumpled, vaguely apologetic. He wore a wrinkled shirt and an old pair of sneakers. His hair stood out in all directions. He did not bow. He stood in front of the desk, with his hands in his pockets, and looked at her with a face that was suddenly, strangely, not rumpled at all.

"I won't waste your time," he said. "You were at Lantern House."

She said nothing.

"You were the first. Number one. 1989."

The lamp hummed on her desk. In the window behind her, the glass reflected two figures, one very upright, one very bent.

"Who told you that?" asked Aoi Tsukishiro.

"A reporter."

"A reporter." She breathed out, a small, flat sound. "Six years, and she is still alive. I did not think she would be."

"You *know* her?"

"I know of her." She folded her hands. "Sit down, Mr. Wataru."

He sat. She did not offer tea. She looked at him with the same stern, level, unhurried look that had frightened two decades of students, and then, without warning, she looked away. It was a small movement, a lowering of the eyes, and it made her suddenly look old.

"I was four years old when I came to him," she said. "I do not remember my mother. I remember a long corridor and a smell of lavender and a man who bent down and asked me what number came after nine. I said ten. He asked what number came after ninety-nine. I said a hundred. He asked what number came after the last number." Her mouth twisted. "I said there wasn't one. He smiled as if I had given him a present."

"And you stayed."

"I stayed for fourteen years. He taught me everything. How to read faces. How to see the weight of a sentence. How to be, as he said, *unobserved.*" She opened her hands. "I was his first. His proof. He told me I would be a headmistress, and that one day he would send me a child that I would know, and that when I saw that child I was to do nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing. Only to watch." She looked up. Her eyes were very dark. "I have watched him for four months, Mr. Wataru. I have watched three students die. I watched that boy stand at the vote and take all of them into his hands and *lift.* I have been the headmistress of this school for twenty years and I have never seen anything so beautiful. And I have never been so afraid."

"You could have stopped him."

"I could not."

"Why?"

"Because I was told not to." She said it plainly, without apology. "Because I was *built* that way. I am very, very good at obeying a man I have never seen. It is the one thing I have ever been trained to do."

There was a long silence.

"I want to show you something," said Aoi Tsukishiro.

She rose. She went to the bookcase at the side of the room, a tall piece of dark oak with glass doors, and from the lowest shelf drew out a thick, leather-bound volume. She opened it. Behind the book was a small steel panel, set flush into the wall, with a keyhole.

She took a key from around her neck. It hung on a thin chain beneath her blouse.

"The foundation papers of this school are sealed," she said. "That is true. I have never read them. But I have a box. It has been in this wall for twenty years. It was given to me by the doctor on the day I took this post, and I was told that it was for the day a boy came who counted the stairs."

"And he's come."

"He has." She turned the key. The panel swung open. Inside was a plain wooden box, no larger than a shoe box, bound with a strip of old red ribbon. "I have waited for twenty years. I thought, perhaps, that I would be relieved."

"Are you?"

"No." She lifted the box with both hands. "I find that I am only tired."

She carried it across the room and set it on the desk in front of him, between the vase of white blossom and the cooling cup of tea.

"I am giving it to you," she said, "because you are the only person in this building who has been paying attention to the right things. And because I think that he intended me to."

"The doctor?"

"Or the boy." She smiled, very faintly. "I have never been able to tell the difference."

Wataru looked at the box. It was small and humble and tied with a ribbon that had faded from red to the color of dried blood.

"What is in it?" he asked.

"I have never opened it."

"Never?"

"I was told not to."

"And you obeyed."

"I always do." She sat down again, and picked up her cup, and found that the tea had gone cold. She did not seem to notice. "Take it, Mr. Wataru. Go. Do not tell me what you find. And if you see him before I do, tell him that I am sorry."

"For what?"

"For letting him be alone."

He rose slowly. He lifted the box in both hands. It was lighter than he expected. Something inside shifted with a faint dry whisper, like paper.

At the door, he stopped.

"Headmistress."

"Yes."

"The name of the school. Aster. Do you know why he chose it?"

"It is the flower," she said, "that the doctor kept on his desk. A purple one. He said it was the only flower he had ever planted that had come up on its own." She did not turn. "He said that the child it was named for did not like to be noticed."

He stood there for a moment, quite still, with the box in his hands.

"Thank you," he said.

"Close the door when you go," said Aoi Tsukishiro.

He did.

On the other side of it, in the corridor, he leaned back against the wall and breathed out, long and slow. Then he looked down at the ribbon on the box and, very gently, untied it.



# Chapter 30: The Box

At 6:19 that evening, a student in a tidy uniform was standing in the corridor outside the headmistress's office, reading the notice board.

It was an ordinary notice board. It held a fire evacuation map, a list of club meetings, a flyer for a blood drive, a yellowed memo about the proper disposal of laboratory glass. He read all of it with perfect attention, from the top left corner to the bottom right, in the manner of a boy with nowhere else to be.

He did not look at the door when it opened.

He did not look at the thin, rumpled man who came out of it carrying a small wooden box in both hands, or at the headmistress's voice behind him saying *close the door when you go.* He did not look when the man paused, and glanced down the empty corridor, and walked away rather faster than he had come. He read the memo about laboratory glass twice.

When the footsteps had faded, he took a plain black notebook from his pocket and wrote a single line in it, in a small, even hand.

Then he closed the notebook, and put it away, and set off along the corridor at an unhurried pace, in the same direction, at the same distance, exactly as far behind as a person could be and still be said, by any reasonable observer, to be going somewhere else.

On the second-floor landing, he stopped to tie his shoe. On the garden path, he stopped to admire a rosebush. At the edge of the lawn, with the man in the rumpled shirt a hundred meters ahead and the gymnasium roof black against the evening sky, he paused for a full minute and looked at the sea.

There was only one place in the school where a man carrying a box might go to open it unobserved: a cellar with no windows, no vent, no camera. It was the only room Ren Aizawa had never been able to see into.

It was, by any measure, a very good choice.

He waited eleven minutes. Then he followed.

---

It was not a long walk to the cellar of the old gymnasium, but Wataru took twenty minutes to do it.

He went the long way, through the gardens, along the cliff path, around the back of the boiler house. He stopped twice to look over his shoulder. He crossed the lawn at an angle, in the shadow of the trees, with the box held tight against his ribs under his coat like something stolen. At the cellar door, he checked the lock, the hinges, the cobwebs in the frame, and only then did he go down.

They were waiting.

Himeno sat on one of the folding chairs, her notes open on her lap. Kuze stood by the wall with his back to the brick, his notebook held to his chest. A single bulb swung gently on its cord.

"Well?" said Himeno.

Wataru did not answer. He walked to the center of the room and knelt, and set the box on the cold concrete, and stayed there on his knees for a long moment with his hands flat on the lid.

"I haven't opened it," he said.

"Why not?"

"I wanted you here. I think I wanted witnesses." He smiled, a little crookedly. "It's a bad habit. I like to be watched when I do something stupid."

"It may not be stupid," said Kuze.

"It's a box that the doctor left for the boy. I think we should assume that anything inside it is meant to be found." Wataru took a long breath. "I think we should assume that we are doing exactly what we have been invited to do."

"Then why open it?"

"Because the alternative is to never know what he wanted us to see."

He untied the ribbon. It came away in his fingers like dried petals. He lifted the lid.

Inside, on a layer of faded blue tissue, lay a stack of paper.

It was old, soft, gone amber at the edges. There were perhaps forty sheets, tied loosely with a length of black thread. Wataru lifted the top page and held it under the bulb.

It was a child's drawing.

It showed a man. He was drawn in pencil, a tall figure in a long coat, with a round pair of glasses and a mug in his hand. He stood on the top of a staircase with his arms outstretched, and from his mouth came a small, squiggly balloon of text, printed in painstaking capital letters.

*GOOD EVENING. I AM Ø. I WILL FIND YOU.*

Wataru did not move.

"What is it?" whispered Himeno.

"A child's drawing," said Himeno.

"Yes."

"Of Ø."

"Of someone who looks very much like him." Wataru's voice was quite flat. He turned the page. There was another, and another. Forty sheets, each a little more elaborate than the last. A man with glasses. A man at a desk. A man with a laptop. A man at a window, looking out at a night full of stars. Beneath each, in wavering capitals, a line of text.

*Ø NEVER MEETS HIS SUSPECTS.*

*Ø ALWAYS ASKS THEIR NAME.*

*Ø SAYS "OF COURSE" WHEN HE UNDERSTANDS.*

Nobody spoke. Himeno pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. Kuze had stopped writing. He stood with his pen above the page and his gray eyes fixed on the drawings, and in the swinging light of the bulb, his face was the color of paper.

"Who drew them?" whispered Himeno.

"I do not know," said Wataru. "I have not yet turned the last one over."

He did not turn it over. He sat there on the cold concrete with the whole stack in his hands, and the bulb creaked on its cord, and from the stairs above there came a faint sound.

A single footfall, soft, unhurried, on the old stone.

The next sounded a moment later. Then the next. They were coming down, one at a time, at an even pace, and each step fell in the rhythm of a boy who had never in his life been in a hurry.

Himeno's hand closed on Kuze's sleeve.

Wataru did not move. He crouched over the open box with the stack of drawings in his hands and his head slowly lifting. He did not seem frightened. He seemed, if anything, relieved.

The footsteps stopped at the bottom of the stairs.

"Good evening," said a soft voice.

It was perfectly courteous. It was perfectly calm.

"I believe," said Ren Aizawa, "that you have something of mine."
