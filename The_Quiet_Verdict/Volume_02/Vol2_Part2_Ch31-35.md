# Chapter 31: Fifteen Steps

Nobody in the cellar moved.

It was a strange tableau, the kind that stays in the mind long after the details have faded. The bare bulb swung on its cord, throwing a slow pendulum of light across the brick walls. Wataru crouched on the concrete with a stack of drawings in his hands. Himeno stood behind him, one hand still gripping Kuze's sleeve. And at the foot of the stairs, with his plain black bag over one shoulder and his hands loose at his sides, stood a first-year student in a perfectly pressed uniform, wearing a small, polite, unchanging half-smile.

"Good evening," said Ren Aizawa again, since no one had answered.

"Good evening," said Wataru.

"I'm sorry to interrupt. I know it's late."

"You aren't sorry."

"I'm sorry that you will think I am being rude." He considered. "It is the closest I can come."

Himeno made a small, choked sound. Kuze said nothing. He had lifted his notebook from his chest and held it open in front of him like a shield, with his pen poised above a clean page.

"How did you find us?" asked Wataru.

"I followed you."

"I looked behind me twice."

"Four times," said Ren. "You looked twice on the garden path, once at the boiler house, and once at the lawn. I stopped to tie my shoe." He glanced down at the stairs. "I counted fourteen steps down. Fifteen, if one includes the half step at the bottom."

Kuze's pen stopped.

"There are fourteen," he said, in a very small voice.

"There is a half step. It's worn. It's easy to miss." Ren turned his head toward him, politely. "I'm sorry, Kuze-kun. I didn't mean to correct you."

"It is I who should apologize," said Kuze. "I do not usually miss things."

"No. You don't."

The silence stretched. In it, Wataru slowly set the drawings down in their box, one at a time, face up, and rose to his feet. He did it with care, brushing the dust from his knees, and when he was standing he was a head shorter than the boy at the foot of the stairs and a good deal less calm.

"You said I have something of yours."

"Yes."

"I don't think I do. I think I have something that was left for you."

"That is the same thing."

"Is it?" Wataru tilted his head. "The headmistress told me it was for a boy who counted the stairs. She didn't say which one. There are at least three of you in this room."

"I'm not going to take it from you," said Ren.

Wataru blinked.

"I said it was mine. I did not say that I wanted it back." The half-smile did not change. "You will read it whether I allow it or not. I would rather you could say you did so freely."

"That's a very odd thing to be concerned about."

"I'm told I'm odd."

---

It was Himeno who broke.

She stepped out from behind Wataru, shoulders squared, her long hair swinging, her face white as chalk. She stopped three paces from him. She was shaking, and she did not try to hide it.

"Did you kill Tachibana Gen?"

"I was in the library."

"That isn't an answer."

"It is the only one I can prove."

"Did you kill Ogami? Aoyagi Hina? The man in the tower?"

"Himeno-san." His voice was very gentle. "May I ask you something?"

"No."

"If I said yes, what would you do?"

She stared at him.

"You would go to the Task Force," said Ren. "You would tell them what I said. They would ask for evidence, and you would not have any, and they would ask whether I had said it in front of witnesses. You would tell them about this room. And there is a man here who has spent four months pretending to a laptop that he does not know who I am." He glanced at Wataru. "I do not think he would find that conversation easy."

"Don't talk about him."

"I'm talking about *you.* You asked a question you cannot afford to have answered. I am being kind."

"You are not being kind!"

"No," said Ren. "I am being accurate. It is a very close cousin."

Himeno took a step toward him. For a moment it looked as though she might hit him. Kuze moved, and Wataru lifted one hand, and the three of them stood very still in the swinging light.

Then, slowly, Himeno's fists unclenched. Her eyes were bright, but her voice, when it came, was level.

"Why are you here?"

"To see whether you would open the box."

"And?"

"You did." Ren looked at the drawings in their tissue. "It was the right decision. I would have made it myself."

"You have *seen* them."

"Once. A long time ago." He paused, and there was something in the pause, something so small that Kuze, who was watching his hands, saw only that the left one tightened by a hair on the strap of the bag. "I was very young."

"You drew them," said Himeno.

"I did not say that."

"You didn't deny it."

"I did not say that either." The half-smile returned. "I notice, Himeno-san, that you are still asking the questions that you wish to be true. It is the one thing that I would advise you to stop."

"You're not going to answer me."

"I am going to give you the best answer I have." He bowed, slightly, with the grave courtesy of a student leaving a teacher's room. "I will not lie to you. In four months I have not told you a single falsehood. I have only declined to speak."

"That's a lie by itself."

"It is the nearest thing to the truth that I am able to offer." He turned to Wataru. "Mr. Wataru. I will say good night. And I would like you to understand that I have no wish to harm you."

"That's a strange thing to say to a man you've framed three people in front of."

"It is true all the same."

"Why?"

"Because you are the only adult in this school whose lies I could count," said Ren. "You have pretended. You have lain on a bench and put a cushion on your face. But you have never once told me a thing you believed to be false. It makes you very easy to read. It also makes you very difficult to replace."

Wataru stared at him.

It was the most extraordinary thing he had ever been told. He heard it, and knew that it was a compliment, and knew that it was a threat, and could not for the life of him tell where one ended and the other began.

"Good night," said Ren.

He turned. He put one hand on the stair rail.

"Aizawa-kun," said Wataru.

The boy paused.

"If I hand that box to the Task Force tomorrow morning."

"Yes?"

"What will you do?"

Ren looked back over his shoulder. The light from the bulb fell on half of his face, and left the other half in shadow, and what could be seen of it was pleasant, courteous, and absolutely still.

"I will do nothing," he said. "It is the Task Force that will have to decide what to do with it."

"And if I burn it?"

"Then I will be sorry. It is the only copy."

"The only..."

"Of what I was," said Ren.

He went up the stairs. He took them one at a time, at an even pace, and they could hear him counting, not aloud, but in the rhythm of the footsteps, in the faint tap of a shoe on worn stone.

Fourteen.

And the half step.

Then there was silence.

---

For a long time, none of the three moved.

At last Wataru knelt again beside the box. His hands, resting on the lid, had begun to shake.

"I think," he said, "that he just told me he did not draw them."

"He said he was very young," said Kuze.

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

"I think..." Wataru swallowed. "I think he told me something else. Something that I do not understand. He said it was the only copy *of what I was.*"

"I heard."

"What does it mean?"

"I do not know," said Kuze. He closed his notebook very slowly. His face was the color of old paper. "But I believe that he is the first person I have ever met who did not lie to us, and I have never in my life been so afraid."

Himeno sat down on a folding chair. She sat quite heavily, as if the cord that held her up had been cut. She put her face in her hands.

"He's not even angry," she whispered. "He just *stood* there. He told us we were right. He told us we were wrong. He told us that he *liked* us."

"Yes."

"I don't know what to do."

"We read the rest," said Wataru. He lifted the stack of drawings from the tissue. His voice had steadied. "All of it. Tonight. Together. And then we decide."

"What if it's a trap?"

"It *is* a trap." He smiled, a thin, crooked, unhappy smile. "Everything in this school is a trap. But I have been in a trap for two years without knowing it, and I would like, just once, to be in one with my eyes open."

He sat down on the cold concrete, with the box between his knees and the swinging bulb above his head, and turned over the last page of the stack.


# Chapter 32: The Good Girl

At 7:40 the next morning, Mio Hanazono went to breakfast as she always did, and sat in the same seat at the long dormitory table, and ate her rice in small, careful bites.

She had not slept. It showed in the faint blue shadows beneath her eyes and in the way her chopsticks trembled each time she lifted them. Around her the cafeteria roared with its usual morning noise. Plates clattered. A first-year was telling a loud story about a physics exam. A girl at the next table laughed, and a boy dropped his tray, and a teacher called out for quiet.

Mio heard none of it.

"Is this seat taken?"

She looked up. Ren Aizawa was standing beside the table with a tray, calm, pressed, immaculate, with his plain black bag over one shoulder.

"No," she said. "Please."

He sat. He arranged his chopsticks with a small, precise click and began to eat. For a minute neither of them spoke. She watched his hands. They were steady and economical, and he used his napkin exactly as often as a man should.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"No."

"Is it the woman?"

Her chopsticks stopped.

"I saw you through the window," said Ren. "At the gate. Three days ago. She gave you a bag."

"It was only pastry."

"Yes." He took a bite of fish. "I believe it was."

"Aizawa-kun..."

"Would you like to tell me?"

She looked down at her tray. Her eyes were suddenly wet. It happened as it always did with her, with no warning, in a small sharp rush, like water rising in a glass.

"She's not just a woman," said Mio. "She's my Matron. She looked after me. For ten years, in the house by the sea."

"Lantern House."

"You know it?"

"I know of it."

"Then you know what it is." She laughed, a thin, cracked sound. "I thought I'd left it. I thought, I've got a new life, a new school. I thought nobody could find me. And then she came, and she said, *Mio-chan, have you been good?* And I said yes. And my whole body just..." She pressed a hand to her chest. "It just *said* yes. Like a bell."

"That is how it was designed."

"Designed?"

"To produce obedience in the absence of choice." He said it with the mild, level air of a man quoting a textbook. "It does not require cruelty. It merely requires consistency. A child who is rewarded for stillness will be still. A child who is rewarded for goodness will be good."

Mio stared at him.

"You make it sound like you've read about it."

"I have lived near it."

It was such a small, plain answer that she almost missed it. She sat quite still, with her hands in her lap, and for a moment the noise of the cafeteria seemed to fall away entirely, leaving only the two of them at the end of the long table.

"Aizawa-kun," she said. "What do they want me to do?"

"I do not know."

"But you know *something.*"

"I know that she asked you a question. And I know that you did not answer it truthfully."

"What question?"

"Whether you were being good."

"I said yes."

"Yes," said Ren. "And I think that is the first lie you have told in your life."

She flinched.

"I'm not criticizing," he said. "I am only noting. It is a very small lie. It may be the beginning of something."

"Of what?"

"A choice."

She did not know what to say. She sat with her lips parted and her eyes wide and wet, looking at him as though he had just handed her a key to a door she had not known was there.

"I'm supposed to report on you," she said.

"I know."

"I'm supposed to tell her everything. Who you talk to. What you do. I've been doing it since April." Her voice shook. "I've told her about the crossing. I told her you saved me. I told her about the roof. She asked me to watch you, and I did, and I told her, and I *liked* it, Aizawa-kun. I liked being useful. I liked having something to say."

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"I have known since the twentieth of May."

She stared at him. "Then why..."

"Why did I let you?"

"Yes."

He paused. It was not a long pause. It was the length of a single drawn breath.

"Because you were kind to me," he said. "And kindness is very difficult to replace."

Mio made a small sound. She put her hand over her mouth. Tears spilled down her cheeks and splashed onto the back of her hand, and she did not wipe them away.

"That's not a reason," she managed.

"No. It is only a fact." He lowered his chopsticks. "You should tell her what you like. You should tell her that I eat in silence, that I study in the library, that I am polite to everyone. It will all be true."

"Won't she know?"

"She will know that you are telling her the truth. It is the most convincing thing in the world."

"I don't understand."

"No," said Ren gently. "But you are trying. That is more than most."

He rose, and picked up his tray, and bowed to her with exactly the correct degree of courtesy.

"Thank you for the company, Hanazono-san."

"Aizawa-kun."

"Yes?"

"Did you really want me to be *good?*"

He stood looking down at her for a moment, quite still, in the cold gray light from the cafeteria windows.

"No," he said. "I wanted you to *choose.* A person who has chosen is far more reliable than a person who has obeyed. It took me a long time to learn it."

He walked away. She watched him go, her heart hammering, her tears cooling on her face, and she did not see the figure at the far end of the room, a small rumpled man with a paper cup in his hand, who had been standing against the wall for the entire conversation and who now turned, very slowly, and went out.

---

In the corridor outside the cafeteria, Wataru leaned against the cold plaster and slid down it until he was sitting on the floor.

Himeno found him there ten minutes later.

"Well?" she said.

"I have been hired for two years by a man who has never raised his voice." He did not look up. "I have just heard him tell a frightened girl, in the gentlest voice I have ever heard, that she had chosen to betray him and was therefore more reliable. And she cried, and thanked him." He laughed, quite quietly. "I do not know whether he meant it as a kindness. I do not think that it matters."

"It makes him *worse.*"

"Yes."

"Because we can't tell."

"Because we *can't,*" said Wataru. "That is the horror. A man who lies can be caught. A man who tells the truth in the way that best serves him cannot."

He got to his feet. He took from his coat pocket a single sheet of yellowed paper, soft with age, folded in four. He held it out.

"I read the last drawings," he said. "Kuze and I stayed up until five. I wanted you to be the one to see this. Because I think you are the one who will understand it."

She unfolded the page.

At the top, in a child's careful capitals, was a title.

*THE PEOPLE I WILL MEET.*

Below it, in a column, were drawings, each no bigger than a postage stamp. A tall girl with a straight spine and long hair, standing with her arms folded. A thin boy with a notebook. A round-faced girl, laughing. A man with a cushion on his face and a cup of tea at his elbow.

Beside each one, a caption.

*THE GIRL WHO IS NEVER WRONG.*

*THE BOY WHO WRITES EVERYTHING DOWN.*

*THE GIRL WHO IS ALWAYS GOOD.*

*THE MAN NOBODY NOTICES. HE EATS TOO MUCH SUGAR.*

Himeno's hands began to tremble.

She turned the sheet over. On the back, in the same pencil, in a hand that had grown steadier with age, was a single line.

*I do not know yet who will draw the last one.*

And below it, in tiny letters, very small, like a thing written for no one, a date.

It was seven years ago.


# Chapter 33: Signed, R.

They met again at midnight, in the same cellar, under the same bulb.

None of them had slept. None of them had spoken of it. All day Himeno had sat in class with her pen frozen above the page, and Kuze had written nothing, and Wataru had lain on the old bench in the staff room with the cushion over his face and the drawings in his lap, and not once had any of them looked at a certain boy in the second row, who had taken notes, solved a problem on the board, and bowed to the teacher at the end of the period.

Now they sat in a circle of folding chairs with the whole stack laid out between them on the concrete.

There were forty sheets. They had been counted and numbered and spread in rows, each one under a stone from the cellar floor to keep it from stirring in the draft. Wataru had arranged them in order of the dates on the backs. It had taken three hours. The first was seven years old and the last was five.

"I think I should explain what we have," he said.

He did not sound like himself. His voice was low and steady, with none of the lazy, cushion-muffled humor that had characterized it for months. It was the voice of a man reading a long list of the dead.

"These are the drawings of a child. They begin when he was nine years old. They end when he was eleven. They are all in the same hand, in the same pencil, signed with a single letter."

"R," said Kuze.

"R. They tell one story." He touched the first sheet. "The story of a detective called Ø."

"We have seen."

"No. You have seen the beginning." He moved his finger down the row. "Look at the next. Look at what the child draws when he is ten. He does not draw Ø anymore. He draws what Ø will *do.*"

Himeno bent forward.

The drawing showed a room. It was a small room, quite plain, with a long table and a bench and a wall hung with pictures. At the table sat nine small stick figures. At the head of the table, on a laptop, in the center of a white sticker, was a single character.

Ø.

Beneath it, in capitals, the caption read:

*THE TASK FORCE. THEY WILL BE SENT TO HUNT ME. THEY WILL OBEY Ø, WHO IS ME.*

She stopped breathing.

"Read the next," said Wataru.

She did.

*THE LIAISON. HE WILL BE THE ONLY ONE WHO NEVER MEETS Ø.*

*HE WILL BE SMALL AND UNTIDY AND NOBODY WILL NOTICE HIM. HE WILL LIE ON A BENCH WITH A CUSHION ON HIS FACE.*

*I WILL KNOW HE HAS TROUBLE SLEEPING BECAUSE HE EATS FIVE SUGARS IN HIS TEA.*

Her fingers went numb.

"No," she said.

"Read the next."

*THE DEAD MAN IN THE CARDIGAN. HE WILL BE KIND. HE WILL BE A VOICE. HE WILL NEVER KNOW WHAT HE IS FOR.*

*THE BALCONY. THE BALCONY IS WHERE Ø WILL DIE.*

"Stop," said Kuze, very softly.

Nobody stopped.

*THE GIRL WHO IS NEVER WRONG WILL FIND ME FIRST. SHE WILL BE ALONE. SHE WILL BE AFRAID. SHE WILL NOT TELL ANYONE.*

*THE BOY WHO WRITES EVERYTHING DOWN WILL FIND THE OTHERS. HE WILL BE THE ONLY ONE WHO TELLS THE TRUTH.*

*THE GIRL WHO IS ALWAYS GOOD WILL BE SENT TO WATCH ME. SHE WILL BE KIND. I WILL LET HER.*

*THE OLD MAN WILL COME LAST.*

Himeno lifted her eyes. They were enormous and dry and horribly bright.

"He drew us," she said.

"Yes."

"Seven years ago. He drew *us.* He drew every one of us. He knew that I would be afraid. He knew Kuze would be the one who wrote it down. He knew..." Her voice cracked. "He knew you would eat the sugar."

"No." Wataru's voice was very quiet. "He did not *know.* He *drew.* And then somebody found the drawings, and made them real."

"Who?"

"The doctor." He lifted the last page. "He built a school. He built a Task Force. He hired a translator and a liaison and a headmistress. He placed a girl in a classroom, and a boy at a window, and a student at the head of the roll who would be the best in his year. He *cast* us. He found the people who were most like the pictures. And then he let them walk into the story."

"That's insane," said Himeno.

"Yes."

"That's not possible."

"It is perfectly possible. It requires only money, and time, and a patient man who does not mind watching." Wataru's mouth twitched. "I was a police officer for nine years. I was a good one. I was passed over for promotion three times, and each time I was told that I did not have *presence.* The liaison position came with a salary that was twice my own. They said I had been recommended." He laughed. "Recommended. By a man I have never met. For a job that was designed, down to the sugar, in the margins of a child's notebook."

He put a hand over his eyes.

"I thought I was investigating a murderer. I was a *character.*"

For a long moment, there was no sound. The bulb ticked on its cord. Far above, the wind pushed at the old gymnasium roof.

At last Kuze spoke. His voice was thin and strained, but it was steady.

"There is one more page," he said.

"Yes."

"The last. The one that you have not read to us."

Wataru lowered his hand. He looked at the stack on the floor. At the bottom, face down, lay a single sheet, a little larger than the rest, folded once. He drew it out slowly.

He unfolded it.

It was not a drawing. It was a letter, written in the same careful pencil, in the same upright capitals, on a sheet of lined paper torn from a school notebook.

*DEAR DOCTOR.*

*YOU ASKED ME TO WRITE DOWN HOW THE STORY ENDS. I HAVE WRITTEN IT.*

*THE DETECTIVE IS NOT REAL. HE IS ME. I KNOW EVERYTHING HE WILL DO BECAUSE I MADE HIM. THAT IS WHY HE CAN NEVER CATCH ME.*

*EVERYBODY IN THE STORY WILL BE KIND. THEY WILL ALL TRY TO HELP ME. THAT IS HOW I WILL KNOW WHO TO TRUST: THE ONES WHO ARE KINDEST ARE THE ONES I MUST WATCH.*

*I WILL KILL THE DETECTIVE FIRST, BECAUSE HE IS THE ONLY ONE WHO COULD EVER UNDERSTAND ME.*

*THEN I WILL BE ALONE.*

*THAT IS THE ENDING.*

*DO YOU LIKE IT?*

*— R.*

*P.S. THE DOCTOR SAID I MUST NEVER TELL ANYONE MY REAL NAME UNTIL THE VERY END OF THE STORY. I THINK THAT IS A GOOD IDEA. IT WILL MAKE EVERYONE WONDER.*

There was a long, cold, absolute silence.

Himeno was the first to move. She got up from her chair. She walked to the wall and stood with her forehead against the brick, and her shoulders rose and fell, and she did not make a sound.

Kuze sat motionless with his notebook shut on his knee. He had lost every drop of color. His lips moved, once, as if he were counting, and then he stopped.

And Wataru, at the center of the circle, folded the letter along its original crease, very gently, and held it in both hands like a thing that might break.

"The detective is not real," he said. "He is the child. He invented every rule that Ø ever followed. The questions. The face. The name. The *tricks.* He gave them all to a drawing, and the drawing grew up, and we spent four months chasing it."

"That is why he is never surprised," whispered Kuze.

"Of course he is never surprised. He wrote the rules." Wataru smiled, terribly. "He has been playing a game against an opponent whose moves he himself decided, when he was nine, in a house by the sea. And he has been winning it for four months, because there is no other way for the game to go."

"Then Ø..."

"Ø was the loneliest thing in the world." He lowered his head. "And the boy killed him anyway. Because he had *promised.* It was written in the letter. *I will kill the detective first.* He was a man of his word."

Himeno turned from the wall. Her face was streaked with tears.

"What do we do?"

"I do not know."

"We can't beat him."

"No."

"We can't beat a person who has already *read the script.*"

"No," said Wataru. "Not by playing the part we were drawn for."

He stood. He stood slowly, like a man much older than his years, and he laid the letter on top of the stack, and he looked around the cellar, at the two white faces, at the dripping brick, at the single bulb.

"But I would like to point out one thing," he said.

"What?"

"The child said that he did not know who would draw the last one."

"The last what?"

"The last character." He lifted the sheet he had shown Himeno that morning, the one headed *THE PEOPLE I WILL MEET,* and turned it so that they could see the bottom. Beneath the stick figures and the neat little captions was a blank space. A rectangle, drawn in pencil, with a small question mark in the center.

"A face he did not know," said Wataru. "A person he had not placed. A character the doctor had not yet cast."

"Who?"

"I think," said Wataru, "that it is whoever opens the box."

He looked at them each in turn, and for the first time in four months, he did not seem rumpled, or tired, or lost.

"It is the only part in this story that nobody has written," he said. "And it is *ours.*"

---

Above them, on the cellar stairs, in the dark, a pale boy in a pressed uniform sat with his back against the wall and his plain black notebook open on his knee.

He had been there for forty minutes. He had not written a word.

On the page before him there was only a single drawing. It was a small one, done in pencil, in the lower right corner. It had been done with great care. It showed a rectangle with a question mark in the center, exactly the same size as the one in the cellar below.

He looked at it for a very long time.

Then he took out a pen, and wrote beneath it, in a small, neat, even hand, a line he had never written in his life.

*I did not draw this one.*


# Chapter 34: The Last Character

In the morning, the school woke to a rumor.

It spread the way rumors do, from a whisper in the stairwell to a murmur in the cafeteria to a full-throated roar by the end of first period. By noon, every student at Aster Institute knew that the Task Force had not, in fact, been dismissed. That a man in a rumpled shirt had been seen on the roof of the north tower. That someone had been in the headmistress's office after dark.

Nobody knew what it meant. A boy in Class 1-B insisted that Ø was alive and living in the walls. A girl in 1-A said she had seen a ghost on the cliff path wearing a gray cardigan. Mr. Kurata, passing a crowd of whispering students in the corridor, said sharply that if they had time to gossip they had time to review their conjugations, and nobody laughed.

Class 1-C, at the end of the morning, went to the cafeteria in a single quiet column.

In the second row, Ren Aizawa took his usual seat. He opened his book. He read the opening paragraph of a chapter on organic chemistry, a page he had already read, and underlined a single line.

"Aizawa-kun."

He looked up.

Sakaki Rio was standing at the end of the table, tray in hand, with the stricken, hopeful look of a boy who has been trying to say something for days.

"May I sit?"

"Of course."

He sat. For a long moment he only moved his food from one side of the plate to the other.

"There was a message last night," he said at last. "At eight. On the channel."

"Was there?"

"It said..." He swallowed. "It said that my services were no longer required. That I had done well. That I should return the notebook to the student from whom I received it."

"I see."

"I think it means you." Sakaki's eyes were bright. "I think it means *you* gave me the notebook. In May. And that you're the one who..." He shook his head. "I don't know what I think. I only know that when I got the message I felt as if someone had pulled a rug from under me."

"That's a very natural reaction."

"It's not that I was sorry to stop. It's that I was sorry to stop being *useful.*" He laughed shakily. "Isn't that stupid?"

"No."

"I think it's the nicest I've ever felt about myself. For five weeks. And I didn't even know who I was doing it for."

Ren looked at him. It was a long look, steady and mild, and in it Sakaki felt, as he always did, the odd, warm, melting sense of being seen very clearly by someone who did not mind.

"Sakaki-kun," said Ren.

"Yes?"

"You were good at it. You were meticulous, and honest, and brave. You were the only person in this school who kept a record of every single thing that crossed your desk." He paused. "I would like you to know that I noticed."

Sakaki's face crumpled. He did not cry, but he wanted to. He ducked his head, and bowed, and mumbled something unintelligible, and fled the table with his tray still in his hands.

Ren watched him go. Then he looked down at his textbook, and after a moment, with a small, deliberate movement, turned the page.

At the next table, behind a book on comparative economics, Kuze Minato had been listening to every word.

---

At four o'clock that afternoon, Wataru called a meeting in the old conference room.

It was a very different room now. The wall of photographs was gone, but in its place a new map had been taped to the plaster. A sheet of butcher paper, hand-drawn, eight feet across, covered in lines and names and arrows. At the center, in red marker, was a single word.

*R.*

Radiating from it were a dozen branches. *Lantern House. The doctor. The matron. Mio. The headmistress. Mikado. The Task Force. The drawings.* Each ended in a smaller question.

"I have been working on a theory," said Wataru.

He stood in front of the map in his rumpled shirt, with a cup of tea in one hand and a marker in the other. Himeno and Kuze sat side by side on the old bench. Between them, quite visibly, lay the box.

"The child drew a story. The doctor built the story. The story has a structure. At its center is the boy, who knows every move before it is made, because he made it." He drew a circle. "He is the author. We are the characters. But a character in a story has one power that an author does not."

"Which is?"

"We can surprise him."

"How?"

"By doing something that was not written." Wataru capped the marker. "He drew the girl who is never wrong. He drew the boy who writes everything down. He drew the man nobody notices. He drew the girl who is always good. He drew a world in which people are *what they are.*"

"That is very pessimistic," said Kuze.

"It is wonderfully optimistic. Because I do not think it is true." He looked at them, one by one. "I do not think that Himeno is never wrong. I do not think that you write everything down, Kuze-kun. I think you left out several things last week that you decided I should not know."

Kuze did not blink. "Yes."

"And I do not think that I am nobody."

There was a short silence.

"I think," said Wataru, "that a nine-year-old child, in a house by the sea, in the dark, drew the people who would be kind to him. And that he drew them as simply as he could. He gave each of us a single trait. He made us easy to predict." He smiled. "And then we turned out to be people. Which is the only thing that nobody can write."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that the next move is not his."

"Whose, then?"

"Ours." Wataru took a long breath. "I would like to propose something very foolish."

"Please," said Himeno, with the ghost of a smile.

"I would like to *tell* him."

They stared.

"I would like to walk up to Ren Aizawa, in front of the whole class, and say to him, *I have read your drawings. I know what you are. I know the doctor sent you here to see whether you would kill your friend.* And I would like to tell him that it is not too late."

"Too late for what?"

"To be a person." Wataru's voice cracked, just slightly. "The child wrote that he would be alone. That is the ending. That is the thing he has been working toward, for seven years, with all of his heart. And I would like to say to him, as plainly as I can, *you do not have to.*"

Himeno stared at him. "He'd kill you."

"Perhaps."

"He killed the translator for asking his name."

"He did. And I think he was sorry." Wataru held up a hand. "I know how that sounds. But I watched his face when he spoke to the girl. I heard what he said to her. *I wanted you to be safe.* That is a person who is willing to be seen. Just very, very badly."

Kuze set down his pen. For a long time he said nothing.

"It is," he said at last, "the most dangerous idea I have ever heard."

"Yes."

"I think that you are right."

"Do you?"

"I think it is the only thing that he has not planned for." Kuze rose. He moved to the map, and with the tip of his pen, very carefully, he drew a small box at the bottom of the page. He labeled it. *The last character.* "A character who refuses to be drawn."

Himeno slowly got to her feet.

"I'll do it with you," she said.

"No."

"Yes."

"Miss Himeno, I..."

"I am the girl who is never wrong, Mr. Wataru." She lifted her chin, and her eyes were wet, but she smiled, with a fierce, bright, shaking defiance. "I would like to see what happens when I am."

Wataru looked at the two of them for a long moment. Then, quietly, he laughed.

"Then we will need a plan," he said.

"A plan?" said Kuze.

"A very bad one. One that cannot be predicted by a child who knows all of our flaws." He picked up the box and set it in the middle of the table. "I suggest that we begin with the one thing he has not drawn."

"What?"

"Us," said Wataru, "being *wrong.*"

---

That night, in the dormitory, at 10:40, Ren Aizawa lay on his bed in the dark with his hands folded across his chest.

His eyes were open. He was looking at the ceiling. The clock above his desk ticked in the silence.

The notebook lay on the desk, open to the page with the small drawn rectangle and the single line beneath it. *I did not draw this one.*

He had lain like that for more than an hour.

At 10:41, his phone lit up on the nightstand. He did not reach for it. It glowed in the dark, a small cold square, and then the screen went black.

At 10:42, a faint, gentle knock sounded on his door.

He turned his head.

"Aizawa-kun?" It was a small, familiar voice. "It's Mio. I know it's late. I couldn't sleep."

He sat up. He crossed the room in the dark, with a quiet, even tread, and opened the door.

She stood in the corridor in her pajamas, with her braids undone and her arms wrapped around herself. Her face was blotchy. She had been crying.

"I told her," said Mio. "The Matron. I told her everything. I did what you said. I told her only true things."

"Good."

"And she said..." Her voice cracked. "She said to give you this."

She held out her hand. In her palm lay a small, cold, old brass key, no larger than a thumbnail, on a loop of faded red ribbon.

Ren took it.

He looked at it for exactly eleven seconds.

"Did she say what it opens?" he asked.

"She said you would know." Mio wiped her eyes. "She said, *Tell him it is time to come home.*"

He closed his hand around the key.

"Thank you, Hanazono-san," he said. "You have done well."

"I haven't done anything."

"You have told her only true things. It is the hardest thing in the world."

"What happens now?"

He did not answer. He stood in the doorway with the key in his fist and the light from the corridor falling on his face, and he looked at her with the same mild, courteous attention he had always given her.

"Go to bed," said Ren. "Please. And lock your door."

She went. She walked the length of the corridor, and turned at the corner, and stopped.

It was only after she had stood for a full minute in the dim light, with her arms wrapped around herself, that she understood what had troubled her. He had looked at the key for eleven seconds. He had not asked her a single question about it. Not who had given it, not where it had come from, not what it might open.

He had not needed to.


# Chapter 35: Two Hundred and Sixty-Five

The key opened a door in the cliff.

Ren Aizawa found it at 2:15 in the morning, after a forty-minute walk along the coast path in the dark. It was set into the rock at the base of the seawall, below the north tower, behind a curtain of dripping ivy. A low iron door, painted the color of the stone, with a keyhole the size of a coin. If he had not been told to look for it, he could have walked past it a thousand times.

He had not been told. He had worked it out.

*Two hundred and sixty-four stairs to the roof. Two hundred and nine to the north tower.* Those had been the numbers in the school. But the school was built on a cliff, and a cliff is a hill, and a hill has a bottom. He had stood on the roof on the last night of June and counted aloud, and added one more, and then had spent a week in the library with the building plans, and found that the old foundation papers listed a staircase in the cliff itself, a service stair, abandoned in the nineteen-seventies.

Two hundred and sixty-five.

He put the key in the lock. It turned with a faint, oily click.

The door opened inward on a flight of narrow stone steps, going down.

He took out a small flashlight. He did not hurry. He stepped across the threshold, closed the door gently behind him, and began to descend.

---

He counted.

The steps were steep and worn and slick with sea damp. The walls sweated in the beam of the torch. He went down for a long time, past the first landing, past the second, deeper than the foundations of the school, deeper than the cellars, into the old core of the cliff where the air smelled of salt and iron.

At the sixtieth step, the stair doubled back on itself.

At the one hundred and twentieth, a draft touched his face, cold and steady, from somewhere below.

At two hundred and sixty-four, the stair ended.

He stopped.

In front of him was a pair of heavy wooden doors, bound with iron, black with age. A single brass lantern hung above them, unlit. Above the lantern, carved into the lintel in letters so worn they were barely legible, was a name.

LANTERN HOUSE.

He stood there for what seemed a very long time.

Then he reached out, set his palm on the wooden door, and pushed. It opened without a sound.

---

The room beyond was warm.

It was the first thing he noticed. After the cold of the stair it was like stepping into a kitchen. A fire burned in a small iron stove at the far wall. A lamp stood on a round table, throwing a yellow circle across the stone floor, and beside it stood a single chair, and in the chair, with a blanket across his knees and a cup of tea at his elbow, sat the doctor.

"There you are," said Emil Kreutz.

Ren stood in the doorway.

"I was beginning to think you would not come." The old man smiled. "You are a quarter of an hour late. It is not like you."

"I took the long way."

"There is no long way. There is only the stair." He gestured at the second chair, which stood across the table, empty. "Come, sit. You must be cold."

"No, thank you."

"Ah." The doctor lowered his hand. He looked at him for a long moment, with that same gentle, bottomless curiosity. "You are angry."

"I am not angry."

"No. Of course not." He sighed, deeply, with what seemed to be genuine regret. "You are *not* angry. You are *tidy.* I forget, sometimes, how long it took me to teach you the difference."

"You gave them the box."

"I gave the box to Aoi. She gave it to the little man. It was *always* to be the little man, in the end. He was the one who needed it." The doctor sipped his tea. "You are not the only person I have been fond of, you know."

"You told them what I was."

"I told them nothing. I only let them find it." He set the cup down. "I wanted to see what they would do, once they knew. And I must say, I am delighted. They are going to *tell* you, my quiet one. They are going to stand in front of you in a classroom and say it aloud. I did not think of that."

"No."

"I did not design it." His face shone. "Do you see what that means? There is a character in the story that I did not write."

Ren was silent.

"I want to ask you a question," said the doctor. "A real one. I have waited seven years."

"Please."

"Why did you stop drawing?"

Ren looked at him.

"The last drawings you gave me are dated five years ago," said Emil Kreutz. "The sheet with the empty box. And then *nothing.* You went to your room, and shut the door, and did not come out for six days. When you came out, you were what you are."

"I was finished."

"Were you?" The old man leaned forward. His voice was almost a whisper. "Or did you draw something that frightened you?"

"I drew a box with no face in it."

"And?"

"And I could not fill it." Ren's voice was exactly as it had always been, polite, level, unhurried. "I knew the detective. I knew the liaison, and the translator, and the girl who is never wrong. I could not see the last one. A story with a space in it is a story that someone else can finish."

The doctor sat very still.

"I did not like that," said Ren. "So I stopped drawing, and began to prepare."

"For what?"

"For whoever filled it."

A long silence. The stove ticked. Then, slowly, with an expression of enormous and childlike delight, Emil Kreutz began to laugh. It was soft, wheezing, helpless laughter, and he put his hand over his mouth and shook his head and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, my quiet one. And someone *has.*"

"Yes."

"The little man. The girl. The boy with the notebook. They have opened the box and walked into the space you left." He gasped. "They are not in your story at all. They have made one of their own."

"That seems likely."

"And you are *afraid.*"

"I am not afraid," said Ren. "I am interested."

"Interested."

"I would like to know what you intend to do about it."

"Nothing." The doctor dabbed at his eyes. "Nothing at all. I intend to watch. I have spent my whole life arranging the pieces, and now, at the very end, a piece has moved by itself. It is the only thing I ever wanted."

"Then we are agreed."

"Are we?"

"I would also like to watch," said Ren.

He crossed the room. He took the second chair, the empty one, for the first time, and sat in it, with his bag at his feet and his hands folded in his lap, and for a moment the old man and the boy looked at each other across the round table in the yellow circle of the lamp.

"I have something to tell you," said Ren.

"Yes?"

"I accept your invitation. I will come home. When the story ends."

The doctor's face lit like a lamp.

"You will?"

"Of course," said Ren.

It was said so mildly, so simply, with such perfect and uncomplicated courtesy, that the old man closed his eyes and let out a long, shaking breath of contentment. He did not see that the boy across the table, who had said it so gently, was looking not at him but at the stove, at the lamp, at the door, in the order in which a man looks for exits.

---

Seventeen minutes later, a boy in a school uniform climbed out of a low iron door at the base of a cliff and stood for a moment on the wet rocks in the first gray light of morning, with the sea breaking at his feet.

He did not look back.

He climbed the seawall, one hand over the other, and crossed the lawn at an even pace, and went in through the side door of the south dormitory, and up the stairs. He counted them all. Two hundred and sixty-five.

At the top, in the corridor outside his room, he stopped.

A single sheet of paper had been slid under his door. It was lined, torn from a school notebook, folded once. He picked it up. He unfolded it.

It was a note, in a small, neat, left-slanting hand.

*We have read everything. We are not afraid of you. We would like to talk.*

*The cellar, midnight. Come alone, or not.*

*— K.*

He read it twice.

Then he went into his room and closed the door, and stood for a long time in the dark with the note in his hand and no expression on his face at all. If you had asked the clock above his desk, it would have told you that he stood there for eleven minutes.

And that when he moved, at last, it was only to take a single sheet of blank paper from the drawer, and a pencil, and sit down at the desk.

He drew a rectangle. In the middle he drew a small, careful oval. Inside it, with the point of the pencil, slowly, with great precision, he began to sketch a face.

It was thin. It was unshaven. It had untidy hair.

Beneath it, in a small, neat, even hand, he wrote a single line.

*THE LIAISON. HE CAN BE USED.*
