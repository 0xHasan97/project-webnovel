Volume 2: The Third Player

Chapters 36 to 40 (Part 3 of Volume 2)



# Chapter 36: A Truce in Three Notebooks

At 11:58 p.m., a pressed gray shape appeared at the top of the cellar stairs.

The three people waiting below heard it before they saw it: the faint tap of a shoe on worn stone, the soft pause at the fourteenth step, the half step at the bottom. Then the boy stepped into the light of the swinging bulb, with his plain black bag over one shoulder and his hands empty at his sides.

"Good evening," said Ren Aizawa. "I did not know whether to knock."

"There is no door," said Kuze.

"No. That is what I found difficult."

Nobody smiled. Wataru was sitting on the cold concrete in the center of the circle, with the box between his knees and a paper cup of tea in his hand. Himeno stood behind him with her arms folded. Kuze sat on a folding chair at the edge of the light, his notebook open on his knee and his pen resting on a clean page, exactly as he had sat on the night the box was opened.

"You came alone," said Himeno.

"The note said *or not.*"

"It was a courtesy."

"I found it very considerate." He crossed the cellar at an even pace and stopped at the edge of the circle. He did not sit. "May I?"

Wataru gestured at the nearest chair. Ren took it. He arranged his bag at his feet, folded his hands in his lap, and looked from one face to the next, pleasantly, with every sign of attention, like a man arriving at a meeting he had agreed to chair.

"I would like to say something first," said Wataru.

"Please."

"I have prepared a speech. It's not very good. I am going to say it anyway, because I have never in my life said what I meant to a person who was listening." He set down the tea. "May I?"

"I am listening."

Wataru took a breath.

---

He said it badly.

He said it with false starts and long pauses, with his hands knotting and unknotting in his lap, with an occasional glance at the floor. He said that he had read the drawings. He said that he knew what the boy was, and who had made him, and why. He said that he was angry, and then that he was not, and then that he did not know. He said that a child of nine had drawn a detective to hunt him, and that it was the saddest thing he had ever heard.

"I think you have been waiting for someone to find you," said Wataru. "For seven years. I think you built a detective who could never be fooled, and a friend who would always be kind, and a story with an ending where everyone is gone. And I think you wrote it that way because you could not imagine another."

Ren said nothing. His face was pleasant and still.

"I would like you to know that it isn't true," said Wataru. "The ending. You are not alone. There are three people in this room who have read everything. We know what you did, and we know why, and we are not afraid of you. We would like you to stop."

"Stop what?"

"All of it. The killing. The framing. The *using.*" His voice cracked. "You do not have to be what you were made to be. I do not care what the doctor wrote. I do not care what the child drew. You are a person. You get to *choose.*"

For a long moment, there was no sound at all.

The bulb creaked on its cord. Far above, the wind moved in the gymnasium roof. Himeno was holding her breath. Kuze's pen had not moved.

Ren looked at Wataru. It was the long, courteous, level look that he gave to everything. When he spoke, his voice was gentle.

"That was very well said."

"Thank you."

"It is also exactly what I wrote."

Wataru stared at him.

"I drew the liaison," said Ren, "at ten years old. I wrote, *He will be small and untidy and nobody will notice him.* I also wrote, *He will say something that he thinks I have not heard.* I wrote, *He will say that I do not have to be alone.*" He paused. "I am sorry. I did not wish to spoil it. You were very sincere."

The color left Wataru's face in a slow, even sheet.

"You said you did not draw the last character," said Himeno, sharply.

"I did not. I drew everyone else." He turned to her. "You were meant to say something like that. *You said you did not.* You were meant to be afraid, and to be angry about being afraid. I wrote that too."

"Stop it."

"I am only observing."

"You're doing it *now.* You're telling us you know what we'll say so that we'll stop saying it."

"Is that what I am doing?"

"Yes."

"Then it is working," said Ren, mildly. "You have stopped being afraid. Now you are only angry. It is a much more useful emotion."

She drew a breath to answer, and found she had nothing to say.

---

It was Kuze who spoke next.

He set down his pen. He did it slowly, and very carefully, as if the pen had become heavy. When he lifted his eyes, they were calm and gray and entirely without fear.

"You have told us what you wrote," he said. "You have not told us what you wrote *last.*"

"The empty box."

"Yes."

"It is still empty."

"It is not." Kuze's voice was thin and steady. "It has three people in it. And a fourth, who is not here. You are not frightened of that. You are *interested.* I believe that is why you came."

Ren regarded him for a moment.

"That is a very good observation."

"I notice things. It is all I am."

"It is a great deal." He inclined his head. "Then I will answer honestly. I came because I would like to propose a truce."

"A truce," said Wataru.

"Yes."

"On what terms?"

"The simplest ones." Ren unfolded his hands. "You will not take the drawings to the Task Force. You will not take them to the police. You will keep them here, in this room, and you will not speak of them to anyone who has not already read them. In exchange, I will give you something."

"What?"

"Information."

Himeno laughed, a short, hard sound. "About what?"

"About the doctor." He glanced at the box. "He is not my ally. I have been told that I am his child, and that I will go home when the story ends. I agreed, because it was the quickest way to end the conversation." The half-smile did not change. "I have no intention of going. And I have no wish for him to watch me any longer."

"You want us to use the drawings against him."

"I want you to *understand* him. That is not the same thing, but it is a very close cousin."

"Why should we believe you?"

"You should not." His voice was perfectly calm. "I do not ask you to. I ask only that you verify what I give you, and decide for yourselves."

"Then give it."

Ren was silent for a moment. He looked at each of them in turn, at the rumpled man and the straight-backed girl and the pale boy with the notebook, and something passed behind his eyes that no one could read.

"There are twelve Quiet Ones," he said. "You have a list. Ten are named. One is withheld. One is me."

"Yes."

"The eleventh is in this school."

Nobody moved.

"He was placed here eight years ago, with a small foundation grant and a recommendation from a man who does not exist. He teaches mathematics. He is respected by his colleagues. He keeps a folded piece of paper in his breast pocket, and he does not know what is written on it."

"Kurata," whispered Himeno.

"Mr. Kurata," agreed Ren. "He is our first-year homeroom teacher. He set the vote in June. And he is, I believe, the most *fragile* of all of them."

"Fragile," said Wataru slowly. "How?"

"He was meant to be an Observer. He was trained to watch, and to report, and never to act." The faintest, most courteous pause. "He has been very faithful. But he has begun to ask who is telling him what to think. It is a dangerous question for a man like that. I would handle him gently, if I were you."

"Why are you telling us?"

"Because it is the one thing I have that you do not." He rose, smoothly, and picked up his bag. "I have given you a name. You may do what you like with it. I will not interfere."

"Will you not?" said Kuze.

"I have said so."

"You *have* said so."

"It is the most honest answer I have." He bowed, correctly, with the grace of a student leaving a teacher's office. "Thank you for the tea. I did not take any. I hope that was not rude."

He went up the stairs, one at a time, and they listened to him go.

Fourteen. And the half step.

---

For a full minute, the cellar was silent.

"He gave us Kurata," said Himeno.

"Yes."

"For free."

"Nothing that boy gives is free," said Wataru. His voice was flat and tired. He lowered his head into his hands. "He told us what we would say. He told us what we would feel. He told us exactly what we would do next, and then he gave us a thing to do."

"You think it is a trap?"

"I *know* it is a trap." He lifted his eyes. "But I do not know what kind. And that is the first time in four months that I have felt the shape of him from the outside."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that he told us *Kurata is fragile.* In the same breath that he handed him over." Wataru's mouth twisted. "He wants us to find a man who will break. He wants us to *break* him. And I would very much like to know why."

Kuze looked down at his notebook. The page was clean. He had not written a single word, and now, very slowly, he wrote two, in small left-slanting letters.

*Handle gently.*

He underlined it twice.



# Chapter 37: The Observer

Mr. Kurata did not teach on Thursdays after four.

It was one of the small rules of the school: the afternoons were set aside for faculty meetings, and no one had ever seen the heavy, tired-looking man in the reading glasses leave his office before six. His door stayed shut. His light stayed on. His students had learned long ago that it was easier to pretend that he was not there.

At 4:10 on Thursday, someone knocked.

"Yes?"

"Sensei." The voice was low and gentle. "May we come in?"

He looked up from the stack of papers on his desk. The door had opened a few inches. A rumpled head had appeared, and behind it, in the corridor, two students, a girl with a straight back and a pale boy with a notebook.

"Mr. Wataru." Kurata took off his glasses and set them on the blotter. "I thought the Task Force had left."

"Most of them."

"What can I do for you?"

"I would like to ask you a question." Wataru came in, and shut the door. He looked, for once, quite calm. "May we sit?"

"I have forty ungraded papers."

"It will not take long."

Kurata looked at him. Then at the girl, who was standing by the door with her hands clasped in front of her, and at the boy, who had already found the chair in the corner and was settling into it like a cat. And then, at last, at the folded square of paper on the desk, the one that had lain in his breast pocket for four months, and which he had taken out that morning and set down in front of him, without quite knowing why.

"Sit," he said wearily.

---

It was Himeno who asked.

"Sensei, did you set the vote?"

"I set the exam."

"Whose idea was the unanimous ballot?"

"I have been asking myself that for a month." He rubbed his eyes. "I had thought it was mine. I remember writing the rules. I remember writing, *the nomination must be unanimous.* But I do not remember *deciding* it."

"No," said Wataru. "You would not."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that you are a Quiet One."

The room went absolutely still.

Kurata sat with his hands flat on the desk. He did not move. He did not blink. For almost ten seconds he sat there like a man who had been struck in the chest, with his face gone gray beneath the fluorescent light.

"Who told you that?" he whispered.

"A reporter." Wataru's voice was gentle. "And a boy."

"Which boy?"

"I think you know."

"Aizawa," said Kurata.

Nobody answered. The wall clock ticked. Outside the window, a gull cried over the sea.

"I was seven when I came to him," said Kurata. He said it quite quietly, to the surface of the desk. "I do not remember my father. I remember a smell of lavender and a man who bent down and asked me to multiply seventeen by twenty-three. I said three hundred and ninety-one. He gave me a sweet." A small, dry, humorless laugh. "I was so proud."

"You were number eleven."

"I was the *eleventh.* He said it was a good number. A prime. He said that primes are very rare." He lifted his hand and touched the folded paper without picking it up. "I was placed in a classroom at twenty-three. I have been teaching ever since. I was told that I would know when my work began."

"And did you?"

"I thought it began in April." His voice dropped. "I thought that was why I was here. I was told that a child would arrive, a very special child, and that I must watch him. Not guide him. Not correct him. Not so much as raise my voice. Only watch, and write, and send my reports to a post-office box in the town."

Kuze looked up sharply. "You wrote reports."

"Every Friday."

"May we see them?"

"They are in the bottom drawer." Kurata gestured, blindly. "Fifteen pages. Fifteen weeks. The key is under the blotter."

Kuze rose. He crossed the room and knelt at the drawer. Himeno made a small sound. Wataru, who had not taken his eyes from the teacher's face, said very gently, "Sensei. Did you ever write what you were *thinking?*"

"No."

"Never?"

"I was an Observer. I was trained to write what I *saw.*" Kurata laughed again, shakily. "I do not think I was ever trained to think."

---

The reports were neat.

They were typewritten, in a spare, careful style, each one a single page. Dates and places. Names and times. A list of every student in Class 1-C and a short, precise description of what each had done that week. Kuze read the first three aloud in a flat voice, and by the end of the third, his hands were shaking.

*Week 2. Subject R seated second row. Initial rank 2nd. Appears to have missed question 48 deliberately. No indication of motive.*

*Week 3. Truck incident at crossing. Subject R present. Subject R's response: immediate, accurate, uninflected. Staff Nurse reports pulse 62.*

*Week 4. Tachibana Gen found deceased. Subject R's alibi: library, 12 witnesses. Alibi correct in every particular.*

"He *saw* it," said Himeno. "He wrote it all down. He knew."

"I did not know." Kurata's face was wet. He did not seem to realize. "I wrote *what I saw.* I did not know *what it was.* I was only a pair of eyes."

"You sat in that room and watched four people die."

"I did not watch them die. I watched *him.*"

"Isn't that the same?"

"I do not know," said the teacher, and his voice broke, and he put both hands over his face.

Nobody spoke. Wataru got slowly to his feet. He crossed the room and sat on the edge of the desk, next to the older man, and put a hand, awkwardly, on his shoulder.

"Sensei."

"I am so tired."

"I know."

"I have been so *afraid.* For a month. Since the vote." The words spilled out in a thin, high rush. "I had the strangest feeling. As if a hand had reached into my head and moved something an inch to the left. I have been a mathematics teacher for fifteen years. I know my own mind. And I *did not think of it.*"

"No."

"Does that mean he can..." Kurata looked up. His eyes were red and terrified. "Does it mean that he can *do* that? Put a thought in a man's head?"

"I do not think so," said Wataru.

"Then..."

"I think he is simply very good at arranging what you will notice. A little pressure here. A suggestion there. A conversation in a corridor." He paused. "It is not magic. It is only attention. He has been paying attention for seven years, and you have been paying attention for four months, and he has had much more practice."

"That is not a comfort."

"No."

"What do I do?"

"You help us," said Himeno quietly.

Kurata looked at her.

"We are going to stop him," she said. "Not with a gun. Not with a trial. We are going to stop him by finding out *everything.* Who the doctor is. Where he is. What he wants. And we are going to use these." She touched the typed pages. "They are the first real evidence we have had."

"He will know."

"He knows already."

"He will..."

"Sensei," said Kuze, and it was so rare to hear his small, steady voice raised in the quiet room that all three of them turned. "I would like to say something. I do not wish to alarm you."

"Please."

"Aizawa gave us your name." He closed the folder. "He said that you were *fragile.* He said we should handle you gently."

Kurata's face went still.

"He said that," he repeated.

"Yes."

"Those words."

"Those words."

There was a long, horrible silence.

"Then I am afraid," said Kurata, in a very small voice, "that we have already made a mistake."



# Chapter 38: Fragile

The first sign was a small one.

On Friday morning, at 8:15, Mr. Kurata did not appear for first period. It was not unheard of. He had been known to be late. But at 8:30 the class began to murmur, and at 8:45 a student was sent to the staff room, and at 9:00 the headmistress herself walked down the corridor with a clipboard under her arm and a look on her face that nobody had seen before.

"Class 1-C," she said, standing in the doorway. "Your homeroom teacher is indisposed. Mr. Takagi will cover the lesson."

"Is he ill?" asked Sakaki.

"He is indisposed."

She turned and left. The door clicked softly shut behind her.

In the second row, Ren Aizawa turned a page of his textbook. His face was pleasant and unmoved. At the back of the room, by the window, Kuze Minato had gone very white, and Himeno, three seats from the front, found that she had stopped breathing.

*We have already made a mistake.*

She had thought that he was speaking of the reports. She had thought that he meant the risk of discovery, the danger of the drawings falling into the wrong hands. She had not thought, for one moment, that he meant it literally.

---

At 11:20, Wataru found him.

He had searched the school for two hours, the north library and the east wing and the staff room, with Himeno and Kuze on either side. It was Kuze who thought of the roof.

Kurata was sitting at the edge of the north tower, on the stone parapet, with his legs hanging over the drop. It was a gray, windy morning. The sea below was the color of slate. He wore the same shirt as the day before, wrinkled and untucked, and his glasses lay beside him on the stone with one lens cracked.

He did not turn when the door opened.

"Sensei," said Wataru, very softly.

"Please do not come closer."

"All right."

"I wished to be alone."

"I understand." Wataru stopped, ten paces off. Behind him, in the doorway, Himeno and Kuze held their breath. "May I sit down?"

"It is a free roof."

Wataru sat. He sat on the cold gravel, with his back against a chimney stack, and put his hands between his knees and looked at the sea, as if he had come up for the view. A gull wheeled overhead.

"I have been thinking about something," said Kurata.

"Yes?"

"About the folded paper. The one that I keep in my pocket." He did not look round. "I wrote it, you know. On the first day. I sat in my office after the first lecture, and I wrote down a sentence. I do not remember what it said. I folded it up, and put it away, and I have been carrying it for four months."

"What did you do with it?"

"I took it out this morning." He lifted his hand. In it was a small, crumpled square of white paper. "I opened it. I read it. And I understood what I was."

"What were you?"

"A *witness.* That is all. A witness who does not know what he is witnessing." He laughed, a high, cracked sound. "It is the most terrible thing in the world. To have been in the room for every one of them. To have written it all down, in a neat typeface, on a Friday. To have been *calm.*"

"Sensei..."

"I could have stopped it." He turned his head. His eyes were red and dry and enormous. "I wrote that Subject R missed question 48 deliberately. I wrote it in *April.* I could have gone to the headmistress. I could have called the police. I could have said, *there is a boy in my class who is not what he appears.*"

"You were told not to."

"I was told to *watch.*" He gave the word a terrible emphasis. "I was told I was a pair of eyes. I was told that it was a privilege. And I believed it, because I was seven when I came to him, and I have never in my life known how to be anything else."

Wataru was silent for a moment.

"Do you know what I was doing, for two years?" he said.

"No."

"I lay on a bench with a cushion on my face. I was told that I was the liaison to the greatest detective in the world. I was told that I was *essential.* And I was a drawing." He smiled, crookedly. "I was a nine-year-old child's idea of a man nobody would notice."

Kurata stared at him.

"I found out last week," said Wataru. "I have not yet decided what to do about it. I think I am going to go on lying on the bench."

"Why?"

"Because it is the only thing I am good at. And because I would like to be the kind of man who is still there in the morning."

For a long moment, the two of them sat there, a few yards apart, in the cold wind, looking at the sea.

"I do not know how to do that," said Kurata, at last.

"Nor do I. We shall have to learn together."

"I am not strong."

"No. But you are *here.*" Wataru held out his hand. "That is the whole of the trick, I think. You simply remain."

Kurata looked at the hand.

He looked at it for a long time. The wind pulled at his collar. A gull cried. Behind him, far below, a single wave broke against the rocks.

Then, slowly, he took it.

---

It was not until noon, when they had brought him down from the roof and settled him in the staff room with a blanket and a mug of tea and Himeno sitting on the arm of the chair, that Kuze noticed the time.

He took out his pocket watch. It was an old one, scratched and plain, his grandfather's. He looked at it, and at the wall clock, and at the watch again.

"Wataru-san."

"Yes?"

"Mr. Kurata was not in his office this morning."

"No. He told us. He was on the roof."

"He was on the roof from 8:00. But at 8:15, the headmistress was told he was *indisposed.* Who told her?"

Wataru frowned. "I assumed a colleague."

"The headmistress does not take reports from colleagues. She takes them from the *registry.*" Kuze's voice was very thin. "The registry sends a notice when a teacher has failed to sign in. But Mr. Kurata signed in at 7:45."

"Then who..."

"Someone *deleted* his sign-in." Kuze raised his eyes. "Someone who wished him to appear absent. Someone who knew that the headmistress would come to the classroom, in person, at 9:00. Someone who wished the *entire class* to see her face."

Himeno went rigid. "He wanted them to know."

"Yes."

"He wanted everyone to see that Kurata was in trouble."

"He wanted them to see *that.*" Kuze closed the watch with a click. "And now he wishes them to see something else."

"What?"

"I do not know." Kuze's gray eyes moved from Wataru to Himeno, and back. "But I believe that he gave us the teacher because he knew how we would treat him. Gently. With the reports. With the folder. And I believe..."

He stopped. His lips parted.

"I believe that the folder is not in the bottom drawer anymore."

He was already on his feet.

---

The office on the third floor had been tidied.

It was very neat. The blotter sat square on the desk. The chair was pushed in. The papers had been stacked and squared. On the shelf, the row of books was in alphabetical order. There was a faint smell of lemon cleaner in the air.

The bottom drawer was open.

It was empty.

Wataru stood in the doorway for a long time with his hands at his sides. Then, very slowly, he crossed the room, and knelt, and ran one finger along the inside of the drawer. It came away clean.

"Fifteen pages," he said.

"Gone."

"All of it. In a morning."

"He took it while we were on the roof." Himeno's voice shook. "He knew where we would be. He sent Mr. Kurata there."

"He did not *send* him." Kuze was standing by the window, very straight, very small. "He only made it possible. A man who has been told that he is a witness, and that he is fragile, and that he has *made a mistake.* A man who is given a quiet morning, and a quiet roof, and a note."

"A note?"

"There was a note in his pocket." He lifted a small folded slip. "I found it on the parapet, when we took him down."

He opened it.

It was typed, in the spare, careful style of the reports.

*Sensei. You have been a very good Observer. When you are ready, you may stop watching.*

*— R.*

There was no signature on the back. There did not need to be.

Wataru took the slip, and held it in both hands, and for a moment he did not seem to see it at all.

"He did it again," he said.

"What?"

"He told us the truth. *Kurata is fragile.* And then he let us handle him exactly as he was handled all his life." His voice was dull. "We were *gentle.* We were so very gentle."

"That is not our fault."

"It is entirely our fault." He lifted his head. His eyes were wet. "And I think, Miss Himeno, that it is going to get a great deal worse."

Outside the window, on the lawn below, in the thin gray light, a student in a pressed uniform was walking slowly toward the library. He carried a plain black bag. He did not look up.

He did not need to.



# Chapter 39: The Disciplinary Hearing

The disciplinary hearing was held on the following Monday, in the great hall, before the entire faculty.

It had not been called by the headmistress. It had been called by a majority of the teaching staff, in a petition delivered to her desk at 7:00 on Saturday morning, bearing twenty-three signatures. The petition cited "grave concerns regarding the conduct of the first-year homeroom teacher, Mr. Kurata, and of the Task Force liaison, Mr. Wataru, in the matter of the cellar."

It had been drafted with remarkable care. Every phrase was precise. Every date was accurate. It spoke of "unauthorized meetings with minors," of "the removal of confidential records from the faculty offices," of "a pattern of behavior consistent with an obsessive fixation on a particular student."

Nobody who read it could say who had written it.

On Monday at ten o'clock, forty-one adults filed into the great hall and sat in rows beneath the high windows. The headmistress took her chair at the head of the long table. She wore her gray suit and her silver pins and an expression of such perfect, polished neutrality that only a very close observer would have noticed that her hands, folded in front of her, had gone white at the knuckles.

"We are here," she said, "to consider the matter of Mr. Kurata."

---

He sat alone at the end of the room, in a chair that had been set apart from the others.

He had been given a clean shirt and a pair of borrowed glasses. His hair was neatly combed. He looked ten years older than he had a week ago, and perfectly composed, and his hands lay flat on his knees.

"Mr. Kurata," said the headmistress. "You have been accused of seven counts of professional misconduct. Do you understand the charges?"

"Yes."

"Do you wish to respond?"

"I do."

He rose. He took a folded sheet from his pocket and smoothed it flat. His voice, when it came, was clear and level and quite without expression.

"I have been a teacher at this school for eight years," he said. "I have never before been the subject of a complaint. I am therefore unfamiliar with the proper form of this response. I shall try to be brief."

A faint murmur. Someone coughed.

"I will say that every charge in the petition is true."

The murmur became a stir.

"I held an unauthorized meeting with two students and a former liaison in my office. I allowed them access to a confidential file. I told them certain things concerning my personal history that I had no business telling." He lifted the sheet. "I did these things because I had been told that I was a certain kind of person, in a certain kind of program, and that I had been placed in this school for a certain kind of purpose."

The headmistress did not move.

"I now believe," said Kurata, "that this was untrue."

A pause. The silence in the hall was extraordinary.

"I believe that I have been the victim of an elaborate delusion. I believe that I have suffered, for some months, from a persistent and increasingly severe belief that I was being manipulated by one of my students. It began in June, when I formed the impression that an idea I had put on an exam was not my own. It has grown. It has taken the form of an organization, a doctor, a set of children, a conspiracy."

"Mr. Kurata..."

"I have since learned that I was encouraged in these beliefs by a man who should have known better." He turned. He lifted his hand, and pointed down the length of the hall.

In the second row, in a rumpled shirt and an old pair of sneakers, with a paper cup in his hand and a look on his face that no one had ever seen, sat Wataru.

"I believe," said Kurata, "that Mr. Wataru took advantage of my fragile state. He planted these ideas. He told me that I was *special.* He told me that I had been *chosen.* He gave me a box of papers, which he said were my own reports, and which I have since discovered to be forgeries."

"I did not," said Wataru.

"He is lying."

"Sensei..."

"He told me that a child had been planning the whole thing for seven years. He told me that every person at this school had been drawn in advance. It is the most absurd story I have ever heard." Kurata's voice had begun to shake. "And I believed it. God help me. I believed *every word.*"

"This is not true."

"I am deeply, deeply sorry."

He sat. He put his face in his hands.

There was a moment of total silence, and then the hall erupted.

---

It took the headmistress eleven minutes to restore order.

She did it with a single raised hand and a voice so cold that it seemed to freeze the air. When she had finished, the room was still, and forty faces were turned toward the second row with an expression that Wataru had seen before, on the faces of juries, in a long-ago life.

"Mr. Wataru," said Aoi Tsukishiro. "Do you wish to respond?"

He stood.

He had not slept. It showed. His eyes were red, and his hair stood out in all directions, and there was a coffee stain on his collar. He looked like a man who had woken from a very long dream and found that it was a very bad one.

"I do," he said.

"Please."

"I did not forge any reports."

"The reports have been examined. They are typewritten, on school paper, signed with Mr. Kurata's initials. Our handwriting specialist has found them to be consistent with a single hand." She paused. "It is the hand of Mr. Kurata."

"Of course it is."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Of course it is," said Wataru. His voice cracked. "He wrote them. I never said otherwise. I said that they were *true.*"

"They describe a student named Aizawa Ren committing four murders."

"They describe a student named Aizawa Ren *being present* at four events. Which is a fact."

"They describe him *manipulating* those events."

"That is Mr. Kurata's interpretation."

"Which he now repudiates."

"Which he *has been made to* repudiate." Wataru lifted his hands. "Headmistress. I know how this looks. I know that I am a rumpled man with no credentials, and that I have been telling a very strange story for a month. But I ask you to consider who benefits. Who *wrote* the petition? Who *called* this hearing? Who, in this entire room, gains from the destruction of a teacher who had begun to ask questions?"

A long silence.

"That is a serious accusation," said the headmistress.

"It is a question."

"Do you have an answer?"

"I have a name."

"Then say it."

He turned. He turned very slowly, to the back of the hall, and looked down the long rows of faces to the last bench, where a student in a pressed uniform sat with his plain black bag on his knee, quite alone. Ren Aizawa had been invited, as a witness. He had been seated at the back, as a courtesy. He sat with his hands folded and his face turned to the front, with a small, polite expression of attentive concern.

"Aizawa Ren," said Wataru.

Every head in the hall turned.

The boy did not stir. He regarded the room with grave, courteous, faintly puzzled interest, like a man who has heard his name called in a language he does not quite speak.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Did you wish me to respond?"

"Yes."

"May I ask what the question is?"

"Did you arrange this?"

"Arrange what, sir?"

"The petition. The hearing. The retraction."

"I did not." Ren rose. He did it smoothly, unhurriedly, with a slight bow. "I was in the library on Saturday. The librarian has confirmed it. I did not see the petition until this morning."

"You *knew* about Mr. Kurata."

"I did. I knew that he was unwell." His voice was gentle. "I saw him in the corridor on Thursday. He seemed very distressed. I took the liberty of mentioning it to the school nurse."

"You told the nurse."

"I thought that someone should."

A murmur ran through the hall.

"And the drawings?" said Wataru. "The box? The Quiet Ones?"

"I have no idea what you mean."

"You were *in the cellar.* You sat in a folding chair and told us that you had drawn the liaison."

"I was not in the cellar." Ren tilted his head. "I was in my room, at midnight, on the night you are describing. Sakaki-kun sat with me until two. He has been unable to sleep since the Task Force left." He glanced, very slightly, at the second row. "Sakaki-kun?"

And Sakaki Rio, white-faced, miserable, shaking, rose from his seat.

"It's true," he whispered. "He was with me. I couldn't sleep, and he sat up with me. He said that he was frightened too."

Wataru stared at him.

"Sakaki-kun."

"I'm sorry." He was crying. "I'm so sorry. I didn't want to. He's been so *kind.*"

Nobody moved.

In the front row, a small, thin, pale boy had risen, notebook in hand. He opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked at Ren, standing at the back of the hall with his hands folded and his face utterly still, and for a long moment he did not speak.

"I see," said Kuze, at last, very quietly.

"Kuze-kun?" said the headmistress.

"I have nothing to add." He sat down.

And at the head of the table, Aoi Tsukishiro let out a long, slow breath, and closed the folder in front of her, and in a voice quite devoid of expression, said:

"The Council will deliberate."

---

They gave the verdict at four o'clock.

Mr. Kurata was granted medical leave, with full salary, effective immediately.

Mr. Wataru was released from his duties as liaison and escorted from the premises. His credentials were revoked. His name was struck from the Task Force registry.

And the Task Force itself, in a brief, regretful statement, was formally dissolved.

---

At 5:10, at the iron gate, a small rumpled man stood with a cardboard box in his arms.

It held a kettle, a cushion, a single paper cup. He was wearing his old sneakers and a creased shirt. He had not been permitted to take the drawings. They had been seized, with the rest of the contents of the cellar, as evidence.

Himeno and Kuze stood on the gravel in front of him.

"I am so sorry," said Himeno.

"Don't."

"It was my idea. I said we should use them..."

"It was nobody's idea. It was a *chain.*" Wataru smiled at her, crookedly. "He gave us a man. We handled him gently. He let us do it. And then he made the gentleness the evidence." He shifted the box on his hip. "It is, I must admit, magnificent."

"Wataru-san..."

"I will not stop." His voice was low and very steady. "You understand that. I do not care if they strike me off every register in the country. I am going to find out where the doctor lives."

"Where will you go?"

"To the one person who might help." He took a slip of paper from his pocket and held it up. It was torn from a notebook, and on it was scrawled a name and a phone number. "I have called in a favor."

"From whom?"

"A student." His smile widened. "Not a very good one. But the only one who ever beat Ø to a solution, in a case that Ø was credited with."

"There was no Ø."

"No," said Wataru. "But there was a case. And somebody solved it."

He turned. He lifted his hand in a small, rumpled salute.

Then he walked down the hill road, in the long light of the July afternoon, until the iron gate had closed behind him, and the cherry trees had swallowed him, and he was gone.



# Chapter 40: Kagami

The man who arrived at Aster Institute on the first of September was nineteen years old and could not stop talking.

He came up the hill road at 9:40 in the morning, on foot, in the rain, with no umbrella and a canvas satchel bumping at his hip, and he spoke the whole way, to nobody, in a rapid, cheerful, unbroken stream. He said that the rain was wonderful. He said that the cherry trees looked like wet umbrellas. He said that the gate was *much* grander than he had pictured it, and that someone ought to oil the hinges, and that he had once been told that a school's character could be known by the condition of its entrance, and he was now quite sure that it was true.

"Visitors must register," said Mr. Taguchi, from the booth.

"Oh, I *know,*" said the young man. He had a long, mobile, freckled face, a mop of unruly chestnut hair plastered to his forehead, and a pair of enormous, bright, restless brown eyes. "I've brought my letter. It's from the headmistress. It says that I am to be given a room, a desk, and *every* record in the building. Isn't that exciting?"

"I'll have to call."

"Please do. I'll wait." He beamed. "I love waiting. It's the best part of arriving."

Mr. Taguchi stared at him.

"What's your name, son?"

"Kagami Touma." He thrust out a wet hand. "I'm told I'm supposed to solve a murder."

---

The headmistress received him in her office at ten.

She did not rise. She sat very straight behind her desk, with her hands folded and the vase of white blossom at her elbow, and watched him come in. He shook the rain from his hair like a dog. He dropped his satchel on a chair. He looked about the room with undisguised delight.

"Oh, this is *lovely.* You have a window that faces the sea. I once had a window that faced a wall, but I loved it, because the wall had a crack in it that looked like the map of Portugal." He sat without being asked. "You must be Tsukishiro-sensei. I've heard so much."

"Have you."

"Wataru-san called me. He was *so* upset." He leaned forward. "I should explain. I'm not a policeman. I'm not a detective. I am a third-year at the Imperial Criminology Institute, which is a very small school for people who think too much. I once solved a case in a weekend. It was a bank robbery, in the city. The police said it was impossible. I said it was a *lie,* and the robbers had been very polite about it."

"Mr. Wataru informed me that you were exceptional."

"He's very kind. He is the only man who ever read my thesis." Kagami's grin faded. "And he told me that you have four dead students, a closed case, and a boy."

"I have no such boy."

"Of course you don't." He smiled again, bright and wide and quite without malice. "I'm sorry. I'm doing it again. I *always* do it. I say what's true, and people get frightened. It's a terrible habit."

"What is it you wish?"

"A desk, as I said. A room. The records." He ticked them off on his fingers. "And permission to speak to anyone I like, at any time, about anything, without the necessity of explanation."

"That is a great deal."

"It is *everything.*" He leaned forward, and for a moment the cheerful, eager face went very still, and what looked out of it was something sharp and grave and ancient. "Headmistress. I am going to be very honest with you. I do not think there is a murderer in this school."

She said nothing.

"I think there is something much worse. I think there is a *story.*" His voice dropped. "And I think that somebody, a very long time ago, decided how it would end."

For a long moment, they looked at one another across the desk. The rain ticked on the window glass. The white blossom trembled in its vase.

"I shall give you the east dormitory room," said Aoi Tsukishiro, at last. "Number 108."

"Wonderful."

"You will find it unheated."

"I like cold. It makes me think."

"Mr. Kagami." She did not smile. "Please be careful."

"Oh," said Kagami Touma, and his eyes sparkled. "I'm *never* careful. That's why I'm so good."

---

He met Class 1-C at lunch.

It was not planned. He had simply wandered into the cafeteria, with his satchel on his shoulder and a pastry in one hand, and stood in the doorway looking at the rows of tables with an expression of rapt curiosity. The room fell quiet. Thirty-eight heads turned.

"Hello," said Kagami. "Don't mind me. I'm only looking."

He walked slowly down the center aisle. He stopped at the second table and studied a girl's face. He stopped at the fourth and examined a boy's hands. He moved on. He lingered for a moment in front of Sakaki Rio, who had gone scarlet, and said, kindly, "You've been crying lately. It suits you."

"I..."

"Don't apologize. It means you have a heart." He moved on.

He stopped at the end of the sixth table. Two students sat there: a straight-backed girl with long dark hair and a pale boy with a notebook. They had not looked up when he entered. They did not look up now.

"Himeno Saya," said Kagami, softly. "And Kuze Minato."

Neither moved.

"I'm a friend of Wataru's."

"We know," said Himeno.

"He told me that you would."

"What did he say?"

"He said that you were the bravest people he had ever met." Kagami's voice was warm. "He said that you were *wrong,* in a few small ways, which he did not specify. But that it was a good kind of wrong."

Himeno raised her eyes. She looked at him for a long moment. Then, quite slowly, she laid down her chopsticks.

"I have a question," she said.

"Please."

"Why should we trust you?"

"You shouldn't." He beamed. "I'd be very worried if you did. I'm nineteen, and I talk too much, and I've been known to lose my shoes. But I *do* have one quality."

"Which is?"

"I tell the truth," said Kagami. "All the time. Whether it helps or not." He lifted his pastry and took a bite. "It's the only thing I've ever found that a clever person cannot predict."

At the far end of the room, in the second row of Class 1-C, a boy in a pressed uniform turned a page of his textbook.

He did not look up.

---

They met at five o'clock, on the lawn.

It was nearly dark. The rain had thinned to a drizzle, and the lamps along the path were just coming on, one by one, in the gloom. Kagami was sitting on a bench with his satchel open beside him, reading a thick folder by the yellow glow of a lantern. He did not look up when the figure approached.

"Hello," he said.

"Good evening," said Ren Aizawa.

"You're early."

"I did not know I was expected."

"Oh, I wasn't expecting you." Kagami turned a page. "I was only hoping."

Ren stopped a polite distance from the bench. He stood with his hands at his sides and his plain black bag over his shoulder, quite still, in the thin wash of lamplight. He looked as he always looked. Neat, forgettable, courteous, untroubled.

"May I ask what you are reading?"

"Your file."

"I see."

"It's very short." Kagami held it up. It was a single sheet. "*Aizawa Ren. Born in a prefecture that does not appear on any map. Educated at an academy that does not appear in any directory. Rank: second.* And that's all." He lowered it. "It's the emptiest file I've ever read. It's like the outline of a person drawn by someone who has only heard one described."

"I'm afraid I have led a quiet life."

"You have led a *drawn* life." Kagami smiled. "That's what Wataru-san said. I didn't understand it at first. I think I do now."

"Do you?"

"I think I'm beginning to." He closed the folder. He stood, and brushed the damp from his knees, and looked at Ren with those enormous, bright, restless eyes. For a moment the light of the lantern lay across his freckled face, and in it there was something so open and so unguarded that it seemed almost indecent. "Aizawa-kun. May I ask you something?"

"Please."

"Are you lonely?"

It was a very simple question. It was asked without malice, without irony, without anything at all but plain, earnest curiosity.

Ren regarded him.

He regarded him for a long time. The drizzle ticked on the path. A bell sounded, far off, from the clock tower. Somewhere in the dormitory, a window slid shut.

"I'm afraid I do not understand the question," said Ren.

"I think you do."

"I am sorry."

"Don't be." Kagami's smile widened, brilliantly. "It's the first lie you've told me. I wanted to see how it looked."

"I have not lied."

"No?" He tilted his head. "Then I suppose you must have learned it from someone. Because I know a person who says exactly those words, in exactly that tone, every time they are asked a question they cannot answer." He took a step closer. "He lies on a bench. He drinks too much tea."

"Mr. Wataru."

"Yes."

"He has been dismissed."

"He has. And I have been *hired.*" Kagami was very close now, quite within arm's length. His voice had dropped to a murmur, light and gentle and almost tender. "I would like you to know, Aizawa-kun, that I am not going to be clever. I am not going to trap you. I am not going to follow you down a corridor, or read your mail, or test you with a box."

"Then what will you do?"

"I will simply *be here.*" He spread his hands. "I will sit at the next table. I will say good morning. I will ask how you slept. I will be exactly, entirely, completely *honest,* every day, until one of us breaks."

"That seems a very inefficient plan."

"Yes."

"I do not think that it will work."

"No," said Kagami, cheerfully. "But I have never tried it before. And I have the feeling that you haven't either."

The rain had stopped. In the silence that followed, the lamps along the path hummed softly, and a thin mist rose from the wet grass, and the two boys stood facing each other in the dusk, one bright and one pale, one restless and one still.

"Good evening," said Ren Aizawa.

"Good evening," said Kagami Touma.

"Of course," said Ren.

He turned, and walked away across the lawn, and the mist closed behind him.

Kagami stood where he was, watching him go. His face, in the lantern light, was quite calm. After a moment, very quietly, he said to himself:

"He said it *first.*"

Then he picked up his satchel, and set off for Room 108, humming under his breath, and if anyone had been near enough to hear, they would have recognized the tune. It was not a melody. It was a rhythm.

Two hundred and nine beats.
