Volume 2: The Third Player

Chapters 41 to 45 (Part 4 of Volume 2)



# Chapter 41: Good Morning, Aizawa-kun

Kagami Touma said good morning to Ren Aizawa every day for nine days.

He said it at the dormitory door, at 7:40, in a bright, carrying voice. He said it at the cafeteria, at 7:55, across a crowded room, with a wave of one arm that sent a tray tumbling from the hands of a startled first-year. He said it in the corridor outside Class 1-C at 8:20, and again, at the door of the classroom, at 8:29, with a small formal bow that was just slightly too deep to be sincere and just slightly too sincere to be mocking.

"Good morning, Aizawa-kun."

"Good morning, Kagami-san."

"Did you sleep well?"

"Yes. Thank you."

"I didn't." He beamed. "I lay awake until three trying to work out why the third stair on the north tower is a different shade from the others. Do you know? It's the *oddest* thing."

"I am afraid I do not."

"No. I thought not." And he would stroll away down the corridor, whistling, with his satchel banging against his hip.

By the fourth morning, the class had begun to wait for it. By the sixth, a small knot of first-years gathered at the door at 8:29 to watch. By the eighth, a bet had been placed in the common room on whether Ren would, at any point, so much as raise an eyebrow.

He did not.

"It's remarkable," Sakaki whispered to a classmate, on the ninth morning. "He just says good morning back. Every time. The exact same way."

"Maybe he likes him."

"I don't think Aizawa-kun likes anyone."

"Then why is he so *nice?*"

Sakaki opened his mouth to reply, and found that he had no answer.

---

On the tenth day, Kagami made his first move.

It was a small one. At the lunch break, he walked into the cafeteria with his tray, scanned the room, and sat down opposite Ren Aizawa in the second row, as if the seat had been reserved for him. Several students in the vicinity pretended not to look.

"May I?"

"Of course."

"I'm *terribly* sorry to intrude." He unwrapped a sandwich. "I am going to tell you a few things, and then I am going to eat. If at any point you would like me to stop, you have only to say so."

"Please."

"The first thing is that I am very fond of you."

Ren raised his eyes.

"I mean it quite literally. I find you fascinating. I have found you fascinating since the evening on the lawn, and I have been thinking about you every day since. It is a little embarrassing. I have been told it is not a healthy way to feel about a murder suspect."

A faint stir went through the nearby tables. A girl dropped her fork.

"I'm sorry?" said Ren.

"You *heard.* I am saying it aloud because I think that is the fairest way to do it." Kagami took a bite. "The second thing is that I do not know what you are. I know a great deal *about* you. I know that you are very clever. I know that you are very polite. I know that you have never once, in front of me, said anything that was not strictly true. But I do not know *what* you are."

"I am a student."

"That is the third thing. You say that very smoothly." He swallowed. "The fourth is that I am going to tell everyone in this school exactly what I have just told you. I am going to tell them what I think, what I suspect, and what I intend to do about it. I will keep no secrets. Not from you, not from the headmistress, not from your friends."

Ren set down his chopsticks.

"That seems very unwise."

"It is *extremely* unwise." Kagami's smile widened. "It is also, I think, the only thing you cannot plan for."

"May I ask why?"

"Because every plan you have ever made depends on what people *don't* know." He leaned forward. His voice dropped, light and gentle, almost confiding. "You are the best I have ever seen at moving through a room with a piece of knowledge that nobody else has. And I am simply going to take that piece, and hold it up, and show everyone."

"I see."

"Does it worry you?"

"I do not worry."

"Of course you don't." He sat back, glowing. "I find that so *restful.*"

He went on eating. Ren, across the table, resumed his own lunch with perfect composure, one bite at a time, at the same rhythm as before. Nothing in his face had changed. Nothing in his hands. He ate his rice and drank his tea and set down his cup in the exact center of the saucer.

But, at the neighboring table, a small, thin, pale boy with a notebook had stopped writing.

He had noticed that the rhythm was not quite the same.

---

At 2:40, Kagami addressed the entire first-year class.

It was an unscheduled assembly, called on an hour's notice by the headmistress at the visitor's insistence. A hundred and sixty students filed into the lecture hall and sat in rows, murmuring. He stood alone on the raised stage in a rumpled gray jacket, with a single sheet of paper in his hand.

"Hello," said Kagami. "I shall be brief. I will tell you what I know."

He told them.

He told them that four people had died at Aster Institute since April: Tachibana Gen, Ogami Tetsu, Aoyagi Hina, and a man known as Mikado Soichi. He told them that a fifth, a boy named Honda Ryuu, was in a juvenile facility, having been placed there, in Kagami's careful words, "by a mistake that was made on purpose."

He told them that the Task Force's "closing statement" had been false, and that he knew it.

He told them that there was a man, who was not a police officer, who had been dismissed for saying so.

He told them that there was a program, and a doctor, and a place by the sea.

There was a stir. Somebody laughed nervously. A girl in the third row began to cry. Mr. Takagi, standing at the side of the stage, took a step forward, and the headmistress, in the wings, raised a single finger, and he stopped.

"I am not going to tell you who did it," said Kagami. "I do not have evidence. I have a *feeling,* and I have been told that feelings are not admissible. But I am going to tell you one more thing."

He folded his paper in half.

"The person I suspect is in this room."

A long, horrible, breathless silence fell.

"I am not going to look at him," said Kagami. "I am not going to point. I am not going to say a name. I only want to say this. If you are in this room, and you are listening, and you are the one I am thinking of..."

He smiled. It was a warm smile, and quite without malice.

"I would like to be your friend."

Then he bowed, and walked off the stage.

---

In the second row, Ren Aizawa remained in his seat while the room emptied around him.

He sat with his hands folded in his lap. He looked at the empty stage. He did not move for a full four minutes, until the janitor came to put away the chairs, and the janitor, a tired, kindly man named Mr. Oba, paused in the aisle.

"You all right, son?"

"Yes. Thank you."

"Heard the speech?"

"I did."

"Strange fellow." Mr. Oba stacked a chair. "Funny, though. I've been here nineteen years, and I never seen anybody say a thing like that out loud. About being friends. With a suspect." He shook his head. "I don't know what to make of it."

"No," said Ren. "Nor do I."

It was the first time anyone at Aster had ever heard him say so.

And it was, in the notebook of a pale boy in the back row, who had stayed behind to watch, recorded at 3:07 p.m. with a small, trembling, left-slanting underline.

*He does not know what to make of it.*

*It is the first thing I have seen him not know.*



# Chapter 42: The Matron's Tea

Kagami went to see the Matron on a Saturday.

He found her where Mio had said he would, outside the iron gate at the bottom of the hill road, in the gray coat and the polished shoes, with her hair in its tight bun and a small brass lantern on her collar. She was standing perfectly still beneath a cherry tree. She did not turn when he approached.

"Hello," said Kagami. "You must be the lady in gray."

"I am."

"I'm Kagami Touma. I've been wanting to meet you."

"I know who you are." She regarded him calmly. "The doctor has told me. He says that you are a very honest young man."

"Oh, he's far too kind."

"He also says that you will be difficult."

"I try my best." He grinned. "May I buy you a cup of tea? There is a café just down the road. It's called the Blue Hour. I'm told they have a very good barley blend."

She considered him for a long moment, with the cool, unhurried attention of a woman who has never in her life been invited anywhere she did not wish to go.

"Very well," she said.

---

They sat at the back of the café, at the table by the window, where the rain-streaked glass looked out on the wet street. Kagami ordered two barley teas and a plate of rice crackers. The Matron declined the crackers. She folded her gloves on the table and sat very upright, with her hands in her lap, and her pale eyes never left his face.

"You are here to interrogate me," she said.

"Not at all."

"Then what do you want?"

"To listen." He poured. "I should warn you that I do a great deal of talking. It is a failing. But I am capable of stopping, if a person has something she would like to say."

"I have nothing to say."

"No, I don't think that's true."

She said nothing.

"I think," said Kagami, "that you have been waiting for someone to ask you a question for a very long time. I think you have been alone with it for years. And I think that you have come to this school every week, in the same coat, to ask a girl whether she has been good, because it is the only question that anyone ever allowed you to ask."

The Matron's gloved fingers tightened, very slightly, on each other.

"You presume."

"I do. I'm sorry. It's a habit." He sipped his tea. "May I tell you what I think happened?"

"If you must."

"I think you came to Lantern House when you were twenty-two. I think you were told that you would be looking after exceptional children. I think the doctor was very kind to you. He gave you a room, and a salary, and a place to be useful. And I think that over the years, as the children grew up, you began to understand what the program was." His voice was quiet. "And you did not leave."

"Where would I have gone?"

"I don't know."

"Where does a woman go, at forty, who has never had a home?" Her voice was flat, perfectly controlled, and it shook. "I was *good.* I was the best Matron he ever had. I kept the stairs clean and the children fed. I read to them at night. I taught them to fold their hands." She drew a breath. "I did what I was told."

"I know."

"You do not."

"I do. I have a friend who did the same." Kagami set down his cup. "He lay on a bench for two years with a cushion on his face because somebody told him that he was important. When he found out that he had been a drawing, he did not go home. He is sitting in a boarding house right now, eating toast, trying to figure out how to go on."

The Matron stared at him.

"He told me to tell you something," said Kagami. "I did not know I would. But I think it is true. He said, *You do not have to keep being good.*"

For a long moment there was no sound in the café but the hiss of the espresso machine and the tick of rain on the glass.

The Matron looked down at her gloves. Her thin, severe, pale face was quite still. When she spoke, it was so quietly that he almost lost the words.

"He had a name."

"Who?"

"The child. The one you are looking for." She did not raise her eyes. "He arrived at the house when he was four. A very small boy. Silent. He sat on the third stair and counted the steps. He did not speak for two months. And when he did, the first thing he said was..." Her voice faltered. "He said, *Is it all right if I have a name?*"

Kagami waited.

"The doctor gave him one. A beautiful one. He wrote it in a letter and sealed it." She closed her eyes. "He said it would be a secret. That the child must not use it until the end of his education, and that nobody else must ever say it aloud. He said that it was the only way to keep him safe."

"Safe from what?"

"From himself." The Matron opened her eyes. "He said that a boy who knew his own name would begin to wonder who he was. And that a boy who began to wonder would stop being *finished.*"

"And he agreed?"

"He was four." She laughed, a small, bitter sound. "He agreed to everything."

"Do *you* know it?"

"No." A long pause. "I saw the envelope once. On the doctor's desk, with the wax seal. I picked it up. I held it in my hand. And I put it down, because I was afraid." She looked at him, directly, for the first time. "I have been afraid for twenty years, Mr. Kagami. It is the only thing I have ever been good at."

He reached across the table and laid his hand, lightly, on hers.

She did not pull away.

---

That evening, in the dormitory, Mio Hanazono received a message.

It came to her phone at 7:14, from an unknown number. She stared at it for a long time before she opened it.

*Mio-chan.*

*I have spoken with a young man today. He was very kind. I have not been kind to you.*

*I want you to know that you are allowed to say no.*

*— M.*

She read it three times. Then she set down the phone, very carefully, on the desk, and put her hands over her face.

On the other side of the wall, in Room 312, a boy in a pressed uniform sat at his desk with a pencil and a sheet of paper. He had been drawing for forty minutes. On the sheet, in small, neat, even lines, was a woman in a long gray coat, standing alone at the bottom of a hill.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took the pencil, and under the figure, in a small, neat, even hand, he wrote:

*THE MATRON. SHE WILL NOT BE GOOD MUCH LONGER.*

He considered the sentence.

He added a second.

*I DID NOT DRAW THAT.*



# Chapter 43: The Trust Exam

The special exam was announced on a Monday morning, in the great hall, by the headmistress herself.

She stood at the lectern in her gray suit and silver pins, with a single sheet of paper before her. Behind her, on the high stage, a large screen had been lowered. It was black. She did not look at it. She looked out at the hundred and sixty first-years in their rows and said, in a voice that carried without effort to the back of the hall:

"This is a conduct examination. It is worth one thousand Class Points. It will be held on Friday."

A rustle went through the hall. One thousand was twice the value of any previous exam.

"It is called the Mutual Trust Evaluation. Each of you will be given a form with two questions. The first is: *Whom do you trust most in this school?* The second is: *Whom do you trust least?* You will write one name for each. The names will be collected, tallied, and published to every student in the school."

She paused.

"The class that demonstrates the greatest degree of mutual trust will receive the full award. A class whose members distrust each other will receive nothing."

The murmuring grew. She raised her hand.

"There is one additional rule. You may write your own name, if you wish. But if more than five students in any class write the same name in either column, that student will be placed under *review.*"

"Review?" called a boy.

"A review is a formal examination of a student's conduct. It may result in the loss of points. It may result in reassignment." Her expression did not change. "I would advise you to answer honestly."

She stepped back. The screen behind her flickered on.

It showed a single word, in plain white letters.

TRUST.

---

By Wednesday, the whole school had begun to talk.

It was a delicate exam. It was also, in the words of a worried first-year in 1-B, "obviously a trap." Every student could see it. If you wrote an honest name for *distrust,* the name might be published. If you wrote a name that was not honest, you might be *caught.* And if too many of your classmates wrote the same name, that person would be reviewed, and perhaps reassigned, and who could say what that meant?

In Class 1-C, the debate lasted three days.

It took place in corridors and in stairwells and in the common room at night. It took place in whispers. Sakaki Rio, the class representative, called a meeting on Wednesday evening and delivered a speech about unity and honesty that nobody believed. Himeno Saya sat at the back with her arms folded and said nothing. Kuze Minato, beside her, wrote in his notebook.

"We will each write our own names," said a girl named Sugiura. "As we did last time."

"That does not work this time," said Nakamura. "The form has two columns."

"Then we write our own in both."

"They'd call that fraud."

"It's a technicality."

"It is a *trap,*" said Nakamura, who had learned a few things in the last three months. "I don't like it. I think they want us to name each other."

"Then what do we do?"

The room fell silent. And then, from the second row, a quiet, courteous voice said, "May I suggest something?"

Thirty-eight heads turned.

Ren Aizawa had risen. He stood with his hands loosely folded, in his usual neat posture, and looked around the room with grave attention.

"I think we should be honest," he said.

A murmur.

"I do not mean that we should name our enemies. I mean that we should write what is true. I will write my own answers on Friday. I will write them in full, and I will show them to anyone who asks." He paused. "I would encourage you to do the same."

"That's a terrible idea," said Nakamura. "If everyone sees who we trust..."

"Then everyone will know who we are."

"That's *exactly* the problem!"

"Is it?" Ren's voice was gentle. "I would prefer to be known. It is a great deal easier than being guessed."

He sat. There was a moment of silence. Then, slowly, awkwardly, a girl in the third row said, "I think he's right."

"I do too," said someone else.

"It's the only way it works," said Sakaki, with an unsteady smile. "If we're all honest. If we trust each other."

"And if someone isn't?" said Nakamura.

"Then we find out."

He did not look at Himeno. He did not look at Kuze. He sat down, flushed and brave, with a trembling smile, and across the room, in the second row, a boy in a pressed uniform inclined his head, very slightly, in thanks.

---

At 9:40 that evening, Kagami Touma found Himeno on the roof of the north library.

"You are avoiding me."

"I am thinking."

"Same thing, in my experience." He sat on the parapet beside her, swinging his legs. "I have been told that you think the exam is a trick."

"It is."

"Well, of course it is. They're all tricks. That's what an exam is." He smiled. "But this one is *particularly* good. Do you want to know why?"

"No."

"I'll tell you anyway. It's a trick designed by a man who has read a child's drawings." He leaned back. "The doctor knows exactly what a boy like Aizawa will do with it. He is going to *publish* his answers, in full, in front of everyone. He is going to be so honest, so open, so *vulnerable,* that it will be almost impossible to doubt him."

Himeno was silent for a moment.

"And then?"

"And then everyone who *doesn't* publish theirs will look like they have something to hide."

Her head turned sharply.

"Yes," said Kagami. "You've seen it."

"He isn't asking the class to trust each other. He's asking them to trust *him.*"

"By being trustworthy in public." He nodded. "It's the oldest trick in the book. And the only possible defense is to refuse. To say, *I will not publish mine.* And then to be the *only* person who says it."

"That would make me the one."

"The one the rest of the class distrusts. Yes." Kagami's smile was gentle. "It's a very clever little cage."

"What do I do?"

"I don't know." He shrugged. "I am only a visitor. I find these things very hard to plan." He slid off the parapet. "But I will tell you what *I* am going to do."

"What?"

"I am going to write the truth. All of it. I am going to write whom I trust and whom I do not, and I am going to hand it in unsigned, and I am going to tell *everyone* what it says." He beamed. "I think it will be the first time in the history of this school that anyone has tried to win a trust exam by being completely, recklessly, *humiliatingly* honest."

"It's going to get you reassigned."

"Oh, I certainly hope so," said Kagami. "It would be *such* a relief."

He bowed and walked away across the roof. At the door he stopped, with his hand on the frame, and turned back.

"Himeno-san."

"Yes?"

"There is one thing I do not understand about him."

"What?"

"He is going to win this exam." Kagami's face was very still. "He is going to win it by being kinder, and truer, and more open than any of us. And I do not know whether he knows that he is *also* telling the truth."

He went in. The door swung shut. In the cold night air, on the roof of the north library, a girl with a straight back sat alone, with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the dark, and thought about that for a very long time.



# Chapter 44: Results

The results were posted at noon on Saturday.

They went up on the main corridor board, in thirty-two-point type, on a sheet of cream paper bearing the school crest. A crowd had been gathering since eleven. By 11:55 it had spilled down the corridor and out the doors. When the clerk fixed the last pin, a sound went through the hundred and sixty first-years like a wave breaking.

CLASS 1-C Most trusted: AIZAWA REN (34 of 38) Least trusted: HIMENO SAYA (19 of 38), KUZE MINATO (17 of 38)

Beneath it, smaller:

*Class 1-C is placed under review in the matter of two students.*

Himeno stood at the back of the crowd. She did not move. She did not breathe. She read the numbers once, and again, and then she read them a third time, and when she looked up, she found that the people around her had begun, very slowly, to turn.

They were not hostile. That was the terrible thing. They looked at her with expressions of concern, of embarrassment, of a kind of awkward pity.

"It's not personal," a girl whispered.

"Everyone was just being honest."

"I'm sorry, Himeno-san."

She said nothing. She took one step back. Then another. The crowd parted, politely, to let her through, and she walked the length of the corridor to the glass doors at the end with her spine straight and her face perfectly blank.

Behind her, in the whispering, a name was said a dozen times, softly, in the tone of a thing already settled.

*Aizawa.*

---

He was waiting for her on the lawn.

He sat on the bench beneath the clock tower, in the thin winter light, with a plain black bag at his feet and a single sheet of paper in his lap. He did not look up as she approached. He did not move until she stopped, a few paces away, and stood there with her hands clenched at her sides.

"You published yours," she said.

"Yes."

"In full."

"As promised." He lifted the sheet. "Would you like to read it?"

"No."

"It is very short."

"I know what it says."

"Then you will not be surprised." He set the paper on the bench, face up, so that she could see. Two lines, in his small, neat, even hand.

*Most trusted: No one.*

*Least trusted: Myself.*

She stared at it.

"It was the truth," said Ren.

"It's a *lie.*"

"It is the most honest thing I have ever written." His voice was gentle. "I do not trust anyone, Himeno-san. I trust no one in this school, or outside it. I also do not trust myself. I would like the class to know. I thought it was only fair."

"You wrote that so they would feel *sorry* for you."

"Did they?"

"They *wept.*" Her voice cracked. "Thirty-four people wrote your name. You told them you trust nobody. And they wrote *you.* They wrote the one person who said he couldn't be trusted."

"Yes."

"How *dare* you?"

He did not answer. He sat with his hands folded, looking up at her with those calm, courteous, unreadable eyes. She had expected a smile. She had expected anything, a flicker, a shadow, a hint of satisfaction. There was nothing at all.

"I am sorry about the review," he said at last.

"You're *sorry.*"

"I did not intend it. I only wrote the truth. The rest was the result of what other people did with it."

"You *arranged* that."

"I arranged nothing. I answered a question." He lifted his hand, palm up. "You may think I am lying. I understand. But I would ask you to consider that I have not told you one thing in four months that was not strictly true."

"You *killed* them."

"I did not say that."

"You didn't deny it."

"No." He looked at her steadily. "I have only declined to speak."

She took a breath. She took another. She had known it would be like this. She had known, for weeks, that every conversation with him would end in the same place, in a polite, bright, airless room where there was no door. And still, standing there in the gray light with the clock tower looming above her and a hundred and sixty students pressing at the glass doors behind, she felt something deep in her chest begin to give way.

"What do you want from me?" she said.

"Nothing."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I wanted to tell you something." He rose. He stood facing her, very close, a head taller, in the pale light. "In seventeen days, the review will conclude. You and Kuze will be assigned to a different class."

"I know."

"It is the only way the class will be able to recover its points."

"I *know.*"

"I would like you to know that it will not be personal."

"It never is," said Himeno. Her voice was shaking, and she hated it. "It never *once* is. That is what I cannot bear."

He regarded her for a moment longer.

"You are the best opponent I have had," he said.

"Don't."

"It is a compliment."

"Don't *speak* to me."

"As you like." He bowed, quite formally, the exact degree. "Good afternoon, Himeno-san."

He picked up his bag. He walked away across the lawn at an even pace, and she stood where she was, shaking, with her hands pressed flat against her mouth, and did not see the boy who had been standing in the shadow of the tower for the whole of the conversation, thin, pale, with a notebook, who now lowered it very slowly and began, in a small, neat, left-slanting hand, to write.

---

Kagami's honest answers had been posted beside the rest.

They were on a separate sheet, at the bottom of the board, almost lost among the larger type. Nobody had noticed them. He had handed them in unsigned, as promised, with a note asking that they be published in full.

*Most trusted: Wataru-san, who is not here.*

*Second most trusted: Himeno Saya and Kuze Minato, who have been very brave.*

*Least trusted: Myself. I have never in my life been wrong about anyone, and I do not like what that means.*

In the corridor outside the board, in the thinning crowd, a young man in a rumpled gray jacket was standing with his hands in his pockets, looking at the sheet. He had been there for an hour. He had not said a word to anyone.

Mr. Taguchi, passing with a mop, stopped beside him.

"Son," he said. "You all right?"

"No," said Kagami Touma, cheerfully. His eyes were wet. "I am not. I have just lost."

"Lost what?"

"A very small game." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "It's all right. I knew I would. I only wanted to see *how.*"

"And?"

"And now I know." He took a long breath. "He did not manipulate the exam, Mr. Taguchi. That is what is so wonderful. He did not lie. He did not cheat. He did not so much as nudge. He simply told the truth, completely, in front of everyone." He shook his head, and laughed, softly, helplessly. "And the truth was the most effective thing he could have said."

"I don't understand."

"No. Nor does anyone." Kagami lifted his hand in a small, rueful salute. "But I think I'm starting to. And I think it is going to take me a great deal of time."



# Chapter 45: The Note

Mio Hanazono left on a Sunday night.

It was Himeno who found out, at 6:40 the next morning, when she came down for breakfast and discovered that Mio's seat at the long table was empty. It was not unusual. Mio was sometimes late. But at 7:00 the seat was still empty, and at 7:15 a plate of rice that nobody had touched had begun to go cold, and at 7:30 Himeno rose and went to the first-year dormitory and knocked on Room 214.

There was no answer.

She knocked again. She tried the handle. It was unlocked.

The room was very tidy. The bed had been made, the pillow plumped. A school uniform hung on the back of the door. Beside the bed stood a small suitcase, the kind a child might take to a sleepover, and it was open, and it was empty.

On the desk lay a single sheet of paper.

It had been folded once. It had been placed squarely in the center of the blotter, with a pen laid parallel beside it, in the manner of a person who had been taught that things should be left neat.

Himeno unfolded it.

*Dear everyone.*

*I am going home. I know that is a strange thing to say, because I have only lived here since April. But there is a place that has always been home, and I think it is time for me to go.*

*Please do not follow me. Please do not worry. I have been very happy at Aster. I have been happier than I have ever been in my life, and I wanted to say thank you.*

*I think I have been good for long enough.*

*— Mio*

*P.S. Aizawa-kun, please tell her it is all right. She will know what I mean.*

Himeno read it twice. Then she stood for a long time, with the page held in both hands, in the stillness of the small, clean room.

"Oh, Mio," she whispered.

---

By eight o'clock the whole dormitory knew.

Teachers came and went. The headmistress appeared at 8:15 in her gray suit and spoke quietly to the nurse. A police officer arrived at nine. By ten, a small cluster of students had gathered in the corridor outside Room 214, with the muted, anxious whispering of people who have been told that nothing is wrong.

Nobody had seen her leave. The night guard, Mr. Taguchi, said that he had made his rounds at midnight and again at two, and that the gate had been locked both times. The camera over the side entrance had recorded a gray figure at 3:12 a.m., carrying a suitcase, walking quickly down the hill road. The figure was too far from the lens to identify.

At 10:20, Class 1-C was called into the common room.

Mr. Takagi told them, in a thick voice, that Hanazono Mio had left the school of her own accord, and that her family had been notified. He said that the class was not to speculate. He said that counseling would be offered. He said that he was very sorry.

Nobody spoke. A girl in the third row began to cry.

In the second row, Ren Aizawa sat with his hands folded in his lap, looking at the teacher with a courteous, attentive, grave expression. He did not move. He did not look at anyone else. When the speech was over, and the class began to rise, he rose with the others and bowed, correctly, to the teacher, and went out into the corridor.

Himeno was waiting for him.

She stood against the wall with the folded note in her hand and her chin up, and when he stopped, she held it out without a word.

He took it.

He read it. It took him less than two seconds. He turned it over, and read the postscript, and turned it back, and handed it to her with a small, slight, formal bow.

"Thank you."

"That's all?" Her voice cracked. "She's *gone.* She wrote *please tell her it's all right.* What does that *mean?*"

"I do not know."

"You *do.*"

"I do not know what she meant."

"Then why do you look like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like you've been waiting for it." Her eyes were bright. "Like it's something you *expected.*"

"I have expected a great many things," said Ren. "Most have not occurred."

"Did you know she would go?"

"I knew that she might."

"And you did *nothing.*"

"What would you have had me do?" His voice was gentle. "I told her she was allowed to choose. I told her that a person who has chosen is more reliable than one who has obeyed." He paused. "I think that she has chosen."

"She's *fifteen.*"

"Yes."

"She's gone to *him.* To the doctor. To the place that made her."

"Yes."

"And you're going to just..." She broke off. She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. For a moment she could not speak. When she lowered her hand, her face was wet. "You are not even going to try."

"It is not a matter of trying."

"Then what?"

"It is a matter of *waiting.*"

Himeno stared at him.

"She will come back," said Ren.

"How do you know?"

"Because I wrote it."

It was spoken so quietly that she almost missed it. She stood very still, with her hands at her sides and her heart hammering, and she looked at his face, at the pleasant, courteous, untroubled features she had studied for seven months, and found that there was not a single thing in them that she could read.

"*What?*"

"I drew the girl who is always good," said Ren. "I drew her, at ten years old, on a sheet of paper in a house by the sea. I wrote what she would do. She would be placed beside me. She would watch me. She would be kind to me. And on a certain day, in a certain winter, she would leave." He tilted his head. "And one day, she would return."

"You can't..."

"It is in the drawings. You have the box."

"We don't. They took it."

"Then I am sorry. You will have to take my word." He inclined his head. "I would not lie to you, Himeno-san. I have never done so."

He went away down the corridor, at an even pace, and she did not call after him. She stood against the wall with the note in her hand, trembling, and slowly slid down it until she was sitting on the floor, with her knees drawn up and her face in her hands.

She did not hear the footsteps approach. She did not look up until a small, pale, familiar hand touched her shoulder, and a quiet voice said, "Himeno-san."

"Kuze-kun."

"I have been reading the note."

"I know."

"I think we have made a mistake." He sat down beside her, quite close, with his notebook closed on his knee. His gray eyes were steady. "I think that we have been looking at the wrong drawing."

"What do you mean?"

"The Matron wrote Mio a message. Kagami told us. She said, *You are allowed to say no.*" He drew a breath. "And Mio said *yes.* She went home. To the doctor. Willingly."

"Because she was ordered."

"No. Because she was *free.*" His voice dropped. "It is the thing that Aizawa told her. And it is the thing that Wataru told Aizawa. And it is the thing that Kagami has been telling everyone for a month." He looked at her. "Do you see?"

"See what?"

"Every one of them said it. *You are free to choose.* And every one of us chose *exactly what had been drawn.*"

The corridor was very quiet. Through the window at the end of the hall, far below, a figure in a pressed uniform crossed the lawn, small and dark against the gray.

"Then we have to choose something else," said Himeno, at last.

"Yes."

"Something nobody drew."

"Yes."

"What?"

Kuze did not answer for a long time. When he did, he spoke very softly, as if to himself, in the voice of a boy who has just seen the shape of a staircase that has been in front of him all along.

"I think," he said, "that we have to stop being afraid of him."

---

At 8:40 that night, in a room at the end of the south dormitory, Ren Aizawa sat at his desk with a pencil and a sheet of paper.

He did not draw. He sat with the pencil in his hand and looked at the blank page for a very long time.

On the wall above him, the clock ticked. Below, in the dark of the lawn, the cherry trees stood bare against the sea. A thin wind came through a crack in the window.

At 8:51, his phone lit up on the desk.

It was a single message, from a number he did not know.

*She arrived safely.*

*She asked me to tell you that she is sorry.*

*The doctor would like you to know that the first act is complete.*

*— M.*

He read it.

Then he set the pencil down, very precisely, parallel to the edge of the paper, and folded his hands, and sat quite still.

If anyone had been watching, they would have seen that he did not move for eleven minutes. They would have seen that his expression did not change. They would have seen nothing at all.

They would not have seen that at the end of the eleventh minute, he reached out and turned over the sheet of paper, and on the back, in small, neat letters, wrote a single word.
