# Chapter 46: The Last Class

Class 1-D occupied the end of the third-floor corridor, behind a door that stuck.

It had always stuck. In the school's unspoken geography, that was understood to be the point. The door of the last class was heavy, warped, and painted a shade of green that had once been cheerful and had since given up. Behind it lay a room that smelled of chalk and damp wool, with thirty-six desks and a window that overlooked the boiler house.

On the first Monday after the review, two students arrived at that door together.

One was a straight-backed girl with long dark hair, carrying a bag that held her whole life. The other was a thin, pale boy with a notebook pressed to his chest. They had been walked there by Mr. Takagi, who had delivered them to the doorway with the miserable air of a man delivering a pair of pets to a shelter.

"I'm sorry," he said, to the floor.

"Please do not be," said Kuze.

"It's only temporary. Until the review is over."

"The review is over."

"Yes. Well. Until it's *reviewed.*" He fled.

Himeno put her hand on the green door and shoved. It gave with a groan.

Thirty-six faces turned.

It was a strange sight, the last class. There were no neat rows. The desks had been pushed into rough clusters, as if a storm had passed through. A girl was asleep with her cheek on her arm. Two boys were arguing over a game. In the back corner, a very large student with a shaved head sat motionless, staring out of the window with the vacant, heavy patience of someone who had given up expecting to be looked at.

And in the second row, hunched over a textbook with his shoulders drawn up around his ears, sat a small, narrow, pinched boy with hair that stood up at the back.

"Kisaki-kun," said Himeno, softly.

He looked up. His eyes were wide. He half rose, and sat down again, and then, all at once, he was smiling, a shaky, astonished, incredulous smile, like a man who has opened a door to find a stranger holding a lantern.

"Himeno-san," he said. "You came."

---

Their homeroom teacher was a thin, dry woman in her fifties named Ms. Arai, who entered at 9:00 and wrote nothing on the board.

"Your standing," she said, "is the worst in the school."

Nobody argued.

"You have the lowest class total, the lowest conduct average, the lowest scores on every examination. You are, in the opinion of the faculty, a mistake." She looked around the room. "I am telling you this so that you will not be surprised when it is repeated."

"Is that supposed to motivate us?" said the boy with the game.

"No. It is supposed to be *accurate.*" She picked up a stub of chalk. "Two new students have joined us today. They have been transferred from 1-C. You will treat them with the courtesy you would extend to anyone who has fallen from a considerable height."

A snort from the back. A cough.

"Himeno Saya and Kuze Minato," said Ms. Arai. "They will sit by the window."

They sat. The chairs were old and one of them wobbled. Himeno set her bag on the floor and looked around the room, at the cracked ceiling and the peeling paint and the lines of tired, wary, unsurprised faces. She had never been in a room where nobody was trying to impress her.

She found, to her surprise, that she did not mind.

"Himeno-san," whispered Kisaki, from the row ahead. "Is it true you were first on the entrance exam?"

"Yes."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I got a thing wrong."

"What thing?"

She looked at Kuze, who was already writing in his notebook, and then back at Kisaki's pinched, hopeful face.

"I trusted the wrong person," she said. "I'm going to fix it."

---

It began, as these things do, with a small conversation at lunch.

The cafeteria was divided in unwritten ways. The first-years of 1-A and 1-C sat near the windows, in the sun. The second-years took the center. And at the far end, by the kitchen doors, under a flickering lamp, was the table that had been left for 1-D. It had a cracked laminate top and a wobbly leg and a faint, permanent smell of boiled cabbage.

Himeno carried her tray there without hesitation. Kuze followed, with his notebook under his arm. They sat at one end, and the other students, who had been about to leave, paused and sat back down.

"I want to propose something," said Himeno.

Thirty-odd faces regarded her with weary suspicion.

"We are last. By a considerable margin. I would like to know why."

"Because we're stupid," said the boy with the game.

"I don't think that's true."

"Because we're poor," said a girl.

"I don't think that's it either." She looked around the table. "I have been looking at the class report for the past hour. I have the marks for every examination since April. I have the attendance, the conduct records, the point totals." She set down a folder. "Do you know what I see?"

Nobody answered.

"A class that has been told that it is last for so long that it has stopped *trying.*"

A silence. The boy with the game looked at his plate.

"Look at this." She opened the folder. "Seven of you scored in the top ten in mathematics last month. Four of you in languages. There is a student here with the second highest score in the school on the practical science exam. He did not hand in his lab report, so he was given a zero." She looked up. "Which of you was that?"

Slowly, with the stiff reluctance of a man being asked to confess to a crime, the very large boy in the back corner raised his hand.

"I forgot," he mumbled.

"You forgot."

"I get nervous."

"What is your name?"

"Dojima Gen."

"Dojima-kun. You scored ninety-three on an exam that I scored eighty-eight on. I would like you to stand up."

He stood. He was enormous, shy, red to the ears. The table was silent.

"This class does not need a savior," said Himeno. "It needs a *list.* A list of what each of you is good at, and a plan to use it. I am willing to make that list. I am willing to write the plan. But I am not going to do it for you." She paused. "I am going to do it *with* you, or not at all."

For a long moment, nobody spoke.

Then, with a loud scrape, the boy with the game pushed back his chair and stood.

"I'm Tomoki," he said. "I'm good at puzzles."

"Good. That goes on the list."

"I'm Rina," said a girl. "I'm good at talking people into things."

"Excellent."

"I'm Kisaki," said a small, thin voice, "and I'm not good at anything. But I'm *very* good at being overlooked."

A pause. Himeno smiled. It was a real smile, wide and a little fierce.

"That," she said, "is the most useful thing anyone has said all day."

---

At the other end of the cafeteria, in the sunlit window seats, a group of students from Class 1-C was eating lunch in a loose, easy cluster.

Ren Aizawa sat at the center of it. He had his tray before him and his book beside it, and he was listening, with polite attention, to a girl who was telling a long story about a physics experiment. Several others were laughing. Sakaki Rio sat at his elbow, flushed and happy, passing the salt.

From where he sat, the end table was clearly visible. It was a long way off. The light was poor. He could see a girl with a straight back standing at the head of a cracked laminate table, speaking, and a ring of faces turned toward her, and a notebook open beside a bowl of rice.

He watched for exactly as long as it took to finish his tea.

Then he set down his cup, in the exact center of the saucer, and turned his head toward the window. The first frost had come in the night. A thin white rime lay on the lawns. And at the edge of the grass, near the iron gate, very small in the distance, a figure in a gray coat stood quite still, looking up at the school.

It was not the Matron.

It was a man, tall and thin, in a long dark overcoat, with a pale face and a hat pulled low. He did not move. He did not seem to be waiting for anyone.

Ren looked at him for eleven seconds.

Then the man, as if he had felt it, lifted his head and looked back across a quarter mile of frozen grass, directly at the window where the boy sat, and tipped his hat.

And Ren Aizawa, for the first time in the history of Aster Institute, set down his tea cup in the wrong place.

It was a very small thing. Half an inch to the left of the center. Nobody noticed.

Except a pale boy at the end table, who had looked up from his notebook at that exact moment, and who wrote it down.

*11:48. The cup.*


# Chapter 47: The Daily Briefing

"I should like to start," said Kagami Touma, "by announcing our plan."

He said it in the staff conference room at 6:30 on Tuesday morning, to six people and a kettle. The room had been reclaimed. The map of butcher paper was back on the wall, covered with fresh arrows. A pot of strong tea steamed on the table beside a plate of rice crackers. Round the table sat Wataru, in a borrowed cardigan, with three days of stubble; Hoshina Aya, in her beret, with a pad on her knee; Himeno and Kuze, side by side; and a very small, very nervous boy who held his cup with both hands.

"We are going to tell Ren," said Kagami.

Silence.

"Tell him what?" said Hoshina, at last.

"Everything."

"I'm sorry?"

"I propose that every morning, at 8:29, before class, I walk up to Aizawa Ren and tell him what we are going to do that day." He beamed. "In full detail. Without omission. I will tell him whom we will interview, what documents we will read, where we will go, and why."

Nobody spoke. Wataru, who had been lifting his cup, set it down slowly.

"You are out of your mind," he said.

"Oh, quite possibly."

"He will know every move."

"Yes."

"He will be able to *counter* every move."

"Yes. If he wishes." Kagami spread his hands. "But think. A man like Aizawa needs one thing above all others. He needs an *asymmetry.* He needs to know something you do not. It is the only lever he has. Every trick he has played has depended on the gap between what he understood and what you understood." He leaned forward. "I am proposing to close the gap."

"By telling him our plans?"

"By taking away the one thing that makes his plans work."

Himeno was frowning. "You are saying that if he knows what we will do..."

"Then he has to *act.*" Kagami's eyes glowed. "He cannot sit back and watch. He cannot let us blunder into a trap. If I say, *today we will visit the library to read the old incident logs,* he must decide, in the next ten minutes, whether to let us, or to stop us. And whichever he chooses, he reveals something."

"He could simply say nothing."

"He could. And that would be the first tell." He sat back. "I do not need him to confess. I only need him to *move.* Every move is a piece of evidence. And I would very much like to see what he does when he has nowhere to hide."

Hoshina looked at Wataru. Wataru looked at his tea.

"It is the stupidest plan I have ever heard," he said, finally, quietly.

"Yes."

"I think it might work."

"Thank you!"

"No. Do not thank me. I am terrified." He rubbed his face with both hands. "But it is *honest.* It is the only thing that he has not seen. A child drew us, and every one of us was a person who would lie to survive. I never imagined I might be a man who told the *whole* truth, on purpose, to the person who is trying to kill us."

"I do not think he is trying to kill us," said Kagami.

"No?"

"I think he is trying to *win.* It is not at all the same." He smiled. "And I do not think he knows yet what it will cost."

---

At 8:29, in the corridor outside Class 1-C, Kagami presented himself.

"Good morning, Aizawa-kun."

"Good morning, Kagami-san."

"I have a statement."

"Please."

"Today at 4:00, we are going to the east wing archive to search for the original blueprints of the north tower observatory. We believe there is a hidden room below the one that we have already found. We will be accompanied by a former liaison, a journalist, and two students." He ticked it off on his fingers. "We will be searching the archive until 6:00. We will then report to the headmistress. I wanted you to hear it from me."

Ren stood quite still.

Around them, the corridor had gone quiet. A handful of first-years had stopped, watching, with the stiff, avid stillness of children at a fireworks display.

"That is very thorough," said Ren.

"Thank you."

"May I ask why you are telling me?"

"Because I do not want you to be surprised."

"I am rarely surprised."

"No. That is what I have been told." Kagami's smile did not waver. "I thought you might like to try it."

Ren regarded him for a moment. His expression was courteous, grave, unchanged.

"I will not interfere," he said.

"Oh, I know."

"You know?"

"You said it in the cellar. *I have said so.* You are a man of your word." Kagami bowed, with exaggerated ceremony. "Good morning, Aizawa-kun."

He walked away. In the wake of his departure, the little crowd of onlookers parted and re-formed, buzzing. Ren stood alone for a moment in the middle of the corridor. Then he turned, and went into Class 1-C, and sat down at his desk.

At 8:31, in the front row, a girl whispered to her neighbor: "He didn't look worried."

"He never does."

"No," said the first girl, slowly. "But he usually *does* something. He usually says something clever. He just *stood* there."

It was Sakaki who heard it, three seats away. He set down his pencil and, for the first time in a month, felt a small, cold, very specific unease.

---

At 4:00 that afternoon, the party arrived at the east wing archive.

It was a long, low room under the eaves, lined with steel cabinets and smelling of mildew. A single skylight let in a gray, watery light. Hoshina found the plans in the third drawer, in a tube marked with a faded stamp: *Foundation Papers, 1962. Property of the Trustees.*

"Here," she said.

They unrolled it on a long table, under a lamp. It was a beautiful drawing, in blue ink on linen, the north tower in section, every stair numbered and every landing marked. Kuze ran a finger down the margin.

"Two hundred and nine," he said. "Two hundred and sixty-four to the roof. And here..." His finger stopped.

At the bottom of the sheet, below the foundation line, was a thin dotted outline, a staircase drawn in a lighter ink, descending into the cliff.

"Two hundred and sixty-five," whispered Himeno.

"It was in the plans all along."

"He told us."

"He told us nothing. He *counted* it." Kuze's voice was very low. "He has been walking down those stairs since July."

Kagami was standing at the end of the table, hands in his pockets, looking at the plan with an expression of pure delight.

"Isn't it wonderful?" he said. "A hidden door in a cliff. It's just like a storybook."

"It's a trap," said Wataru.

"Of course it's a trap." Kagami grinned. "But it's an *honest* trap. It has been waiting for us for sixty years."

A faint sound came from the doorway.

They turned.

Ren Aizawa was standing on the threshold, in his pressed uniform, with his plain black bag over his shoulder. He did not seem to have approached. He had simply, quietly, arrived. His face was courteous and attentive and entirely without expression.

"I am sorry to interrupt," he said. "I was told that you would be here."

"You were," said Kagami. "By me."

"Yes. I thought I should come." He looked at the plan on the table, and then at each of them in turn. "I believe that I may be able to save you some time."

"Oh?"

"I know where it leads."

A pause.

"I will take you there," said Ren, "if you would like."

Nobody moved. In the long, cold, sunless room, the six of them stood motionless around the blueprint, and Kagami Touma, for the first time in his life, was quite unable to think of a single thing to say.

"Is that a *move?*" whispered Wataru.

"I believe," said Kagami slowly, "that it is the best one I have ever seen."


# Chapter 48: Nothing Happens

For three weeks, nothing happened.

It was the most disturbing thing that had ever happened at Aster Institute. The day after the offer in the archive, Ren Aizawa did not appear at the east wing. He did not send a note. He did not repeat the invitation. At 8:29, when Kagami approached him in the corridor and asked whether he would like to name a date, he said, courteously, "Not yet," and went into the classroom.

The next morning, the same.

The morning after that, the same.

"He is waiting," said Himeno.

"Of course he is waiting," said Kagami. "It is what he does."

"For what?"

"For us to want it badly enough."

They sat in the conference room with a plate of rice crackers growing stale on the table, and the map of arrows on the wall, and the hanging, humming silence of people who had braced for a blow and found only air. Wataru lay on the bench with a cushion on his face. Hoshina flipped a pen between her fingers. Kuze wrote nothing.

"He gave us a door," said Wataru, from beneath the cushion.

"Yes."

"He told us where it leads. He told us that he would take us. And then he said *not yet.*"

"Yes."

"I think that is the cruelest thing I have ever seen."

"I think it is *brilliant,*" said Kagami. His eyes were bright, but there was a strained edge in his voice. "It is the exact opposite of what I did. I gave him every piece of information I had, and he has given me *none.* He has handed me a single, glittering fact and then put his hands in his pockets."

"What do we do?"

"We wait."

"For how long?"

"For as long as he does."

Nobody said what they were all thinking, which was that Ren Aizawa had been waiting his whole life, and that they had not.

---

On the second Wednesday, Kisaki Haru raised his hand.

It was a small gesture. He was sitting in the second row of Class 1-D, with a pencil in his fist and a sheet of paper before him, and Ms. Arai had just finished a lecture on comparative economics. She turned slowly, her chalk stub dusty between her fingers.

"Yes, Kisaki."

"I have a question." His voice cracked. He tried again. "I have a question, sensei."

"Then ask it."

"On the practice exam. Question twelve. You said the answer was *demand elasticity.* But I think it's *cross-price.*"

A hush fell. Thirty-six heads turned. A girl in the back gasped.

Ms. Arai looked at him for a long, long moment.

"Show me your working."

He did. He walked to the board, white-faced and trembling, and wrote it out in a hand that shook so badly that the chalk skittered. It took four minutes. When he had finished, there was a total silence.

"That is correct," said Ms. Arai.

A rustle.

"I made an error. In the printed key." She set down her chalk. "It has not been caught by the examiners in six years. Kisaki Haru, you have just earned this class twenty points."

"Twenty?"

"For *diligence.* It is a category I have never had occasion to use." She did not smile. But something in her face had altered. "Sit down, Kisaki."

He sat. He was shaking. And from the window, over the rim of her notebook, Himeno Saya watched the small, thin, white-faced boy who had once been used as a ghost, and found that she had to look away.

---

By the third week, 1-D had climbed from last to fifth in the school's weekly standings.

It was a tiny gain. It was invisible to the rest of the school. But it was *recorded.* The clerk who posted the rankings noticed. Mr. Takagi noticed. And a quiet, tired, silver-haired woman in a gray suit, who sat in a high office with a vase of white blossom at her elbow, noticed, and for the first time in many months, picked up her pen and wrote a single line in a private notebook.

*1-D. Interesting.*

In the cafeteria, at the cracked laminate table, the class had begun to change. They ate together now. They had a list on the wall, the one Himeno had started, with names in one column and talents in the other. They had a system. Dojima, the large shy boy, had been put in charge of the practical sciences. Rina, who could talk people into things, ran negotiations with the other classes. Tomoki, the puzzle-lover, kept the point ledger.

"We are going to be third by Christmas," said Himeno.

"We are going to be *first,*" said Tomoki.

"Don't say that."

"Why not?"

"Because it's a thing people say before they are wrong."

"Then what should I say?"

"Say that we are going to be *accurate,*" said Kuze, softly. It was the first thing he had said in three days. He did not look up from his notebook. "It is the only thing worth being."

Nobody laughed. But a few of them smiled, in the weary, wary, hopeful way of people who have been told for so long that they are nothing that they have almost forgotten how to be anything else.

---

On the last Friday of the third week, Kagami stood in the corridor outside Class 1-C at 8:29 and said:

"Good morning, Aizawa-kun."

"Good morning, Kagami-san."

"I have no statement today."

"No?"

"No. I have nothing to say." He smiled, a little too brightly. "I thought you might like to know. We have not found anything. We have not done anything. We are sitting in a room eating crackers. It is the most boring thing I have ever done."

"I am sorry to hear it."

"I thought you might be." He paused. "Aizawa-kun. May I ask you something?"

"Please."

"Why are you waiting?"

Ren regarded him, with grave, courteous attention.

"Because it was your idea to tell me everything," he said. "I am only following it."

"I'm sorry?"

"You wished to remove the gap between what I knew and what you knew. I have given you my word that I will not interfere. I have told you that I know where the stair leads. You have the door, Kagami-san. You have always had it." The half-smile did not change. "I am simply declining to open it for you."

"Why?"

"Because if I open it, it is mine. And if you open it, it is yours." He inclined his head. "I think that you would prefer it to be yours."

Kagami stood very still.

Then, slowly, his bright, restless, freckled face did something it had not done in three weeks. It went quiet.

"Oh," he said.

"Yes."

"You are not stopping me."

"No."

"You are *letting* me."

"I am doing exactly what you asked."

"That's..." Kagami laughed, shakily. "That is the most infuriating thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I am glad that you find it so."

"Why?"

"Because it means that you are paying attention." Ren bowed. "Good morning, Kagami-san."

He went into the classroom. Kagami stood alone in the corridor with his hands in his pockets, rocking slightly on his heels. The bell rang. A pair of first-years brushed past him, giggling, and he did not notice.

At last, very quietly, he said to himself:

"He has made honesty *work for him.*"

And, a little later, so softly that nobody heard it:

"I think I have just lost again."


# Chapter 49: The Festival

The announcement came on the first Monday of November, in the form of a bright orange poster pinned to every notice board in the school.

AUTUMN FESTIVAL THREE DAYS. EVERY CLASS. EVERY TALENT. OPENING CEREMONY: SATURDAY, NOVEMBER 22.

Below it, in smaller type, were the rules.

*Each class will operate one booth or performance. Proceeds will be added to the class total. A prize of one thousand points will be awarded to the most successful class. The Festival Committee will be selected by the headmistress.*

"A thousand," said Tomoki, in the cafeteria, staring at the poster with the slack, glazed expression of a man shown a mountain of gold. "It's a thousand points."

"It is a trap," said Kuze.

"Of course it's a trap. They're all traps. But a thousand *points.*"

"Which class is first at present?"

"1-C. Four thousand nine hundred."

"And we are?"

"Fifth," said Himeno. "At three thousand and forty."

"Then the gap is..."

"Eighteen hundred and sixty. A thousand-point prize would not close it." She tapped the poster. "But it would put us third. And it would put 1-C at nothing."

"How?"

"Because the prize is not an addition. It is a *redistribution.*" Her eyes narrowed. "Look at the last line. The class with the highest proceeds takes the points *from the lowest.* The second takes from the second lowest. It is a zero-sum exam."

A pause.

"We are not the lowest," said Rina, slowly.

"No."

"1-B is."

"Yes."

"So if we win..."

"We take a thousand from 1-B. And someone takes it from us."

"Who?"

"Whoever wins is the person who *chose* the structure." Himeno's face was very still. "I think we should find out who is on the Festival Committee."

---

The list was posted on Wednesday.

It was a short one. Seven names, in the headmistress's crisp, upright hand. Four teachers, two third-years, and, at the bottom, set a little apart from the rest, as if added in a hurry:

*Chairman: Aizawa Ren (1-C).*

A murmur ran through the corridor. A girl said, "Of *course.*" A boy said, "He's only a first-year." Another said, "It's because of the Trust exam. They love him."

In the second row of Class 1-C, Ren Aizawa read the notice with an expression of mild, polite surprise.

"I had not expected it," he said, to nobody in particular.

"You were nominated," said Sakaki, beaming. "By the whole class."

"I did not ask."

"I know. That's why it was so *nice.*"

"I see." Ren straightened his cuffs. "Then I shall have to do my best."

At the other end of the school, in the conference room, Kagami read the same notice with the bright, startled look of a child who has been handed a small, unexpected knife.

"Chairman," he said. "Of the *festival.*"

"It is the largest event of the year," said Wataru, from the bench. "Four hundred students. A hundred visitors. A security detail of twelve."

"And the committee has full control over the layout, the schedule, the guest list, the staffing, and the security arrangements."

"Yes."

"For *three days.*"

"Yes."

"He wants us to watch him," said Hoshina slowly.

"No." Kagami's voice was soft. "He wants us to *want to.*"

---

The first meeting of the Festival Committee was held that Friday, in a small room on the second floor.

Seven people sat around a table. The four teachers, polite and slightly bored. The two third-years, a pair of sleek, ironic young men in perfect uniforms, who had sat on the committee for two years running and regarded the first-year chairman with amused skepticism. And, in the chair at the head, a pressed, pleasant, composed boy with a folder open before him and a pen at his hand.

"Thank you for coming," said Ren. "I shall be brief."

He laid out a plan. It was clear, reasonable, and in every respect admirable. He proposed a schedule of events. He proposed a seating plan. He proposed a rotation of volunteers, a list of vendors, a map of the grounds. He proposed that the proceeds be pooled and distributed according to a point system of his own devising, which was fair, transparent, and just slightly more favorable to the larger classes.

"I notice," said one of the third-years, a tall boy named Maki, "that the plan has no provision for security."

"No. I thought that the Task Force should handle it."

"The Task Force has been dissolved."

"It has been reconstituted." Ren turned a page. "I have taken the liberty of inviting Mr. Kagami Touma to serve as the festival's chief security consultant."

A silence.

"The *visitor?*" said Maki.

"Yes."

"The one who has been going around telling everyone that there is a murderer in the school?"

"Yes."

"You *want* him?"

"I want him close," said Ren. "I would like him to see everything I do."

Maki blinked. The other third-year laughed, a short, sharp, surprised sound. One of the teachers said, "Is that wise?"

"I think it is the only way to be believed," said Ren, gently. "If there is something to see, I would like him to see it. If there is nothing, I would like him to be able to say so."

He closed the folder.

"I am told," he said, "that honesty is the best policy."

---

At 6:20, in the dusk, Kagami Touma found him on the second-floor landing.

"You invited me."

"I did."

"On the committee."

"As a consultant."

"You said you wanted me to see everything."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ren was standing at the window with his hands folded behind his back, looking out at the lawn. The light was nearly gone. Below them, in the half-dark, a group of first-years were stringing paper lanterns between the trees.

"I told you," he said. "Because I would like to be believed."

"By whom?"

"By you."

Kagami laughed, high and nervous. "That's not a reason."

"It is the only one I have." Ren turned. In the dusk, his face was pale and calm and perfectly clear. "I wish to ask you a question, Kagami-san. And I would like you to answer it honestly."

"Of course."

"If, in two weeks, at the festival, someone were to die. What would you do?"

The words fell into the dusk like a stone into a well. Kagami did not move. His grin stayed on his face a moment too long, stiff and strange, like a mask that had begun to slip.

"Who?" he said.

"I do not know."

"Are you going to kill someone?"

"I have not decided." Ren's voice was entirely level. "I am telling you only that it is possible. I have been honest with you since the day we met. I would like to be honest now."

"Why would you tell me?"

"So that you may prevent it."

"You are *warning* me?"

"I am informing you." He inclined his head. "What you do with the information is a matter for you."

For a long moment, they stood on the landing, two shapes in the dusk, one bright and one pale. Far below, in the garden, somebody laughed. A string of paper lanterns, newly hung, caught the last of the light and glowed orange against the dark.

"I think," said Kagami, very slowly, "that this is the worst thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I am sorry."

"No. You are not."

"No," agreed Ren. "But I thought it would be polite to say so."

He bowed, and went down the stairs.


# Chapter 50: The Festival Plan

The weeks before the festival passed in a haze of paint and paper and string.

The school transformed. Every class commandeered a corner. Banners went up between the dormitories. Booths were nailed together from old desks and bedsheets. In the music room, the second-years rehearsed a choral number so badly that the janitor took to wearing earplugs. In the north library, a group of first-years built a papier-mâché replica of the clock tower and, on a dare, set a small firework off in its base. It exploded with a rather feeble pop. Nobody was hurt.

And in the conference room, at the end of the third-floor corridor, a small, determined group of people stared at a large map and tried to decide how to stop a murder that had not yet happened.

"We have eleven days," said Himeno.

"Ten and a half."

"We do not know the target."

"We do not know the *method.*"

"We do not know," said Wataru, from the bench, "whether he is lying."

"He is not lying," said Kagami.

They looked at him. He was sitting cross-legged on the table with a pad of paper in his lap and a pencil behind his ear. He had been very quiet for ten days, and when he spoke, it was in a voice that was neither bright nor restless, but flat and thoughtful.

"He told me the truth," said Kagami. "He is a man who tells the truth. It is the thing that makes him so very hard to catch."

"Then he really will kill someone."

"He said it was *possible.* He said he had not decided." Kagami rubbed his eyes. "I think that is true. I think he has built a situation in which a death is one of several outcomes, and he will choose the one that serves him best. He will wait until the last minute. He will let the festival unfold. And then he will see which of the paths is open."

"That is monstrous."

"Yes."

"How do we stop it?"

"We remove the paths."

He slid off the table and went to the map. It was a large drawing of the school grounds, in colored pencil. He took a red marker.

"The festival has three days. On the first, the booths and the opening ceremony. On the second, the performances and the dance. On the third, the closing banquet and the prize." He drew a line. "Each day has a high-risk window. The opening, when the crowd is thickest. The dance, when the lights are low. The banquet, when the visitors have gone and the staff is thin."

"He has designed it that way."

"Yes. It is a good schedule. It is a schedule I would have designed myself." He tapped the map. "So we do not guard the windows. We guard the *people.*"

"Which people?"

"The ones he could use." Kagami turned. His face was unusually solemn. "Himeno. Kuze. Wataru. Hoshina. Kisaki. Sakaki-kun. The Matron. Mio, if she returns. Mr. Kurata. The headmistress." He counted on his fingers. "That is ten. If we keep each of them under observation for the full three days, he will have nobody left to take."

"And himself?"

"I shall be watching him."

"Alone?"

"Of course. He has asked me to."

A long silence.

"That is the one part of the plan that I do not like," said Wataru.

"Nor do I." Kagami smiled, wanly. "But I have been told that it is the right kind of foolish."

That night, Kuze Minato sat alone in the north library with his notebook.

It was 11:40. The room was dark except for a single lamp on the corner desk. Outside, a thin sleet was falling. He had been writing for two hours, and the page before him was crowded with tiny, left-slanting lines.

*Day 1. Opening ceremony. 4:00 p.m.* *Day 2. Dance. 7:00 p.m.* *Day 3. Banquet. 6:00 p.m.*

He had been thinking about the drawings.

*THE BOY WHO WRITES EVERYTHING DOWN WILL FIND THE OTHERS. HE WILL BE THE ONLY ONE WHO TELLS THE TRUTH.*

He had been thinking about how the child had drawn him. A pale boy, thin, with a notebook. A pale, thin, nobody boy, with a talent for noticing.

*He will be the only one who tells the truth.*

It did not say *he will live.*

He closed the notebook.

He sat for some time, looking at the lamp. Then, very slowly, he drew a fresh page toward him and wrote at the top, in careful capitals, a single heading.

*IN THE EVENT OF MY DISAPPEARANCE.*

Beneath it he wrote, in order:

*1. The notebooks are in the third drawer of the north library desk. The key is under the clock.*

*2. Himeno-san should read them first. She will know what to do.*

*3. Do not look for me.*

*4. If I am dead, I would like it recorded that I was not afraid.*

He looked at the last line for a long time.

Then, with a small, dry, almost rueful movement of his mouth, the nearest thing to a smile that had ever touched his face, he crossed out the fourth.

*4. If I am dead, I would like it recorded that I was a little afraid, and went anyway.*

He folded the sheet. He put it in the envelope. He put the envelope in the drawer.

He did not hear the footsteps behind him until they had stopped.

"Kuze-kun."

He did not turn. He knew the voice. It was low, calm, courteous, and it was no more than three paces away, in the dark between the shelves.

"Aizawa-kun."

"You are working late."

"I am always working late."

"Yes." A pause. "I wondered if I might ask you for something."

"I will not say yes."

"I did not expect you to." The footsteps came a little closer. The lamp lit the edge of a pressed gray sleeve. "I should like to ask you to take part in the festival."

"In what capacity?"

"As a witness."

Kuze set down his pen.

"You will understand," said Ren, gently, "that I have a great deal of respect for what you do. You notice things. You write them down. You keep a record." He stopped, at the edge of the light. "I have been thinking about what happens to a record when the person who keeps it is no longer able to."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It is not."

"What is it?"

"An invitation." The faintest pause. "I should like you to be present at the festival. In a particular place, at a particular time. I should like you to see something. I cannot tell you what."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will not see it."

"Is that all?"

"That is all."

Kuze turned, finally, in his chair. He looked up at the boy standing in the shadow of the shelves, at the pleasant, forgettable, composed face that he had studied for seven months, and at the small, polite half-smile that had not changed since April.

"I have known since the first day," he said, "that you would one day ask me for something."

"I know."

"I have known that it would be something I could not refuse."

"I did not say that."

"You did not have to." His voice was very small and very steady. "I will do it."

"Thank you."

"On one condition."

"Yes?"

"That you tell me one true thing. Now. In this room. Something that I may write down."

Ren was silent for a long moment. The sleet ticked against the window. The lamp hummed.

"I did not kill Tachibana Gen," he said.

Kuze stared at him.

"I did not touch him. I did not speak to him after the stairwell. I did not give him a push, or a shove, or a word. I set up the evening, and I left him to it." The half-smile had not changed. "He died, I believe, because he was afraid of me. I did not have to do a thing."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the truest thing I know."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because you asked for something that you might write down," said Ren. "And I would like it to be accurate."

He bowed, and went out into the dark.

Kuze sat for a long, long time in the lamplight, with the envelope in the drawer beside him and the sleet ticking against the glass. At last he drew the notebook toward him, and opened it, and in a small, trembling, left-slanting hand, he wrote:

*Nov 11. 11:52 p.m. Aizawa confirms: Tachibana was not killed by his hand.*

*He did not say that anyone else was not.*
