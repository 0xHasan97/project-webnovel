# Chapter 51: Ten Names

On Thursday night, Kagami Touma laid ten index cards on the conference table, face up, in two rows of five, and said nothing for almost a minute. It was the longest he had been silent since September.

Round the table sat Wataru, in his borrowed cardigan; Hoshina Aya, with her beret beside her notepad; Himeno and Kuze, side by side; and a plate of rice crackers that nobody had touched in an hour.

"Himeno Saya," said Kagami at last, touching the first card. "Kuze Minato. Wataru-san, who will object. Hoshina-san, who will object louder. Kisaki Haru. Sakaki Rio. The Matron, if she comes. Mr. Kurata. The headmistress." His finger stopped over the tenth card, which lay face down. "And Hanazono Mio, who is not here and whom I cannot guard. I put her on the table anyway so that I would not forget."

"That is nine people and a ghost," said Wataru.

"It is ten. I counted twice."

"You count like a man who wants it to be ten."

"I count like a man with ten hands." Kagami smiled, but it was thin. "I cannot hold more. I have tried."

He gave out the pairings the way a man deals cards he does not trust. Wataru would stay at Kuze's shoulder for three days. Hoshina would take Himeno. Dojima, the very large boy from 1-D, who could not be argued with because he did not argue, would sit beside Kisaki at the ticket table. Rina, who could talk anyone into anything, had volunteered to adopt Sakaki and was already planning his schedule. Mr. Takagi would sit outside the headmistress's door, because the headmistress had agreed to nothing else. Detective Sato had lent two plainclothes officers, one of whom would park outside Mr. Kurata's flat in the town.

"And the Matron?" asked Hoshina.

"She has refused. She says she has never been guarded in her life and would not know where to put her hands."

"I am not a thing to be guarded," said Himeno.

"You are the girl who is never wrong. He drew you. If he wants a chair empty at the end of this, he will want it to be yours."

"That is not comforting."

"It was not meant to comfort," said Kuze, without looking up from his notebook. "It was meant to be accurate."

Nobody laughed. Kagami gathered the cards, squared them, and set them in the center of the table like a hand he had decided to play.

"And you?" said Wataru quietly. "Who has you?"

"Him," said Kagami. "I have him. I will be at his elbow for three days."

"That is the part I do not like."

"You have said so." He brightened, painfully. "I like it less every hour. It is how I know I am not being clever."

---

On Friday, at 8:29, he told Ren everything.

"Good morning, Aizawa-kun."

"Good morning, Kagami-san."

"I have ten names." He recited them, one after another, in the flat, careful voice of a man reading out a will. He recited the pairings. He recited the posts. At the end he added, "And I will be with you."

A cluster of first-years had stopped to listen. A girl whispered, "He's telling him everything again." Ren stood among them with his bag on his shoulder and his hands loose, and did not so much as shift his weight.

"That is a good list," he said.

"Is it the one you would have made?"

"It is close."

"What is the difference?"

"I would have made it shorter." The half-smile did not change. "But I am not guarding it."

He reached into his bag and took out a folder, and from the folder a single laminated sheet, which he held out in both hands. Kagami took it. It was the festival schedule, minute by minute, three days long, with every booth, every stage, every stair and gate marked in tiny, even letters. Beside each, in a second color, the name of the volunteer assigned to it.

"I have sent copies to the headmistress, to Detective Sato, and to Kuze-kun," said Ren. "This one is yours. I would like you to know where everyone will be."

"You are giving me your map."

"It is also yours. I made it for the festival." He bowed. "Please excuse me. There are forty green armbands to count."

He went into class. Kagami stood in the corridor with the laminated sheet in his hand, and read it twice from the first line to the last, and found that it was perfect. There was no gap in it. There was no hour that was not accounted for, and no door that was not watched.

"It is the safest festival in the history of the school," he said aloud, to nobody.

It occurred to him, a little later, in the cafeteria, over a pastry he did not eat, that this was exactly what a man would say who was about to be surprised.

---

At 11:52 that night, a slip of paper was pushed under the door of a room in the north library annex.

Kuze Minato had not been asleep. He had been sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark for some time, with his notebook shut on his knee and his shoes still on, as if he had known the paper would come. He heard it whisper across the floorboards. He did not move for a full minute. Then he knelt and picked it up.

It was typed. It was brief.

*Tomorrow, 4:12 p.m. The base of the clock tower, east stair. Bring the notebook. You will not be harmed. Mr. Wataru may stand ten paces away. He will not be needed.*

*It will be something worth writing down.*

He read it three times. He turned it over. The back was blank, and there was no signature, because there did not need to be one.

In the morning he showed it to Himeno, and then to Wataru, and last, at 8:29, to Kagami, who read it in the corridor with his lips moving and said, in a voice that was almost gentle, "I have told him everything for nine days, and he has told me nothing. And now he writes to you."

"Yes."

"Will you go?"

"I said that I would," said Kuze.

"That is not the same as wanting to."

"No," said Kuze. "But it is the same as having noticed that I have already gone."


# Chapter 52: The Ceremony

The Autumn Festival opened under a high white sky, at four o'clock on a Saturday afternoon, with the smell of frying oil and the sound of eight hundred paper lanterns knocking softly together in the wind.

They had been strung between every tree on the lawn. They hung over the gate and the cliff path and the long walkway to the tower, red and gold and a deep, dusty purple, and when the sun came out from behind a cloud they all lit at once, like a sentence finished. A hundred visitors had come up the hill road. Parents, alumni, three men from the ministry in dark overcoats, a woman with a camera who said she was from a magazine. They stood on the lawn in the cold with paper cups of tea and looked up at the school with the polite, uneasy expressions of people who had read the newspapers.

The headmistress spoke for ninety seconds. She welcomed the guests. She thanked the faculty. She said that Aster Institute was proud of its students and that the school had endured a difficult year. She did not say which parts had been difficult. She stepped back from the lectern, and the applause that followed was warm, and a little relieved.

"And now," she said, "the Chairman of the Festival Committee."

Ren Aizawa came up the steps of the low stage at an even pace. He wore his uniform, pressed, with a green armband on the left sleeve, and he carried no notes. He looked out at the lawn, at the lanterns, at the faces turned toward him. He bowed.

"Thank you for coming," he said. "I will be brief."

His voice was not loud, but it carried. It was the voice of someone asking a stranger to pass the salt.

"Please enjoy yourselves. Please be kind to the visitors, who have come a long way. If something frightens you, tell the nearest person wearing a green armband. There are forty of them. I counted them myself." A small ripple of laughter. "And if you see someone who is not enjoying himself, I hope you will sit with him for a while. It does not cost anything."

He bowed again. The applause was louder this time.

On the roof of the north library, with a pair of opera glasses he had borrowed from the drama club, Kagami Touma counted ten people. Himeno, with Hoshina at her elbow, by the puzzle house. Kisaki and Dojima at the ticket table, the smaller boy visibly vibrating. Sakaki and Rina by the tea stall, Rina already talking. Mr. Takagi in a folding chair outside the headmistress's door, looking like a man guarding a very ill-tempered bird. Wataru, hunched, cushion under one arm, six paces behind a pale boy in the crowd.

He did not count the Matron, because she had not come. He did not count Mio, because she could not.

At the foot of the stage, Ren stepped down from the platform and stopped to speak with a woman from the ministry. He did not look up at the library roof. He did not need to. Kagami was exactly where he had said he would be.

"Eight," said Kagami, lowering the glasses. "Eight in sight. Nobody has so much as sneezed."

In his earpiece, a very small, very tired voice said, "Tell me when it is over."

---

At 4:07, Kuze Minato left the crowd.

He walked alone along the cliff path with his notebook against his chest. A pace behind him and a little to the left, with the sullen, patient air of a man trying to look like part of the scenery, Wataru followed at what he judged to be ten paces. He counted them. He did not like counting them. It reminded him of the person who had asked him to.

The clock tower stood at the east end of the grounds, a squat stone column with a copper roof gone green and a clock face that was always three minutes slow. A narrow stair wound up its outer wall. At the base of the stair, where the lanterns thinned and the festival noise fell away to a murmur, two people were standing in the shade.

One was Ren Aizawa. He had arrived first, by some route Kuze had not seen, and he stood with his hands loosely folded in front of him, looking at the tower wall as if reading it.

The other was a man Kuze had seen once before, through a window, across a quarter mile of frozen grass.

He was tall and thin, in a long dark overcoat with one mismatched button, and he held a battered hat in one hand. His face was long and gray and deeply tired, a face that had been put away damp in a drawer many years ago and had never quite dried. He might have been fifty. He had the stillness of a man who had spent most of his life waiting for something to walk past.

"Good afternoon," said Ren. "Inspector."

"I haven't said that I am one."

"No. You have tipped your hat twice, and you have a notebook in your left pocket that is the same make as the ones Detective Sato carries." The half-smile did not change. "I assumed."

The man looked at him for a long time. Then, slowly, he turned the hat in his fingers.

"Ishikawa Daigo," he said. "Fifteen years in Organized Crime and Missing Persons. I received an invitation to your festival. It was addressed to me at my flat, in a hand I did not recognize, and it said that I would find something I had been looking for."

"I did not send it."

"No?"

"The school sent it, on behalf of the committee. I am the committee." Ren inclined his head. "I thought you would like to be asked."

Wataru, ten paces off, had stopped breathing. Kuze did not look at him. He had opened his notebook and uncapped his pen, and he was writing in tiny, quick, left-slanting letters, and he did not dare to lift his eyes.

"I have been inside a great many burned buildings," said Ishikawa. "A great many rooms where something was meant to be found and was not. I am told this school stands on top of one." He reached into his coat and took out a photograph. "I would like to show you something, and watch what you do."

"Please."

It was an old group photograph, the kind taken on the front steps of an institution once a year, in rows, with the smallest at the front. Forty-odd children in plain clothes, squinting into the sun. Behind them, a stone doorway, and above the doorway, carved in worn letters, a name.

"Lantern House," said Ishikawa. "A hundred and eleven children went through it in ten years. Forty were transferred when it burned. Seventy-one are not on any list." He laid his thumb at the edge of the print. "I have collected thirty-two of these photographs, from thirty-two different years, in attics and church basements and the desk drawer of a dead cook. I want you to look at the third step."

Ren looked.

On the third step, in every row, sat one small boy. In this photograph he was perhaps six years old. He was dark-haired, thin, and held very straight. Where his face should have been there was a small, clean, pale oval. It had been cut out with something very sharp.

"In every picture," said Ishikawa, "the same child. And in every picture, the same hole."

"That is unfortunate," said Ren.

"I have fifteen years of photographs with a hole in them. In all that time I have never met anyone the right size to fit it." The tired gray eyes did not leave the boy's face. "You are the first."

"I am told that I am average."

"What is your name?"

"It is on file."

"I have checked," said Ishikawa. "There is a blank."

"Then it is on file," said Ren, gently, "in the place where it was put."

A long silence. In the distance, a child shrieked with laughter. A paper lantern bumped the tower wall and swung back.

"I will write 'the boy,'" said Ishikawa at last. "In my notes. I do not have anything else."

"That seems fair."

"I do not like you."

"I understand."

"I do not think you have done any of the things I would like to believe you have done. I think I am being shown a very large door by someone who wishes me to walk through it." Ishikawa put the photograph away. "I would like you to know that I will walk through it anyway."

"I would be disappointed if you did not."

For the first time, the Inspector's gaze shifted. It moved past Ren's shoulder to the small pale boy with the notebook, who had not looked up.

"And who is this?"

"A witness," said Ren.

"Of what?"

"Of what you do with what you see."

He bowed, correctly, to both of them. He walked away along the cliff path, at an even pace, toward the lanterns and the noise, and neither man moved until the green armband had disappeared among the crowd.

---

"Son," said Ishikawa, at last.

Kuze looked up. It was the first time he had done so in eleven minutes.

"Write what you saw. All of it. I will do the same." The tired voice had dropped. "In the spring we will compare."

"Yes, sir."

"And I will tell you one thing for nothing." He put on his hat. "That boy has just introduced me to you on purpose. He has put an honest policeman and an honest student in the same patch of shade, and he has walked away and left us to talk. Ask yourself what he wants us to do together."

He nodded to Wataru, who stood very still with a cushion under his arm, and went away down the path.

Kuze looked at his notebook. The last line he had written was a small, flat, precise record, in his own cramped hand.

*4:19 p.m. Two honest men, introduced.*

He read it for a long time. Then he added, underneath, a second line, and underlined it twice.

*Witnesses are not evidence. Witnesses are what evidence is for.*


# Chapter 53: A Fair Price

The rules of the festival were posted at 5:30, in thirty-two-point type, on the board outside the great hall.

Himeno Saya read them standing, with a paper cup of tea going cold in her hand and Hoshina Aya at her shoulder, and then she read them again, and then she put the cup down on the edge of a bench and did not seem to notice.

"It has changed," she said.

"What has?"

"The prize." She tapped the board. "It said that the class with the highest proceeds would take a thousand points from the class with the lowest. It was a transfer. It was a trap, because it meant that somebody had to lose." She stepped back. "Look at it now."

Hoshina squinted. *Revised by order of the Festival Committee, 5:15 p.m. The prize will be paid from the school's festival fund. No class will lose points on account of another. Every class is guaranteed to retain no less than ninety per cent of its pool.*

"That's kind," said Hoshina.

"Yes."

"That's good for us, isn't it? We are not going to be robbed by a class that is better at selling cabbage."

"Yes."

"Then why do you look like that?"

"Because he took away the trap in front of everyone," said Himeno, "and he did it at 5:15, after the doors opened. It is the only time of day it could look like generosity."

---

Class 1-D had a booth, and a very good one.

It was a long wooden counter at the foot of the lawn, built out of old classroom desks and painted a dark and cheerful red. Dojima Gen, the enormous shy boy who forgot his lab reports, stood behind a griddle in a borrowed apron, turning savory pancakes with a gravity that was frightening to watch. The queue stretched forty meters. Rina stood at the front, taking orders, bargaining with the second-years, laughing, selling a pancake and a sticker and a promise that the next one would be bigger. Tomoki ran the puzzle house behind them, a tent full of locked boxes and brain-teasers, charging a hundred yen for the chance to open one and winning, by his own admission, nothing at all.

"I am very bad at business," he told a visitor, cheerfully.

At the ticket table, a small, pinched boy with hair that stood up at the back kept the till. Kisaki Haru had balanced the cash twice. He had been a ghost, a login, and a rumor. Today, for the first time in his life, he was a person whom customers thanked.

"Himeno-san!" He flushed to his ears. "We've taken twenty-one hundred."

"Already?"

"Since five. It's the pancakes. A man from the ministry came back for a third."

"A man from the ministry," said Himeno slowly.

"He said that it reminded him of his grandmother's."

She looked at the long red counter and at the line and at the large boy, who had gone scarlet with pleasure, and found, to her annoyance, that her eyes had begun to sting.

"You have all done very well," she said, in a stiff voice. "Carry on."

Behind her, Hoshina raised her eyebrows and said nothing, which was the kindest thing she could have done.

---

The 1-C tea house stood at the far end of the lawn, in the shade of a gingko tree whose leaves had turned an extraordinary gold.

It was not a booth. It was a room, built of folding screens and paper, with a low table and a dozen cushions and a kettle on a charcoal stove. There was no menu. There was no price. On the table sat a small wooden box with a slot in the lid and a handwritten card.

*Tea. Whatever you like to give.*

It was very quiet. It was the quietest place on the grounds. Visitors who stepped out of the noise and the oil and the paper lanterns found themselves, a little to their surprise, sitting cross-legged on a cushion with a cup in their hands, saying nothing, listening to the kettle tick. Several stayed for an hour. A woman from the magazine wept without apparently knowing why, and left a folded bill in the box that was a good deal larger than the cost of the tea.

Ren Aizawa knelt at the stove. He wore his green armband. He poured with both hands, and he did not speak unless he was spoken to.

Kagami sat across from him with a cup in his own hands, on a cushion that was too low for him, with his knees up around his ears.

"You are not trying to win," he said.

"No."

"You are the only class on the field that is not."

"I have no wish to." Ren refilled the cup. "It would be inappropriate for the chairman."

"That is the first thing you have said in nine days that I do not believe."

"I am sorry."

"No, you are not."

"No," agreed Ren. "But it is true that I would prefer not to win. I think that is the reason you do not believe it."

A faint, bitter, delighted laugh escaped Kagami before he could stop it. He put down the cup.

"Why did you change the prize?"

"Because a rule that makes someone lose is a rule that someone will break." Ren set the kettle on the stove. "I would prefer the festival to finish."

"You told me that someone might die."

"Yes."

"And then you made the rules kinder."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"So that no one has a reason to be desperate."

The words were said so mildly, and so simply, that Kagami almost let them go by. He sat very still, with his cup in his hands and the kettle ticking, and the paper walls glowing amber with the lantern light. Then he heard what he had been told.

---

At 6:40, Himeno found him on the edge of the lawn, standing alone under the string of lanterns, in the half-dark, with his hands in his pockets and his whole face gone quiet.

"You look like someone who has been hit," she said.

"I have been." He did not turn. "Do you know the first thing I learned at the Institute? When a person dies, the police ask three questions. Who gained. Who was desperate. Who was near."

"Yes."

"He has just answered two of them. In public. Before anyone has died." He drew a breath. "The prize is paid from the fund. No class gains from another's loss. The points guarantee removes the last reason for a student to be afraid. He has made this the most comfortable festival in the history of the school."

"And?"

"And whatever happens now, nobody will be able to say that it was a desperate act. Nobody will be able to say it was a debt, or a fear, or a cheat. If someone dies, it will not look like a boy who was pushed. It will look like a murder, with no motive anywhere in the room."

"That is horrible."

"Yes." Kagami finally turned. His freckled face, in the dim colored light, was white. "And the only person in the entire school who is visibly innocent of every one of those motives is the man who removed them."

Himeno looked at him for a long moment.

"He is not preventing a death," she said slowly. "He is clearing the stage for one."

At 7:00, the day-one tally was read aloud from the steps of the great hall. Class 1-A, 2,310. Class 1-D, 2,205. Class 1-C, 1,640. Class 1-B, 1,590.

There was a roar from the red counter at the bottom of the lawn. Dojima lifted a pancake over his head like a trophy. Rina screamed. Tomoki fell backward into a bin of wrapping paper and lay there laughing.

At the far end of the grounds, in the shade of a gold gingko, a boy in a green armband knelt at a charcoal stove and set a kettle back on its trivet, and did not look up.


# Chapter 54: The Lanterns

At dusk, they lit the wishes.

It was an old Aster custom, though nobody could say how old. On the evening of the first day of the festival, visitors and students alike were handed small squares of paper and a pencil, and told to write one thing they hoped for, and to hang it, folded, from a lantern. By eight o'clock the whole lawn glowed. A thousand small, folded hopes rocked in the wind among the red and gold and purple, and in the dark between the trees, couples stood close together and children ran with their arms out.

Kagami Touma stood at the foot of the cliff path with a cup of tea going cold and his eyes on a green armband forty meters away.

He had not let Ren Aizawa out of sight in nine hours. It had been, as far as he could tell, the longest day of his life. He had followed him from the stage to the tea house, from the tea house to the ticket table, from the ticket table to a ministry official who wished to shake his hand. He had sat on a cushion that was too low and drunk four cups of tea he did not want. He had watched the boy bow to strangers and fold his hands and say, to a weeping woman, *It is all right to stay as long as you like.*

"He has not done a single thing," he told Wataru, in the earpiece, in a low voice. "Not one. I would give a year of my life for him to take one step that I could not account for."

"Be careful what you offer."

"I am being careful. It is exhausting."

"Mr. Kagami."

He turned.

She had come up out of the dark without a sound, as she always did. The gray coat. The tight bun. The brass lantern on her collar, catching the light. She stood on the path with her gloved hands folded and her face as composed as ever, but she was a little paler, and there was a small tremor in the fingers.

"Matron," he said, delighted. "You came."

"I was invited."

"By whom?"

"By a boy with a green armband. In a letter. He said that I might like to see the lanterns." She did not smile. "I did not tell the doctor."

Kagami did not know what to say. For a moment they simply stood there in the dark, a bright, untidy, freckled young man and a stern, upright woman in a coat too heavy for the evening, while the paper lights swung in the wind.

"Would you like to write one?" he said.

"I do not know what to wish."

"Anything."

"That is the difficulty."

He held out a square of paper and a pencil. She took them, and looked at the pencil as if she had never held one. Then, slowly, with a care that was almost painful to watch, she wrote a single short line. She folded it twice. She reached up, on tiptoe, and hung it from the lowest lantern in the row, and stepped back.

He did not read it. He would have liked to. It was the only time in his life that he had wished to disobey himself, and he stood very straight and kept his hands in his pockets.

"It is not a wish," said the Matron. "I am afraid that I have not learned how."

"What is it?"

"A practice." She looked up at the folded square swaying in the lantern light. "Mio-chan. You are allowed to say no. I am saying it to myself first."

---

They walked along the cliff path together for a time without speaking. The wind came off the sea, salt and cold. Below them the surf broke on rocks the color of old iron, and ahead, around the curve of the wall, the clock tower stood black against a sky full of low stars.

"The doctor was at dinner on Thursday," said the Matron.

Kagami went still.

"I do not say that he is here. He is not. He is where he always is." She did not look at him. "But he spoke of tonight. He was in an excellent humor. He ate two helpings of the rice pudding."

"What did he say?"

"He said that the child would warn you."

"Aizawa-kun has warned me already," said Kagami slowly. "He told me on the second-floor landing. He told me that someone might die."

"Yes. The doctor said so." Her voice was perfectly level. "He said, *The child will warn the honest one, because it is the one thing the honest one cannot refuse to believe.* And then he said that you would protect ten people."

"Ten."

"He said the number twice. As if the number were the joke." She stopped walking. The wind pulled at the hem of her coat and it did not stir. "I asked him what he meant. He laughed. He said, *Ten is how many the honest one can hold. Eleven is how many the child needs.*"

A gull cried somewhere in the dark below. The tide went on striking the rocks.

"Eleven," said Kagami.

"I do not know what it means. I have never known what any of it means. I only remember what I am told." She turned at last and looked at him, and her pale eyes were wide and very tired. "I thought that you would wish to know. I did not think that I would be allowed to tell you."

"You are allowed," said Kagami, gently.

"Am I?"

"I think you are the only person here who is."

She lowered her eyes. She stood for a moment with her gloved hands folded, a stiff, small, upright figure in the lantern light, and then, so quietly that he almost lost it in the wind, she said, "Thank you. I did not know that I could be thanked."

She walked away along the path, toward the gate and the hill road. She did not look back. After a time, he saw the gray coat pass beneath the last of the paper lanterns and disappear.

He stood where he was, with his cold tea in his hand.

*Ten is how many the honest one can hold.*

In his earpiece, Wataru was saying something. He did not hear it. He was counting, on his fingers, in the dark, the names of the people he had been told to guard, and he came to ten, and stopped.

It was the first time in his life that he had counted something and been afraid of the number.

---

At 9:40 that night, on the other side of the school, a pale boy with a notebook sat in the north library, writing.

*Day one. 4:12. Ishikawa. 5:15. The prize is paid from the fund. 6:40. K. says: no desperation. 8:10. The Matron came, was told, told us.*

He stopped. He read the last line twice. The lamp hummed. Wataru was asleep in the next chair with his cushion on his face.

Underneath, in smaller letters, Kuze wrote:

*Everything today was true. That is what frightens me. When has he ever needed to lie to me?*

He had been told it would be something worth writing down. He had written it. He did not yet know what it was.


# Chapter 55: Eleven

The first day of the festival closed at eleven o'clock, to the minute.

The lanterns were dimmed. The booths were shuttered, one after another, in a long, rattling, good-natured line. The visitors went down the hill road with their paper bags and their small sleepy children, and the three men from the ministry were walked to their car by the headmistress herself. At 10:58, Mr. Takagi folded his chair and reported that no one had so much as knocked on her door. At 10:59, Detective Sato's officer phoned from the town: Mr. Kurata had dined at six, watched a documentary on herring, and was now asleep in his armchair with the television on.

At 11:00, Kagami called the roll.

He did it in the conference room, with the map of arrows behind him and the ten index cards in a line on the table. One by one the pairs came in, tired, rumpled, smelling of oil and woodsmoke, and one by one he turned a card face down. Himeno. Kuze. Wataru. Hoshina. Kisaki. Sakaki. The headmistress, by telephone. The Matron, who had been seen leaving the gate. Mr. Kurata, by report. And the tenth, the card that had been face down all along, which he turned over last and held for a moment in his fingers, and did not put on the pile.

"Nobody dead," he said.

Nobody cheered. The room was too tired for it. Hoshina let out a long breath through her nose and began to take off her beret, and Wataru slid down the wall until he was sitting on the floor with his cushion in his lap.

"We did it," said Kisaki, from the doorway, in a small, wondering voice. "We got through the first day."

"Yes," said Kagami. "We did."

He said it as a man says a thing that he does not wish to be true.

---

At 11:11, there was a knock.

It was a very polite knock, two taps and a pause. Kagami did not need to look up. He had been waiting for it since nine.

"Come in, Aizawa-kun."

The door opened. Ren Aizawa stood on the threshold in his pressed uniform with the green armband still on his sleeve. In one hand he held a single sheet of paper. His face was courteous, attentive, and quite unchanged, as it had been at the stage, and the tea house, and the foot of the clock tower, and at every other minute of the day.

"I am sorry to disturb you. I thought you would wish to see this."

He crossed the room and laid it on the table, face up, beside the ten cards.

It was typed, on school paper, in an ordinary font. It had no signature. It said:

*The eleventh will not wake.*

Nobody moved.

"Where did you find it?" said Kagami. His voice had gone very quiet.

"In my tray, in the committee office, at 9:47. I was there for four minutes. I read it, and I brought it to you as soon as the roll was done."

"You waited an hour and twenty minutes."

"I did not wish to interrupt the roll."

"You wished me to finish it," said Kuze, softly, from the window. "You wished me to have the ten."

"Yes," said Ren. "I thought it would be fair."

Himeno had half risen. Her face was white. "Did you write it?"

"I did not."

"You would say that."

"I would," Ren agreed. "It is also the case."

Kagami was staring at the paper. His lips moved. *The eleventh.* He was counting again, and this time he did not stop at ten. There was a list in his pocket, the list Hoshina had given him in September, folded small and soft with handling. Twelve lines, numbered, in a copyist's hand. Ten of them circled.

*11. Withheld.*

And Mr. Kurata, in a borrowed shirt, in a staff room, in July, saying with a kind of quiet shame: *I was the eleventh. He said it was a good number. A prime.*

"Kurata," said Kagami.

"Yes," said Ren.

"The eleventh Quiet One."

"Yes."

"He is one of my ten."

"Yes. He is guarded." The half-smile did not change. "I thought you might like to check."

Kagami was already moving. He crossed the room in three strides and snatched up the telephone and punched in the number that Sato had given him, and the room fell absolutely still, so that everyone heard the thin, tinny ringing, one, two, three.

Then a click, and a voice. A young man's voice, bright and professional.

"Officer Mori."

"It is Kagami. How is Mr. Kurata?"

"Fine, sir. Sleeping like a stone. I looked in on him at ten."

"Looked in. Not a minute ago?"

"No, sir. His sister brought him a cup of tea around nine-thirty, and she said not to wake him. I thought it was kind of her."

The room was silent. Kagami stood with the receiver at his ear and the cord wound tightly round his fingers, and it was a very long time before he spoke.

"His sister."

"Yes, sir. Tall, quiet. Gray coat. She said she had come in from out of town."

"Officer Mori. Mr. Kurata does not have a sister."

A pause. Then, in a voice that had suddenly lost all its brightness, the officer said, "Oh."

---

They ran.

It took nine minutes to reach the town in Sato's second car, with Wataru in the back seat clutching his cushion and Himeno beside him with her hands clamped over her knees. They found the flat dark. They found the officer on the stairs, gray-faced, with his notebook out. They found, in a small neat room that smelled of lemon polish and herring, a man in an armchair with the television murmuring in the corner and a cup of cold tea on the table beside him.

He was breathing. He was warm. His pulse was slow and even. He did not stir when Wataru took his hand and said his name, and he did not stir when Kagami, kneeling in front of him with his freckled face stripped of every trace of cheerfulness, said it again, louder.

"He is drugged," said the paramedic, ten minutes later, in a low voice. "Something very mild. A sedative. He should be fine. But he won't wake before Monday."

"Monday," said Kagami.

"He will be perfectly all right. He will simply sleep through it."

"Through the festival."

"Yes."

Wataru sat on the floor beside the armchair, with his rumpled head in his hands. When he spoke, his voice came out muffled and cracked.

"He told us. *The eleventh will not wake.* He did not say it would be death. He said *wake.* I should have heard it."

"We both should have."

"I did not think..." He lifted his head. "Where was Aizawa? When she brought the tea?"

Nobody spoke.

It was Kuze who said it, quite softly, from the doorway, with his notebook open against his chest and his eyes on a single line.

"He was with you," he said. "All day. Nine hours. At the stage, and the tea house, and the lanterns. You had not let him out of your sight."

Kagami did not answer.

"He was never nearer than forty meters from this town. And you were the one beside him." The small steady voice did not waver. "It is the best alibi in the history of the school, and it was you."

In the corner of the room, on the television, a quiet voice was explaining something about herring. Mr. Kurata slept on in his armchair with his glasses crooked on his nose and his hands folded on his chest, a heavy tired man who had asked, in July, for someone to take his hand, and been given it.

On the table beside the cold tea lay a sheet of paper that none of them had seen. It had been folded in half and placed with care at the exact center of the saucer.

Kagami unfolded it.

It was a hand-drawn card. It was the head and shoulders of a woman in a long gray coat, with her hair in a tight bun and a small brass lantern on her collar, drawn in pencil in small, careful, upright lines. Beneath it, in a hand that every person in the room knew by heart, a single line.

*THE MATRON. SHE WILL NOT BE GOOD MUCH LONGER.*

Beneath that, in a second hand, in cramped, unfamiliar, hurried letters, as if added later by someone else:

*He did not draw the sister.*
