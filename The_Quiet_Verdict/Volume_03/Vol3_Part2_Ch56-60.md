## Arc 2 Recap: The Hunt

*Ren Aizawa has finished his first term at Aster Institute with four dead and no charge. The Task Force is gone, the box is gone, and a boy of nineteen who tells the truth has arrived to hunt him.*

*The festival opens in three days. Ren has told the hunter that someone may die. He has not said who.*

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event comes within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the ledger, in the writer’s own handwriting, and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not say how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows.*

# Chapter 56: The Empty Cup

By the time they reached Mr. Kurata’s flat, the tea was cold.

The cup sat on the table beside the armchair, a thin skin of milk cooling on its surface. The television murmured about herring. Mr. Kurata slept with his hands folded over his stomach and his glasses crooked on his nose. A paramedic had checked his pulse twice and found it steady. He had not woken when Wataru said his name.

“He’ll be all right,” the paramedic said. “He’ll sleep through tomorrow. Maybe longer. We’ll take him in and keep him under observation.”

Kagami looked at the cup. “Who brought it?”

Officer Mori stood in the narrow hall, very straight, very pale. “A woman. She said she was his sister.”

“Did you ask her name?”

“No.”

“Did you see identification?”

“No.”

“Did you ask Mr. Kurata whether he had a sister?”

Mori’s mouth opened. It closed. “I thought she was family.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No,” said Mori. “I didn’t.”

The officer had been assigned to watch the flat. He had checked the windows, the front door, the stairwell. He had sat in the car below and watched the building for three hours. When a woman arrived carrying a paper cup in a covered tray, he had let her pass. She knew his name. She knew he had been sent by Detective Sato. She had said Mr. Kurata would be embarrassed to have a policeman watching him sleep.

“I thought she was trying to be kind,” he said.

“Did she seem frightened?” asked Kuze.

Mori considered. “No. She seemed… expected.”

“Expected by whom?”

“I don’t know.”

Himeno stood beside the chair. Her gaze moved from the sleeping teacher to the cup, then to the small table, then to the narrow strip of light under the front door. “What time?”

“About nine-thirty. She stayed less than ten minutes.”

“Could you describe her?”

Mori rubbed his forehead. “A woman. Middle-aged, maybe. She wore a coat. Gray, I think. She spoke softly.”

“Hair?”

“I don’t know.”

“Height?”

“About yours.”

“That isn’t a height.”

“I’m sorry.” He looked toward the armchair. “She said he’d been working too hard. She said he needed one night when nobody asked him to remember anything.”

Wataru stopped moving.

“What?” said Kagami.

“Nothing.” Wataru stared at the sleeping man. “That sounds like something a person who knew him would say.”

“Or someone who read his file,” said Kuze.

“Or someone who wrote it,” said Himeno.

The paramedic covered the cup and placed it in a paper bag. There was no dramatic evidence on the table, no broken lock, no overturned furniture. The flat looked exactly as it had looked before the woman came, except that Mr. Kurata was asleep and the room was no longer safe.

At the center of the saucer lay a folded card.

Wataru opened it carefully. The paper was cut from one of the festival’s wish slips, white with a faint gold fleck at the edge. On the front was a small pencil drawing of the Matron, her gray coat and brass lantern rendered in the same neat, upright strokes they had seen in Ren’s childhood sketches.

Beneath it, in Ren’s handwriting, were the words:

THE MATRON. SHE WILL NOT BE GOOD MUCH LONGER.

On the inside, in a second hand—smaller, cramped, unfamiliar—someone had added:

He did not draw the sister.

No one spoke for several seconds.

“Do we know that?” said Kagami.

“Know what?” asked Himeno.

“That he didn’t draw her.”

Kuze studied the two lines. “The first is a statement. The second is an objection.”

“An objection to what?”

“To the idea that every person here belongs to his drawing.” Kuze looked toward the sleeping teacher. “Someone wants us to know that there is at least one person he did not cast.”

“Or someone wants us to think that,” said Wataru.

Kagami took the card, but did not touch the writing. “Either way, the stranger knew the drawing was Ren’s.”

“And knew we would find it,” said Himeno.

The card was photographed, sealed, and set beside the cup. Mori gave his statement again, this time with every uncertainty written down. He did not defend himself. He did not ask them to understand. When he finished, he signed the report with a shaking hand.

---

Sato arrived before the ambulance left. He did not touch the cup. He asked who had moved it, who had covered it, and who had first noticed the card. Each answer was written in a separate line. The cup would go to the laboratory; the card would be photographed under proper light; Mori would be interviewed again at the station, with someone present who had not heard the first account.

“Do you think the tea did it?” Himeno asked.

“I think Mr. Kurata is asleep,” Sato replied. “I do not know why. Until the doctor tells us, everything else is a guess.”

It was not the answer she wanted. It was the first one that made the room feel less dangerous.

Mori sat on the stairwell landing while another officer took his place by the door. His hands were clasped between his knees. “I have known him for six years,” he said. “He used to bring us tea when the office ran late. He never asked anyone to stay awake with him. I thought the woman was doing the same thing.”

“Did she say anything only his family would know?” Wataru asked.

“No. She said he had been working too hard. That could be true of anybody.”

“Did she call him by his first name?”

Mori shook his head. “Sensei. She called him sensei.”

Kuze wrote that down. The woman had chosen the safest possible word: one that proved nothing, but made the lie feel familiar.

When they carried Kurata out, his glasses slipped from his face. Kagami caught them before they fell and folded them shut. For a moment he held them in his palm, small and ordinary, while the paramedics moved around him.

“Tomorrow,” Kagami said, “we do not protect a list. We protect each other’s decisions.”

“Meaning?” Himeno asked.

“Meaning no one decides alone that a stranger belongs here.”

At the door, Wataru looked back once. Mr. Kurata slept on, his breath even, his face heavy with the exhaustion of a man who had spent too long watching other people.

“Ten people,” Kagami said quietly. “And she walked straight through the guard.”

“Then the list was never the protection,” said Kuze.

“What was?”

“The belief that we knew who was coming.”

Outside, the festival lanterns trembled in the wind. Beyond the town, Aster’s clocks continued toward midnight.

Kagami waited until the ambulance had turned down the hill before he let anyone leave the landing. He gave each person one task: Wataru would stay with Hoshina; Himeno would tell the volunteers that Kurata was ill, without repeating the unconfirmed details; Kuze would keep the card and the list separate. No one was to take a copy home.

“You’re dividing the evidence from the people,” Himeno said.

“I’m trying to stop us from turning every person into evidence.”

She studied him. “That sounded like something Ren would say.”

“It sounded like something I needed to hear.”

For once, she had no quick answer.

# Chapter 57: The Number After Ten

At nine o’clock, Kagami wrote eleven names on the board.

He did not use the index cards. He wrote them in a column, large and plain, then added a twelfth line and left it blank.

The meeting room smelled of wet coats and burnt tea. Wataru stood by the window. Himeno sat with her arms folded. Kuze had placed the two versions of the card side by side on the table: the Matron’s portrait and the stranger’s sentence. Hoshina Aya had brought her old folder of Lantern House records, and kept one hand on it as if the paper might walk away.

“Mr. Kurata is still one of the ten we agreed to protect,” Kagami said. “He was not left off the list. The failure was that we treated the word ‘guarded’ as if it meant ‘safe.’”

“Then add Mori,” said Himeno.

“I have.” Kagami pointed to the eleventh name. “Not because I think the woman will go after him. Because she has already learned how to make him open a door.”

Mori had been ordered to stay at the station and give no more unsupervised reports. He had agreed without protest. Kagami did not know if that meant he was ashamed or afraid. He suspected both.

He drew a line between Mori and the blank. Then he rubbed it out.

“Don’t make a chart yet,” Kuze said. “We don’t know whether Mori was careless, manipulated, or deliberately chosen because he would be blamed.”

“Those are three different explanations,” said Himeno.

“And they lead to three different people,” said Kuze. “If we choose one before the evidence arrives, we will start seeing it everywhere.”

Hoshina opened her folder again. The old list had been copied so many times that some letters were nearly gray. She showed them the paper’s edge, where the original staple holes had widened into tiny tears.

“I got this from a retired clerk,” she said. “She said it came from a placement file. She could not tell me who made the copy. She could not tell me why two lines were withheld. She kept it because she thought somebody would ask for it one day.”

“Did she say who?” asked Kagami.

“No. She said, ‘If they ask, I’ll know.’” Hoshina closed the folder. “She died before I could ask what that meant.”

The blank line on the board no longer looked like a dramatic mystery. It looked like a debt they had inherited without knowing the creditor.

Kagami turned back to his new list. “We will verify every arrival twice. One person checks the name; another checks the reason. No one borrows another person’s authority. If we cannot confirm someone, we keep the door closed and call them ourselves.”

“Even if they say it is urgent?” asked the student volunteer.

“Especially then.”

The boy nodded, then looked toward the hall, where the festival noise rose and fell. “What if they really need help?”

“Then we help them from the safe side of the door,” Kagami said. “We do not confuse kindness with letting somebody choose the rules.”

Himeno watched him write the rule at the bottom of the board. It was the first time he had sounded less like a detective and more like a person who had learned what a mistake cost.

“And the blank?” asked Wataru.

“The person we haven’t identified.”

“An empty line is not a person.”

“Neither is a disguise. We’re keeping the line until we know which one we’re dealing with.” Kagami capped the marker. “No one moves alone. No family member gets waved through because they sound familiar. Every visitor is checked by someone other than the person who lets them in. If that slows the festival, it slows the festival.”

“Good,” said Himeno. “And if the person refuses to be checked?”

“Then we let them refuse from outside the door.”

Hoshina opened the folder. “There are twelve Quiet Ones on this list. Ten names I could verify. One marked ‘Withheld.’ One marked ‘R.’”

“Could the withheld person be the sister?” asked Kuze.

“Could be.” She tapped the page. “Could be a clerk who lost a form, a person who was never placed, or a name removed to make somebody look more important. The list is a copy of a copy. I have never seen the original.”

“Then we have a theory, not an answer,” Kuze said.

Hoshina looked at him. “You sound disappointed.”

“I am relieved. Answers that arrive before the evidence usually belong to the person who wants us to have them.”

A knock sounded at the door. Everyone looked up at once.

It was a student volunteer with a green armband, carrying a paper bag from the tea house. He stopped when he saw the room’s faces.

“Sorry,” he said. “They sent extra cups. I was told to bring them here.”

“Who told you?” Kagami asked.

The student glanced down at the bag. “A woman at the gate. She said she was with the festival committee.”

“Did she give you a name?”

“No.”

“Did you check her armband?”

The boy’s face reddened. “I thought she was staff.”

Kagami took the bag from him. “You did the right thing bringing it here.”

He waited until the student had gone, then opened the bag. Ten paper cups. One was still warm. Beneath them lay a narrow strip of paper, blank except for a shallow crease down the middle.

“Is this a message?” Wataru asked.

Kuze held the strip up to the light. “It may be the place where one was removed.”

“Or it may just be a crease.”

“Yes.” Kuze set it down. “We should preserve it anyway.”

Himeno walked to the window. From there she could see the lawn, the lanterns, the clusters of visitors moving between the booths. The crowd had been thinner than the previous day. Now it had filled again, with parents and alumni and children holding paper masks. At the far end, a gray-coated woman stood beneath the gingko tree.

Himeno turned sharply.

The woman lifted one hand—not a wave, not quite—and disappeared behind a line of students.

“Did anyone else see her?”

Wataru joined her at the window. “I saw someone move.”

“You don’t know it was her.”

“No,” he said. “I don’t.”

Kagami came to stand between them. “We don’t chase a coat. We keep the doors covered.”

The three of them watched the crowd. The gray coat did not appear again.

Kuze closed the folder and wrote a new line in his notebook.

He asked Hoshina where she had obtained the photocopy and whether the retired clerk had ever mentioned a second copy. Hoshina said no. The clerk had kept it in a drawer, not a safe, and had never shown it to anyone at Aster. That did not make the document authentic. It only made its chain of custody shorter than they had feared.

“Can you get the clerk’s estate papers?” Kuze asked.

“I can ask her niece. I can’t promise she’ll help.”

“Don’t tell her what we think it means.”

Hoshina’s eyes narrowed. “I wasn’t going to.”

“I know. I wanted to say it aloud before the rest of us decide we’re certain.”

She tucked the folder beneath her arm. “You’re all very careful with certainty for people who keep collecting mysteries.”

“Collecting is safer than solving them badly.”

“Not for the people in the middle.”

“No,” Kuze said. “Not for them.”

A person can be made invisible by a uniform, a title, or a story everyone wants to believe.

He paused, then added:

The sister may be a story.

# Chapter 58: The Second Morning

At 9:29, Kagami told Ren everything.

He told him about the woman at Kurata’s flat, the cup, Mori’s mistake, the drawing, and the second hand beneath it. He described the missing visitor’s name and the empty line in Hoshina’s old list. He did not leave out the possibility that the woman had been the Matron.

Ren listened without interrupting.

They stood beneath the covered walk between the dormitory and the main building. Students passed behind them in pairs, carrying folded festival banners and boxes of paper cups. A cold wind moved along the glass roof.

When Kagami had finished, Ren said, “Thank you.”

“Why?”

“For telling me.”

“I tell you everything.”

“Yes.”

“You’re not going to deny you sent that note?”

“I did not write it.”

“You expect me to believe that.”

“I expect you to decide what you believe.” Ren adjusted the green armband on his sleeve. “The woman may have been the Matron. It would explain the coat. It would explain why she knew Mr. Kurata’s habits. It would not explain the second handwriting.”

“Could she have written it?”

“She could have.”

“Could you?”

“I could have.”

Kagami studied him. “You’re not helping.”

“I am answering.”

“You could answer in a way that helped.”

“That would be a different kind of answer.”

The bell rang in the main building. A group of first-years hurried past. One of them stumbled over the edge of a mat, and Ren caught the box she was carrying before it struck the ground. He handed it back, bowed, and returned his attention to Kagami.

“You made the first list,” Ren said. “Ten people.”

“I did.”

“You believed that knowing where they were would keep them safe.”

“I believed it would help.”

“It did. Mr. Kurata is alive.”

“He is asleep because someone reached him.”

“Those facts can both be true.”

Kagami’s smile was brief and without humor. “You’re very good at making a failure sound like a success.”

“I did not say it was a success.”

“No. You let me say it.”

Ren inclined his head. “That is fair.”

For a moment, they stood in the covered walk with the sound of the festival drifting through the school. Kagami looked at the clean, laminated schedule in his hand. It still showed every booth, every gate, every duty station. There was no place on it for a person who came as someone’s sister.

“I am changing the plan,” he said.

“What will you do?”

“Not tell you.”

Ren’s half-smile did not change. “That is a sensible choice.”

“You don’t sound disappointed.”

“I am not.”

“Why?”

“Because you have finally stopped trying to prove that honesty makes you predictable.” Ren stepped aside to let a pair of students pass. “It does not. It only makes you responsible for the choices you make.”

Kagami did not move away. “I was with you from the opening ceremony until the lights went out.”

“Yes.”

“You were never near Kurata’s flat. You didn’t leave the grounds. You didn’t send anyone away from my sight.”

“I do not remember leaving.”

“That is not the same as saying you didn’t.”

“No.”

Kagami searched his face for the small satisfaction he expected and found only the same attentive stillness. “If you had a second person working for you, I would have no way to know.”

“That is true.”

“And if the woman acted on her own, you may not know who she is either.”

“That is also true.”

The answer landed harder than a denial. Kagami had built the last three days around a man who seemed to know the shape of every room before he entered it. Ren had just admitted that there were rooms he could not see.

“Which is it?” Kagami asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Do you want to know?”

“Yes.”

Kagami looked down at his laminated festival map. “That sounded almost honest.”

“It was an honest answer.”

“Then I’ll have to decide what to do with it.”

Ren inclined his head. “That is your responsibility.”

A bell rang inside the main building. The students passing them began to move faster, glad for an excuse to stop listening. Kagami folded the map once, then again, until the marked routes disappeared inside it.

“Today’s guard plan isn’t on this sheet,” he said.

“I assumed not.”

“Does it bother you?”

“No.”

“Does it interest you?”

Ren paused for the length of a breath. “Yes.”

Kagami almost smiled. “Good. Then you’re not the only one with something to learn.”

Kagami watched him walk away, in the direction of the courtyard and the tea house. He did not look back.

---

At ten, the festival opened for its second day. Himeno had divided the volunteer rota into smaller groups. Nobody worked alone. Nobody went to fetch supplies without signing out. The security desk had a list of approved adults, and a second list of adults who were not to be admitted without direct confirmation.

The list did not contain a sister for Mr. Kurata.

At eleven, a girl from the tea house brought Kagami a cup of barley tea. She had not been assigned to his group. She had not been on the schedule.

“Who sent you?” he asked.

“Nobody. I made it.”

“Why?”

“You looked tired.”

He almost laughed. “That is not a safe reason to trust me.”

“I didn’t say I trusted you.” She set down the cup. “I said you looked tired.”

He watched her go. The tea was warm. He did not drink it until one of the other volunteers had tasted it first.

At 12:14, the station called. Mr. Kurata remained asleep, stable, and under observation. The doctor expected him to wake sometime Monday morning.

The doctor had not confirmed what had been in the tea. He had not confirmed that the tea had caused the sleep. He had not even confirmed that Kurata would wake on Monday. Kagami wrote each qualification into the margin, though the page was already crowded with times and names.

At the festival gate, the new checks delayed a group of parents by four minutes. One father complained that he had shown his invitation already. Himeno apologized, checked the name again, and let him in. He accepted the answer, then asked if the school was expecting trouble.

“Only the ordinary kind,” she said.

It was not a lie. It was not the truth he had asked for, either.

When he was gone, she looked at the visitor tags. Each number was written twice: once on the tag, once on a list kept behind the desk. A simple precaution. Not protection, not proof. A way to notice if one of the pieces vanished.

Kagami wrote the time on a fresh page. Beneath it, he wrote a second line.

A person can care and still be used.

Then he crossed out the word “used” and wrote “wrong.”

At 12:15, Kagami’s phone rang again. Himeno’s voice, usually steady, said, “The guest book has a page missing.”

# Chapter 59: The Guest Book

The festival guest book was kept in a red binder at the east gate.

It was a modest thing, a ledger of names and addresses and a box for the reason a visitor had come. Aster had kept one for as long as anyone could remember. On the first day, the pages had filled with parents, alumni, and people from the ministry. On the second, a volunteer noticed that a leaf had been torn out.

He brought it to Himeno.

“I didn’t do it,” he said quickly. “I was here all morning.”

“I know,” she said. “Show me.”

The torn edge was clean. The missing page had been near the back, where late arrivals signed. The next page held three names, a retired teacher, an alumnus, and a woman whose handwriting was so careful it looked printed.

Name: M. Kurata.

Relationship to student or staff: Sister.

Time of arrival: 9:18 p.m.

The entry had been made on Saturday night. The ink had dried before anyone noticed it. Beneath the name was a street address in the town, but the last two digits had been smudged by a thumb.

“Who checked her in?” Himeno asked.

The volunteer looked at the desk. “I was on duty until nine. Then Ms. Arai took over. She said a family member had come to see Mr. Kurata.”

“Did she see the woman?”

“I don’t know. She went to get the office key.”

Himeno closed the binder. “Bring Ms. Arai here.”

The teacher arrived ten minutes later. She studied the entry and did not touch the page.

“I didn’t write this,” she said.

“Did you see the woman?”

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you that she was his sister?”

“Officer Mori called. He said a relative had arrived and was going to bring Mr. Kurata tea. I left the guest book open because I thought it had been checked.”

“By whom?”

“By the officer.”

The relay had been simple. Mori trusted the woman. Ms. Arai trusted Mori. The volunteer trusted Ms. Arai. The name had passed through three people and none had verified it.

Wataru read the entry and frowned. “She came to the school first?”

“Or someone wrote that she did,” said Kuze.

---

The address led to a narrow street behind the station. Detective Sato sent an officer to check it. The building existed. The room number did not. The tenant named on the mailbox had moved out the previous spring.

“An address that almost belongs to someone,” said Hoshina. “A name that almost belongs to someone. The whole thing is made of almosts.”

Sato arrived with a clean evidence sleeve and asked them not to touch the binder again. He photographed the torn edge, the neighboring entries, and the place where the missing page had been. The tear ran through the perforations. Whoever removed it had not needed a knife; they had needed only to know where the book was kept and when the desk would be unattended.

“Can we recover the writing?” Himeno asked.

“Possibly. We can examine pressure marks on the page beneath it.” Sato closed the sleeve. “That may tell us whether the missing sheet was filled in here. It will not tell us who wrote it.”

“And the address?”

“I sent someone. It is a real building. The room number is not.”

Hoshina leaned back. “Someone made a lie that survives the first question.”

“Most lies do,” said Sato. “The trouble is how many questions you can afford to ask before the person gets tired and stops answering.”

Kuze looked at the entry again. The pen strokes were steady, but the letters leaned forward. He compared them to the three names above and saw no obvious match. That did not mean there was no match. It only meant his eyes wanted one.

“Could the woman have signed herself in?” asked Kagami.

“She could,” said Sato. “Could someone else have written it? Also possible. Could it be a planted entry meant to send us to a false address? Yes.” He put the binder away. “The only confirmed fact is that the page is missing.”

They copied the visitor list from the previous day and checked every name against the school’s own invitations. Two alumni had arrived without replying. One parent had brought a guest. A magazine photographer had signed in under a shortened surname. All three were locatable. None had visited Kurata.

The missing entry sat between the last verified guest and the closing signature of the volunteer.

They checked the desk drawer, the supply cabinet, and the wastebasket. The missing sheet was not there. They asked the cleaning staff whether anyone had been in the room after closing. One remembered a person in a gray coat near the east corridor, but could not say whether it had been a woman or a man. The memory had come only after the description was offered, so Sato marked it as unreliable.

“Could the guest book have been altered before the festival?” Wataru asked.

“Could be,” said Sato. “Could be the entry was made after the page was torn out. Could be there was never a valid entry at all.”

“Then why write it?”

“To make us look for the sister. Or to make us doubt the record. Or because the person wanted us to know the sister’s name.” Sato slid the binder into its sleeve. “Those are three different motives. We have no reason to choose among them yet.”

Himeno glanced toward the gate. A volunteer was explaining the new tag system to a child who could not read the number. The child held still while the tag was pinned to his coat, solemn as a witness.

“Whatever the motive,” she said, “the next person who claims to be family gets checked.”

At the gate, the volunteer who had been on duty looked at the empty space and began to cry. She had not torn the page. She had not seen the woman. She had only left the binder open while she fetched a tag.

Himeno sat beside her until she could breathe again. She did not tell her it was all right. It was not. She told her that the report would say exactly what had happened, and that no one would make her carry the whole mistake by herself.

When they returned to the meeting room, Kuze added one sentence to his notes: A system that depends on one careful person is not a system. It is a person waiting to be tired.

Kuze copied the entry into his notebook. “That is what makes it difficult to disprove. Every piece is plausible by itself.”

“Then we stop treating plausibility as proof,” Himeno said.

At three, Kagami announced a change to the festival schedule. No one under eighteen would be sent off the grounds alone. The team at the east gate would work in pairs. The guest book would be replaced, and every visitor would receive a numbered paper tag.

A few people complained. Kagami listened to each of them and did not change the rule.

At five, a man in a ministry coat arrived with no invitation and no tag. He said he had been expected. The gate volunteer asked for his name. The man gave it. The volunteer checked the approved list and found nothing.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I can’t let you in.”

The man looked past her, toward the school. His eyes found Ren at the tea house, across the lawn. For a second the crowd seemed to thin between them.

Then he smiled, handed over a business card, and left.

No one chased him.

Ren walked to the gate after the man had turned down the hill. He picked up the card the volunteer had been given and read it.

“What did he want?” Kagami asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Did you know him?”

“No.”

“Did he know you?”

“Possibly.” Ren handed the card back. “He knew where to look.”

The card carried the name of a foundation that had funded Aster’s renovations years before. The phone number had been disconnected. On the back, in blue ink, was a single small mark: eleven short strokes, drawn in a row.

Hoshina stared at it. “That isn’t a name.”

“No,” said Kuze. “It’s a count.”

At the bottom of his page, he wrote:

Someone is asking us to count the people we cannot see.

# Chapter 60: The Empty Line

At the end of the second day, the festival had made more money than it had on the first.

Class 1-D’s pancake queue had reached the road. The puzzle house had collected enough to replace the broken desks in the back row. The tea house at the gingko tree had earned almost nothing, which Ren said was a success, and Himeno told him that was a dishonest use of arithmetic.

“You said you did not want to win,” she said.

“I don’t.”

“You keep making the quietest booth on the grounds. People pay more when they feel guilty for sitting in silence.”

“That is a possibility.”

“Did you plan that?”

“I planned the tea.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No,” Ren said. “It is not.”

She watched him pour. He had not once looked at the guest book or the visitor tag on his own sleeve. The man from the foundation had not returned. The eleven strokes on the back of the card had been copied by the police and left in a sealed envelope.

“You’re not going to ask me what I think they mean?” she said.

“I know what you think.”

“What do I think?”

“That they count the Quiet Ones.”

“And you?”

“I think there are eleven strokes.”

She set down her cup. “You do that deliberately.”

“Do what?”

“Give people the smallest possible truth and let them build the dangerous part themselves.”

“I find it is often more accurate.”

“Do you ever wish you were wrong?”

Ren looked at her. The lantern light moved across his face, leaving the same expression it had found there. “That would depend on what I was wrong about.”

Himeno stood. “Good night, Aizawa-kun.”

“Good night, Himeno-san.”

She walked away without looking back. Ren set the kettle down and folded the tea cloth once, then again, into a square.

---

At 10:40, Kagami assembled the guards in the conference room. He asked each person to report what they had seen, not what they believed it meant. The report took thirty minutes. No one had been approached. No one had left the grounds alone. The guest book had not been touched. The man from the ministry had not returned.

Mr. Kurata remained asleep at the hospital.

“Tomorrow is the final day,” Kagami said. “We do not assume the pattern repeats. We do not assume it changes. We watch what happens.”

“Ten people,” said Wataru. “And one empty line.”

“Eleven strokes,” said Hoshina.

“An unverified sister,” said Himeno.

“A name withheld,” said Kuze.

They all looked at Ren. He sat at the end of the table with his hands folded and his face quiet.

“What do you think?” asked Kagami.

“I think you have four separate facts,” said Ren. “You have not yet earned the right to call them one mystery.”

Kagami’s expression tightened. “And if they are one?”

“Then someone has already decided what answer they want you to find.”

Nobody answered. Outside, the last lanterns were being lowered from the trees. One by one the colored lights went dark, leaving the school’s windows bright against the sea.

Kuze opened his notebook and looked at the list he had been keeping since April. On the first page he had written every clue he could prove. On the back page, the one he never showed anyone, he kept a list of the names of people he could not yet account for.

He added one more line.

The sister.

Below it, he wrote the number eleven, then drew a box around it.

At 11:11, a knock sounded on the conference-room door.

No one moved.

The knock came again, two taps and a pause.

Kagami gestured to the nearest guard. The door opened.

A girl stood in the corridor. She was perhaps fifteen. Her hair was cut close to her jaw, and her school uniform was old-fashioned, with a narrow gray collar and no crest. She held a folded paper lantern in both hands.

“I’m looking for Mr. Kuze,” she said.

Kuze rose. “I’m Kuze.”

She looked past him at Ren.

“No,” she said. “Not him.”

The room became still.

The girl’s voice had been calm, but her fingers pressed hard into the folded paper lantern. Kuze noticed that the school uniform had no crest and that its cuffs were frayed in the same places on both sleeves. He noticed these things because he did not know what else to ask.

“Where did you get the lantern?” he said.

“From the festival.”

“Which booth?”

She looked down at it as if surprised to find it in her hands. “The one by the old wall.”

“There isn’t a booth by the old wall,” said Wataru.

The girl’s gaze returned to Kuze. “You know where it is.”

He did not answer. The old wall ran behind the closed archive building, beyond the public part of the grounds. It was not a place visitors were directed to. A student could still reach it by taking the north stairs and crossing the covered walk.

“Who told you to find me?” Kuze asked.

“No one.”

“Who wrote the message?”

She tightened her grip on the lantern. “I don’t know.”

This time, the answer sounded less like a riddle and more like fear.

The guard took one step into the corridor. “Should I follow?”

“Not alone,” said Kagami. He looked at the door, then at the volunteer who had opened it. “You stay here. Tell us exactly what she looked like and which way she went.”

“She went toward the stairs.”

“Which stairs?”

“The north ones. I think.”

Kagami and Wataru moved together. They reached the landing in less than a minute. The stairwell was empty. A side door on the next level stood ajar, opening onto the covered walkway. Beyond it, students crossed between buildings, their green armbands bright in the afternoon light.

No one could say they had seen the girl leave.

“Did she go outside?” Kagami asked the guard.

“I don’t know.”

“Did she pass you?”

“I don’t think so.”

“Those are different answers.”

The guard swallowed. “I didn’t see her.”

They did not search every classroom. They did not stop the festival. Kagami sent two people to the gate and one to the library annex, then returned to the room. A search of the side corridor found no dropped paper, no missing tag, and no open window. The girl had left the same way any student could have left: by walking through a school full of people who were busy watching one another.

Himeno looked at the folded lantern. “Did you know her?”

“No,” Kuze said.

“Did she know you?”

“She knew my name.”

“That doesn’t answer it.”

“No,” he said. “It doesn’t.”

He held the lantern carefully by its wire handle. The message faced inward, protected by the paper. Someone had written it for him, not for Ren. That distinction felt important. It also felt like bait.

“Don’t open it yet,” said Wataru.

“I already read the line.”

“Then don’t read it again.”

Kuze put the lantern on the table. For a moment, nobody touched it. The paper was ordinary, the fold slightly crooked. On one corner was a faint smear of blue ink, the same color as the foundation card from the previous evening.

It might have meant something. It might have been from a school pen.

Kuze did not write either possibility down. He waited until Sato arrived to photograph it, and wrote only the time the girl had appeared and the time she was no longer there.

The empty line on the board remained blank.

The girl held out the lantern to Kuze. On its white paper side, in a small, neat hand, was a single line:

The eleventh has a name.

Kuze looked up.

The girl had already turned away. She walked down the corridor at an ordinary pace, and the guard went after her, and the hallway swallowed both of them before anyone could ask the question that mattered.

“Did anyone see her come in?” Kagami said.

Nobody answered.

Ren lowered his eyes to the empty line on the board.

For the first time that day, the tea in his cup had gone cold.
