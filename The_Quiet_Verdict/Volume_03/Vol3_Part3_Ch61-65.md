# Chapter 61: The Wall Behind the Archive

At 7:08 on Monday morning, Kuze Minato returned to the place the girl had named.

The old wall ran behind Aster’s archive building, where the grounds narrowed between a line of pine trees and the slope down to the water. It had been built from gray stone, long before the school’s bright windows and trimmed paths. Moss filled the joints. A small brass lamp hung over the locked service door, its glass clouded with age.

There was no festival booth there.

Wataru came behind him with two paper cups of coffee. Detective Sato followed at a distance, accompanied by a uniformed officer. They had agreed not to enter the archive or touch anything without permission. The girl had said the lantern came from the festival. She had not said she found it at the wall.

“Maybe she meant the lantern booth,” Wataru said. “The one nearest the old wall.”

“There isn’t one.”

“Maybe there was.”

“Yesterday?”

“I’m trying to be helpful.”

“You’re trying to make the sentence mean something we can use.”

Wataru handed him the coffee. “You’re welcome.”

They inspected the path. The soil was packed with footprints from the weekend, too many to separate. The service door was locked. A length of twine, caught in the hinge, might have come from a festival tag or from one of the gardeners’ bundles. Sato photographed it. No one called it evidence of the girl.

Near the end of the wall, a new paper lantern hung from a nail. It was plain white, with no wish slip or school crest. The bottom had been folded inward and tied with a thread of blue cotton.

Wataru stopped short. “That wasn’t here last night.”

“Were you here last night?” Kuze asked.

“No.”

“Then you don’t know.”

Sato called the groundskeeper. He arrived in a work jacket, wiping his hands on a rag, and said the lantern had not been there when he closed the maintenance shed at six. He did not know who had put it up. He had not checked the wall after dark.

Sato photographed the lantern before removing it. Inside was a rectangle cut from a guest-book page. The left edge bore the same small perforations as the school’s red binder. The ink showed only two marks: a vertical line and, beside it, a shorter stroke.

“Eleven?” Wataru asked.

“Or a one and a mistake,” said Kuze.

Sato held the fragment by its corners. “Or part of a letter. We’ll compare the paper with the binder.”

Kuze looked at the wall. It gave no sign of what had been written there, or who had chosen this place. The note on the lantern belonged to him only because the girl had said his name. The missing visitor’s page belonged to the school only because someone had torn it out. Their edges did not meet.

“Don’t put them together yet,” Wataru said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were looking at them like they were going to introduce themselves.”

Kuze took out his notebook. He recorded the time, the location, and the fact that the paper had been collected. He left a space beneath it for the result of Sato’s comparison.

---

At ten, the festival opened for its final day. Kagami had ordered the north side of the grounds checked before the first visitors arrived. The service door remained locked. The white lantern was sealed in an evidence bag. The guest book was replaced with a new binder, its pages numbered in advance.

“Every visitor gets a tag,” Kagami told the volunteers. “Every tag gets a second mark on the copy. If either number is missing, you report it. You don’t fill in the gap yourself.”

The volunteer from the previous day stood beside Himeno. She had asked to return to the desk.

“Are you sure?” Kagami asked.

“No,” she said. “But I don’t want the person who tore the page to decide where I can stand.”

He gave her a small nod and assigned another volunteer to work beside her. Himeno checked the list, then looked at the girl’s hands. They were still shaking, but she had placed the new binder squarely on the table.

By noon, the festival had found its noise again. The pancake queue turned past the tennis court. The old clock tower rang three minutes late. Children carried paper masks between the booths, and a boy from 1-D chased a stack of menus that had come loose in the wind.

Kuze did not see the girl from the night before.

The previous evening’s dance had ended without incident. The lanterns had gone out one row at a time, and Kagami’s list of names had been checked twice before the students were sent to their dormitories. The paper slip that fell onto Kuze’s notebook was the only thing left unexplained.

At breakfast, the school had returned to its weekday manners. Students queued for rice and miso soup. Volunteers wore their green armbands over sweaters. The festival banners, still tied to the railings, stirred in the morning wind. From the dining hall windows, the old archive wall was just visible between the pine trunks.

Wataru leaned over Kuze’s shoulder. “You’re looking at it again.”

“I’m looking at the path.”

“That’s what I said.”

Kuze shut the notebook. “The message was meant to make me look.”

“Then don’t.”

“I already have.”

Wataru didn’t smile. “You can still choose what you do next.”

Kuze put the notebook away. He finished his tea before returning to the grounds.

At 7:08, the office clerk arrived with the archive key register. She confirmed that the headmistress’s copy had not been checked out, but she had not seen the key since Friday. The last person to sign for the clerk’s copy was still on the grounds, helping prepare the closing program.

Sato asked to speak with him. The man showed them the key on his ring and the maintenance slip for the work he had completed. He had not entered the archive. The record was consistent; it was not an alibi for anyone else.

Kuze asked whether the paper lantern could have been made at the festival booth. The student who ran the lantern station said no. Their lanterns were larger, printed with the school crest, and folded with a reinforcing strip at the base. The one delivered to Kuze had no crest and thinner paper.

“Then it came from somewhere else,” Wataru said.

“Or someone made it somewhere else.”

“That sounds like the same thing.”

“It isn’t. We don’t know where the paper came from.”

He wrote down the student’s description. The lantern, the thread, and the guest-book fragment might have been connected. They might have been chosen because they looked connected. Until the paper analysis came back, both readings remained open.

At the archive wall, a fresh strip of blue thread appeared on the nail where the lantern had hung.

Kuze found it on his way back from the gate. It was tied in a flat knot, too small to have caught by accident, and no longer than his thumb. He called Sato rather than taking it down. While they waited, he asked the groundskeeper whether anyone else had a key to the service door.

“The headmistress, the archive clerk, and me,” the man said. “The clerk’s key is in the office. Mine stays in the maintenance shed. The headmistress keeps hers.”

“Can you show us the record?” Sato asked.

The groundskeeper unlocked a cabinet and produced a logbook. Each person had signed when borrowing a key. The most recent entry was dated three weeks earlier: archive clerk, inventory, returned at 4:12. No one had signed for the headmistress’s key.

“Does that prove it wasn’t used?” Wataru asked.

“No,” said Sato. “It proves nobody recorded it.”

The distinction stayed with Kuze. They photographed the knot, bagged the thread, and left the service door closed. A record could show what people admitted doing. It could not show what they had done in secret.

# Chapter 62: A Margin of Fifty-Three

---

At one o’clock, Class 1-D moved into first place.

The score was announced from the steps of the great hall. The school had counted the festival proceeds through the second day, then added the morning’s receipts. Class 1-D had passed Class 1-A by fifty-three points.

For a second, nobody cheered. The number was too small to understand. Then Rina shouted, and the students at the ticket booth answered her, and the applause went through the lawn in a wave that surprised even the people making it.

Dojima lifted the pancake griddle above his head. The metal spatula slipped from his hand and clattered across the stage. A first-year caught it. The laughter loosened the crowd.

Himeno let herself smile once. “You have a very dignified victory.”

“We’re still counting,” said Kagami.

“Let them enjoy it for thirty seconds.”

“We have forty-seven minutes before the next tally.”

“Then they can enjoy it for forty-seven minutes.”

At the edge of the steps, Ren stood with the festival schedule folded in his hand. He did not applaud. He did not look toward the other class’s booth. He waited until the cheer faded, then told the student announcer to repeat the rule about the fund-paid prize: every class retained at least ninety percent of its pool, and no class would lose points because another had earned more.

The announcement drew a few groans from Class 1-A, who had expected to be ahead. It also stopped the 1-D students from turning the result into a victory over them.

“Did you plan the margin?” Himeno asked Ren.

“What margin?”

“Fifty-three points. Close enough to keep everyone leaning forward. Small enough that one bad hour changes the result.”

“I didn’t choose the number.”

“You chose the contest.”

“I changed the prize.”

“After the festival opened.”

“Yes.”

“And you made a quiet booth that earns money from people feeling guilty about sitting in silence.”

“It has tea.”

“That is not a defense.”

“No,” said Ren. “It is a description.”

Himeno looked beyond him, where the volunteers were already rewriting the chalkboard with the new total. “The class is proud. Don’t use that to make them careless.”

“I won’t.”

“Don’t tell me you can promise that.”

“I can promise what I choose to do.”

She held his gaze. His expression did not change. It never changed enough to tell her when he thought she had found something important.

Before she could answer, a girl from the tea booth approached with a small tray. Three cups, one for each of them. She had tied her green armband so tightly that the skin beneath it had gone pink.

“Who gave you these?” Himeno asked.

“I poured them.”

“Who told you to bring them?”

“No one.”

“Then why?”

The girl glanced at the crowd. “The kettle was full. I thought people would be thirsty.”

Himeno turned to Ren. “You have trained them to expect that every ordinary gesture is a test.”

“I have trained no one.”

“Not directly.”

The girl began to take the tray away. Himeno stopped her with a light touch on the rim. “Thank you. Leave them here. We’ll choose for ourselves.”

She took a cup, checked its lid, and drank. Kagami followed. Ren did not touch his.

The girl waited. “Is something wrong?”

“No,” Himeno said. “We are deciding.”

The girl nodded. She had nothing to do with the stranger at Kurata’s flat. She had nothing to do with the missing guest-book page. She was still made to stand there while three people examined the cup she had carried.

Kagami set his down. “We can’t make caution feel like an accusation.”

“No,” Himeno said. “But we can make the rules clear enough that the girl doesn’t have to prove she deserves trust.”

Ren picked up his own cup. He took one sip, set it down, and said, “The next tally is at two.”

The girl left with the empty tray.

At the booth, Class 1-D’s students had put the new score on a sheet of paper and passed it around. Several names were written beside it: the people who had worked the night shift, the ones who had cleaned the griddle, the first-years who had sold the last of the menus. It was an ordinary list, with no blank spaces.

Kuze copied the number into his notebook. Beneath it he wrote: A lead of fifty-three is a fact. A victory is a prediction.

The next tally would be at two.

At one-fifty-eight, the score board was turned to face the lawn. At two, the committee clerk opened the envelope.

Class 1-D had lost the lead by eleven.

The result landed badly, though no one argued with the count. One student took the chalk and wrote the new totals beneath the first. Another checked the envelope seal and the committee signatures. The figures matched the posted receipts.

“They didn’t take points from us,” said a first-year. “The fund still gives us the guarantee.”

“That was never the question,” said Himeno. “We wanted to win.”

The girl looked down at her apron. “I thought we were winning.”

“We were. Then they sold more.” Himeno reached for the chalk and added the time beneath the numbers. “The count changed. That doesn’t make the work you did disappear.”

Across the lawn, Class 1-A’s booth was busy. Their students were selling the last of their paper flowers, not celebrating. One of them gave the 1-D pancake stand a box of fresh batter when they saw the empty bucket.

Dojima stared at it. “Are we accepting this?”

“Unless you’d rather throw away the next hour,” Himeno said.

He took the box. The two classes worked side by side until the next tally.

Himeno asked the committee clerk to show both class representatives the receipts behind the total. They went through the envelope one by one: cash, ticket stubs, refunds, and the time each box had been sealed. The 1-D representative made a mark beside each figure. The 1-A representative checked the sums on a pocket calculator.

They found no missing money. They did find a late receipt from the tea stall, entered under the wrong class number and corrected before the next count. The adjustment had moved the totals by eleven points.

“Was that the whole swing?” Kuze asked.

“No,” said the clerk. “The rest came from sales.”

“Can we see the receipts?”

“You just did.”

The papers were ordinary, the handwriting varied, the arithmetic sound. The school had not hidden a number in the ledger. That did not make the contest impossible to influence; it only meant this particular change had an explanation.

Himeno thanked the clerk and signed the page. The two representatives signed beneath her. The first-year who had worried about the count watched the signatures dry.

“I still don’t like losing,” she said.

“Neither do I,” Himeno answered. “That’s why we check the count instead of pretending it doesn’t matter.”

# Chapter 63: The Price of an Answer

---

Himeno found Ren in the old gym, where the festival committee had stacked the folding chairs for the evening dance.

He was alone except for a student volunteer tying a frayed curtain cord. A paper list of the evening’s music lay on a crate. Ren was checking the names against the final schedule, a pencil held lightly between his fingers.

“You knew the score would change,” Himeno said.

“I knew it could.”

“You didn’t tell them.”

“It wasn’t certain.”

“Neither was the lead.”

“Correct.”

She crossed the floor. “You have an answer for every part of the sentence and none for the question.”

“Then ask the question.”

“Did you arrange the contest so 1-D would be close enough to win that they would become a target?”

Ren set the pencil down. “No.”

“Did you move the score announcements to keep people watching the board?”

“No.”

“Did you bring Kagami into the festival committee because you wanted him looking at you instead of the gate?”

“I didn’t bring Kagami into the committee.”

“He volunteered to watch you.”

“Yes.”

“And you let him.”

“I did.”

Himeno looked at the chairs. Each row had been arranged with enough space for people to pass between them. “That’s what you do. You don’t need to push if someone will choose the direction that helps you.”

“Sometimes.”

“Do you hear how convenient that sounds?”

“I hear what you said.”

She picked up the schedule. “The girl last night asked for Kuze. Not you.”

“Yes.”

“Did that surprise you?”

“I don’t know her.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Ren looked at the paper in her hand. “No.”

Himeno placed it back on the crate. “You expect us to treat every unknown as a separate fact. You say we haven’t earned the right to connect them.”

“We haven’t.”

“Maybe that’s true. Maybe it’s how you keep people from seeing the pattern until you’re ready to show it.”

“Those possibilities are not mutually exclusive.”

She gave a short, disbelieving laugh. “That’s the worst answer you could have chosen.”

“It’s still accurate.”

The volunteer at the curtain had stopped tying the cord. Himeno glanced toward him. “Would you give us a moment?”

He left without asking why.

“You told Kagami the Mechanism has rules,” she said. “You let him believe that knowing the rules gives him a way to protect people.”

“The rules are real.”

“Rules don’t protect anyone if the person using them understands more than the people watching.”

“No.”

“What does protect them?”

“People who notice when the rules stop being enough.”

“Then stop making everyone guess whether you’re the danger or the warning.”

Ren did not answer at once. Outside the gym, the festival announcer called for the next volunteer shift. A door opened and closed. Someone laughed in the corridor.

“I can’t do that,” Ren said.

“Can’t or won’t?”

“I can’t make you believe something you don’t.”

“And you won’t tell me what you know.”

“I’ve told you what I know.”

“You know more than you say.”

“Yes.”

The admission was quiet, offered without challenge. It made Himeno’s next sentence harder to choose.

“Then here’s what I know,” she said. “I’m taking the 1-D committee off your schedule. We’ll run our own booth and keep our own count. You don’t get to decide where our students stand.”

Ren nodded. “That’s your choice.”

“It is.”

“If the committee needs a copy of the gate plan—”

“We’ll ask for it.”

She turned toward the door. The student volunteer came back in and resumed tying the cord where he had left it. Ren picked up the pencil again.

“Himeno-san,” he said.

She stopped.

“The final tally is at six. If 1-D wins, they will ask you to speak.”

“Is that a warning?”

“It’s on the program.”

“That is not an answer either.”

“No,” he said. “It isn’t.”

In the corridor, Himeno found Kagami waiting with a clipboard. She told him what she had changed. He did not ask whether she trusted Ren. He only checked that the new roster had two names beside every station.

“Why did you move your students?” he asked.

“Because they should know where their work goes. They shouldn’t have to ask Ren.”

“He wasn’t keeping them from the count.”

“He didn’t have to. They kept waiting for him to tell them what the count meant.”

Kagami checked the updated roster. “They need a reason to follow their own judgment.”

“They have one. They did the work.”

The two of them walked through the booths. At the pancake stand, Dojima was writing down each sale before putting the money in the cash box. At the puzzle house, Rina had added a second exit route to the sign. Neither had asked Ren to approve it.

“Better,” Himeno said.

“Safer?”

“More theirs.”

Kagami considered the answer, then marked the roster. “I can work with that.”

At six, the final tally showed Class 1-D in second place by twenty-nine points.

The students cheered anyway.

After the announcement, the committee clerk posted the signed ledger in the great hall. Kagami asked the class representatives from 1-A and 1-D to check it together. They compared each receipt, each refund, and each transfer to the festival fund. No one found a missing amount. No one claimed a point had gone to the wrong class.

When they finished, Kagami thanked them and stepped away. He did not ask them to be satisfied. The result had been fair; it did not have to feel good.

Himeno found the first-year who had worried about the eleven-point swing. The girl was helping carry the batter box back to the booth.

“Do you still think someone changed the number?” Himeno asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Good. Keep the uncertainty until we have something better.”

The girl nodded. “That’s what Kuze-kun says.”

“Then today he was right.”

Himeno looked toward the stage. The festival’s final program would begin after the booths closed. The banquet guests were already arriving at the main building, where staff had set out the place cards.

At the edge of the great hall, the treasurer collected the final cash boxes with two class representatives watching. Each box was counted, sealed, and signed for. The school’s guarantee meant no class would lose points to another, but the students wanted the proceeds entered correctly all the same.

Himeno stayed until the last receipt was copied. When a student asked whether Ren had approved the procedure, she said the headmistress had approved it weeks ago and the committee had agreed to follow it.

“Then why did everyone ask him?” the student said.

“Because he was standing where they could see him.”

“And that matters?”

“It shouldn’t matter more than the rules.” Himeno closed the ledger. “That’s why we keep the rules written down.”

The student nodded and carried the sealed box to the office. The path to the banquet hall was clear, every entrance staffed, every visitor tag checked. The school looked more secure than it had on Saturday.

Himeno still counted the names twice.

“Go help with the chairs,” she told the student. “And don’t let anyone tell you that coming second means you failed.”

# Chapter 64: The Final Performance

---

The final performance began at 7:30 p.m. in the old gym.

It was not a dance. The students had moved the folding chairs to the walls and strung paper lanterns from the basketball hoops. Someone had borrowed a record player from the music room. The committee had prepared a short program of songs and readings to close the festival. The dance had taken place the previous evening; this was the last performance before the banquet.

Class 1-D entered together. They had finished second, behind Class 1-A, and the narrow loss had not spoiled their mood. One of the first-years had brought a sheet cake. Rina cut it with the confidence of a surgeon and distributed the pieces before anyone could object.

The first act was a pair of students reading festival wishes aloud. They had covered the names, but left the handwriting visible. One note asked for a quieter dormitory. Another asked that a grandfather’s cough get better. A third, written in a child’s careful block letters, said only: I hope my mother comes next year.

The reader paused at that one. The room did not laugh or clap. The student folded the slip and placed it in a wooden box at the edge of the stage.

Then Dojima played a short piece on the school’s battered upright piano. He had never told anyone he could play. His hands were too large for the keys, and he stumbled at the start, but he kept going. When he finished, the applause came from every side of the room.

“You didn’t put him on the program,” Kagami said.

“I didn’t know,” said the committee clerk.

Dojima gave a small bow and stepped down. Rina met him at the foot of the stage and handed him a plate of cake. He took it with both hands, still looking a little surprised that anyone had heard him.

Kuze made a note of the song’s title, then crossed it out. He had written the wrong one. He asked Dojima afterward and corrected the line, carefully, without making a show of it.

Kagami stood near the double doors, watching the guests arrive. His clipboard had been replaced by a paper cup of water. He had not drunk it.

“You’re allowed to sit down,” Wataru said.

“I’m working.”

“You’re standing by the door.”

“That is where the work is.”

“What work?”

“Making sure no one leaves alone.”

Wataru looked at the crowded floor. “There are forty people in the room.”

“And one door.”

“Two.”

“The side door is for staff.”

“Then there are two doors and one rule.”

Kagami finally smiled. Wataru pointed to two empty chairs beside the wall.

“Ten minutes,” he said.

“I don’t have ten minutes.”

“You have a cup of water you haven’t touched.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“It is if you’re sitting down while you drink it.”

A student took the stage and began a quiet song on the piano. The first few notes came out uncertain, then steadied. Kagami watched the doors for the length of the piece. Wataru sat beside him without speaking. When it ended, Kagami stood again, but he finished the water before he returned to his post.

Across the room, Kuze sat near the wall with his notebook open on his knee. He was recording the names of the songs, not the people watching. A girl from the first-year class offered him a slice of cake. He took it, thanked her, and returned to the page.

“Put that away,” she said.

“I’m keeping a record.”

“Of a song?”

“Of the event.”

“Then record that you ate the cake.”

He looked at the paper plate in his hand. A spot of icing had landed on the corner of his notebook. He moved the plate farther from it.

“I’ll note the damage.”

The girl laughed. “You’re impossible.”

“People keep telling me that.”

Kuze closed the notebook and set it on the chair beside him. He ate the cake while the next song played. For the length of that song, nobody asked him what the girl meant by the eleventh.

At the far end of the gym, Ren stood by the music table. Himeno had removed the 1-D volunteers from his rota, and he had not tried to put them back. He checked the speakers, found the loose cable, and secured it with tape. When the student in charge thanked him, Ren bowed and returned to the edge of the room.

Himeno approached him. “You’ve been demoted from festival chair to electrician.”

“The cable was loose.”

“You could have left it for the music committee.”

“They were onstage.”

“Were you going to?”

“No.”

She watched his face. “You’re allowed to have a preference.”

“I do.”

“Which is?”

“To make sure the speakers work.”

“That isn’t what I meant.”

“I know.”

He held the cable in place while she wrapped the tape around it. Their fingers did not touch. Across the floor, Kagami and Wataru nearly collided with a pair of first-years. All four of them laughed, and the room seemed, for a moment, to belong to the students and not to the people counting exits.

At 8:14 p.m., the lights dimmed for the final song. The lanterns glowed in the dark. A paper wish slip, cut loose from one of the strings, drifted down and landed on the chair where Kuze had left his notebook.

He opened it.

The handwriting was cramped and unfamiliar. It said only:

You already know the name.

Kuze looked around the gym. Every student was in view. No one was watching him.

The final song ended. He folded the wish slip and put it between the pages of his notebook.

# Chapter 65: The Banquet List

---

The banquet was scheduled for nine o’clock Monday evening.

It was Aster’s tradition to close the festival with a meal for the committee, the headmistress, the visiting alumni, and the people who had funded the school’s rebuilding. The room was the long hall above the kitchen, with windows facing the sea and a low stage at the western end. The tables were set in three rows. The place cards had been written by hand.

Kuze received the seating plan from the committee clerk at 8:26 p.m.

He looked first for his own name. It was at the second table, between Wataru and a retired teacher. Kagami had been seated near the headmistress. Himeno was beside the festival treasurer. Ren had been placed at the end of the first table, where the donors could see him.

There were thirty-one names.

There were thirty-two chairs.

“Who is the extra seat for?” Kuze asked.

The clerk checked the list. “There are thirty-two guests.”

“I count thirty-one names.”

“Then someone hasn’t given me their name yet.”

“Who?”

The clerk glanced toward the hall. “The headmistress said there would be a late guest.”

“Did she give you a name?”

“She said to leave the card blank.”

Kuze looked at the empty place card. It had been set at the end of the third table, away from the headmistress and the donors. The paper was new. No crease, no ink, no mark. It could have been a simple oversight. It could also have been the only place in the room where nobody had been counted.

He folded the seating plan and carried it to Kagami.

“Don’t move the chair,” Kagami said after reading it.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Don’t let anyone fill the card in.”

“That might be difficult if the guest arrives.”

“Then ask for identification and confirm with the headmistress.”

Kuze watched him take out his phone. Kagami called the headmistress’s office. No answer. He called the assistant’s number. She was on the way to the kitchen and promised to call back.

Himeno joined them with a stack of folded programs. “What happened?”

“One extra chair,” Kuze said.

“One extra chair is not a threat.”

“No,” said Kagami. “But an unnamed guest is a question.”

“Then ask it.”

They returned to the hall together. The blank place card had not moved. The door to the kitchen stood open, and the smell of broth filled the corridor. Volunteers carried covered trays to the serving table. A pair of alumni entered with their invitations ready in their hands.

At the end of the third table, someone had placed a folded paper lantern on the empty chair.

Kagami stopped. “Who put that there?”

The clerk shook her head. “It wasn’t there a minute ago.”

Kuze took one step forward, then waited for Sato, who was checking the guest list at the front door. The lantern was white, folded small, tied with blue thread. There was no name card visible beneath it.

Sato put on gloves and lifted the lantern by its thread. A paper slip fell onto the table.

It was blank on one side.

On the other, in the cramped handwriting from the dance, was a short message:

At nine, count again.

The kitchen clock read 8:41 p.m.

Kagami checked the guest list. Thirty-one names. Thirty-two chairs.

This time, no one called it an oversight.

At 8:52 p.m., the headmistress’s assistant called back. She confirmed the headmistress had not asked for an unnamed guest. She also confirmed that the headmistress was in the west office, where the reception was poor. Kagami sent a volunteer to fetch her, with another person beside him.

Sato asked the banquet staff to pause service. He checked the entrances and the guest list. The front doors remained open, but no one crossed the threshold. The alumni who had already arrived stayed in the adjoining room, where Himeno took attendance and asked them not to leave until the list was reconciled.

“Thirty-one names,” Kuze said. “Thirty-two chairs.”

“Thirty-one names we can confirm,” Sato corrected. “The clerk says there should be a late guest.”

“From the headmistress.”

“Which the headmistress has not confirmed.”

The distinction made the empty chair feel less like a threat and more like a problem they had not solved. Kagami preferred it that way. He asked the clerk for the original seating plan, then compared it with the program sent to the kitchen. Both showed thirty-two meals. Only one blank place card.

At 8:58 p.m., the volunteer returned without the headmistress. The west office was empty. Her coat was gone from the hook.

The banquet hall clock ticked once toward nine.

The front doors opened.
