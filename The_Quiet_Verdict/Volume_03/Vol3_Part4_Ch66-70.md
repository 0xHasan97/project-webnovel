# Chapter 66: The Name in the Empty Place

---

The man at the door did not step inside.

He held a cream-colored envelope in one hand and kept the other visible at his side. His overcoat was dark from the evening mist. Behind him, the path lamps threw long shadows over the stone steps.

Kagami moved between him and the hall.

“Your name?”

“Ishikawa Daigo.”

Detective Sato looked up from the guest ledger. “Inspector.”

“I’m not here in an official capacity.” Ishikawa held out the envelope, not his hand. “I received this at my address on Saturday. It says the school’s closing banquet begins at nine that evening.”

Kagami did not take it. “Who sent it?”

“The envelope was addressed by hand. There was no return address.”

“Did you accept it from the person who delivered it?”

“It was in my mailbox.”

Sato asked for the letter. Ishikawa let him inspect the paper without releasing it. The school seal was pressed into the flap. The card inside carried the Aster letterhead and a formal invitation to the festival’s closing banquet. The lower line read: One place reserved. Please arrive at nine.

There was no name on the place card.

The hall clock struck nine.

For several seconds, nobody moved. The clerk looked toward the empty chair. The assistant looked at the headmistress. Kagami kept the doorway clear and let the man wait outside while the invitation was checked. He did not accept the clock as a reason to hurry.

“May I see your identification?” Kagami asked.

Ishikawa produced his police identification. Sato verified it, then checked the invitation against the school’s stationery and the seal on the committee’s copy. The signature was the festival secretary’s, not the headmistress’s.

“Your name is not on the guest list,” Kagami said.

“It wasn’t on the card either.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No. It’s the problem.”

Kagami looked toward the extra chair, visible through the open hall doors. “I can’t admit you until the committee confirms the invitation.”

“Then don’t.” Ishikawa lowered the envelope. “I’ll wait here.”

The assistant arrived from the west corridor, breathless. The headmistress was behind her. She had no coat, and a thin line of rain darkened one shoulder of her dress.

“I sent that invitation,” she said. “The secretary prepared the card from my instruction. I reserved a place for Inspector Ishikawa.”

“Why was his name left off the list?” Kagami asked.

“Because he had not confirmed he was coming.”

“I told your office I would attend the festival,” Ishikawa said. “I did not say I would attend dinner.”

“And yet you came.”

“I received an invitation.”

The headmistress looked at the blank card on the chair. “That part I did not authorize.”

“Did you tell anyone to leave it blank?” said Himeno.

“No.”

The clerk shook her head. “I was told the headmistress wanted one place for a late guest.”

“By whom?” Kagami asked.

“I thought it was the assistant.”

The assistant’s face tightened. “I never said that.”

There it was again: one person trusting another person’s authority, a name passed from mouth to mouth until no one could tell where it had begun.

Kagami asked the clerk to describe the person who had given her the instruction. She said she had heard the words in the corridor, not seen the speaker. A cart had been passing. The voice had sounded like a woman’s, but she could not be certain. The assistant remembered being asked about the invitations earlier in the day, but not about an extra chair.

“Could the clerk have misunderstood the headmistress’s instruction about Ishikawa?” Himeno asked.

“Possibly,” said the assistant. “I told her one place was still uncertain. I didn’t say to leave it blank.”

“That would explain the chair,” Kagami said. “Not the lantern.”

Sato asked who had access to the banquet hall during the final setup. The clerk, the servers, the committee volunteers, and the cleaning staff had all passed through. More than twenty people had a legitimate reason to enter. The hall’s side door had not been locked.

“Then the person who put it there could be one of us,” Wataru said.

“Or a guest,” said Himeno. “Or someone who was never supposed to be here.”

No one wrote any of those possibilities in the official report. They recorded who had used the room and when, then let the list stay a list.

The headmistress turned to Ishikawa. “The invitation is genuine. You may come in.”

Kagami checked the card once more, then stepped aside. “You’ll stay with the group. No unsupervised access to the school offices.”

“I came to eat,” Ishikawa said.

“No one comes here only to eat,” said Wataru.

Ishikawa gave him a faint, tired smile. “That may be true.”

The clerk brought the guest book. Sato watched as Ishikawa wrote his full name, the time, and the reason for his visit. The ink was dark blue. His writing leaned slightly to the left.

Sato asked Ishikawa to write his name a second time on a separate card. The letters matched. The inspector signed the ledger himself, then watched Sato add the time and the witness mark. Neither man accepted the other’s memory as a substitute for the record.

“Did you bring anyone with you?” Kagami asked.

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you to wait outside?”

“No. The invitation said nine that evening. I arrived at nine.”

“Did anyone approach you on the hill?”

“Only the driver from the school van. He asked whether I knew where the main hall was.”

“Did he know you were coming?”

“He saw the invitation in my hand.”

Sato recorded the exchange. The driver was still outside, and Kagami sent a volunteer to bring him in for a separate statement. He had not entered the hall and was not on the guest list, but he had been near the gate when Ishikawa arrived. They would not turn that into an accusation without asking him.

Kuze copied the name into his notebook.

“Thirty-two,” he said.

“Now the count matches,” said Kagami.

Kuze looked at the blank place card, still untouched on the table. “The count matches. The card doesn’t.”

The headmistress’s eyes turned to him. “Then leave it blank.”

Ishikawa took his seat at the end of the third table. He placed the invitation beside his plate, face down.

Ren was seated at the first table. He looked once toward Ishikawa, then lowered his eyes to the program.

The guest list had a name now.

No one knew who had left the extra place empty.

Sato asked the secretary to add a note to the guest count: the total had been checked against the sign-in ledger, not against every person who had crossed the school grounds. The driver had waited outside; his name belonged on the staff transport sheet, not among the guests. The distinction kept a person who had been near the gate from being counted as someone seated inside.

Kagami read the wording back to her. She confirmed it without adding a name for the place. The page now had a total, a source and a limit. It still did not explain who had asked for the extra setting.

Before the doors opened for the meal, Kagami asked the secretary to read the three counts once more: signed guests, chairs set, meals prepared. She read them as separate figures. Ishikawa was included among the named guests; the blank setting remained outside the guest total. The unserved meal stayed on the kitchen sheet.

Sato signed the time of the recount. The headmistress confirmed that the figures reflected the papers in the committee office, not a claim that every person who entered the grounds had been identified. Kuze copied the wording into his notebook and left a space below it for later correction.

# Chapter 67: The Second Signature

---

The banquet began with a toast to the students.

The headmistress stood at the front of the hall with a glass of water. She thanked the committee, the teachers, and the visitors who had supported Aster through the year. She did not mention the missing page, the false entry, or the two messages. She did not say the festival had been safe. She said the students had made it theirs.

That much was true.

The first plates were served. Class 1-A’s representatives sat beside the students from 1-D. The fundraiser had been counted and closed. The prize went to the winning class from the festival fund, as promised. No one lost points. The students still argued about the eleven-point swing, but now they argued over the receipts instead of accusing one another.

Himeno sat at the committee table with her ledger open. She had brought the original tally sheet, the signed copy, and the corrected receipt. She would not let the school’s public figures become something people had to remember differently later.

Kagami watched the doors while he ate. Sato sat close enough to speak with him without raising his voice. Wataru had taken the seat beside Kuze, leaving the place on Kuze’s other side for the retired teacher.

Ishikawa did not touch the first course. He unfolded the invitation and studied the inside of the envelope.

“What are you looking for?” Kuze asked.

“A second signature.”

“There isn’t one.”

“There was.” Ishikawa turned the card sideways. “See the pressure marks? Someone wrote on a sheet above this one. The paper underneath caught the impression.”

Sato leaned across the aisle. “Can you read it?”

“Not all of it.” Ishikawa angled the card toward the light. “A capital. Maybe an R. Then a line.”

Ren, across the room, set down his glass.

“Could be a mark from the printer,” Himeno said.

“It could.” Ishikawa slid the invitation into a clear sleeve. “I’m not calling it a name.”

The assistant brought a new dish to the table and asked if anyone needed anything. Kagami used the moment to ask whether the invitations had been printed in the school office.

“Some were,” she said. “The rest were sent to the print shop.”

“Which one is this?”

“I don’t know. I can check the order tomorrow.”

“Who had access to the seal?”

“The committee secretary. The headmistress. Me.”

“And the printer?”

“The seal was applied here, after the cards came back.”

Kagami made a note. He did not ask the assistant to guess who had handled the envelope. The school had already had enough guesses.

When the assistant had gone, Wataru leaned toward Kuze. “Do you believe him?”

“I believe he saw pressure marks.”

“Do you believe the R is there?”

“I believe he thinks it may be.”

“Is that enough?”

“No.”

Kuze wrote the observation down. His notebook had two columns now: what a person said, and what could be checked. Ishikawa’s card went in the first column. The pressure mark could be examined later.

Across the hall, the headmistress paused beside Ren. She spoke to him quietly. He listened, gave one small nod, and returned to his place. The headmistress did not sit beside him. She took the chair that had been assigned to her, beside Kagami.

Himeno watched her. “She went looking for him before she came back.”

“Do you know that?” Kagami asked.

“No.”

“Then it stays in your notebook, not in the report.”

“I know the difference.”

The meal continued. A teacher raised a glass. The students answered with a cheer. The hall warmed with the smell of rice, broth, and damp coats drying near the entry. For a little while, the only people asking questions were the ones passing the serving plates.

Kagami asked the committee secretary to bring the printing order after dinner. The secretary promised to find it in the office. Himeno wrote the request in the margin of her ledger and asked the assistant to countersign it. The assistant did. It was a small, dull procedure, and exactly the sort of thing no one could later remember differently.

At the second table, Wataru leaned toward Kuze. “Did you see Ren look at the envelope?”

“I saw him put down his glass.”

“Do you think that means something?”

“I think he put down his glass.”

“You’re impossible.”

“So I’m told.”

Wataru took another bite of the main course. “I’m going to ask him anyway.”

“Ask him what?”

“If he knows the inspector.”

“Then ask that. Don’t ask what his glass means.”

Wataru waited until a server passed, then caught Ren’s attention across the room. “Aizawa-kun. Have you met Inspector Ishikawa before tonight?”

Ren considered him. “I saw him at the clock tower.”

“Did you know he’d be here?”

“No.”

“Did the headmistress tell you she invited him?”

“No.”

Wataru glanced at Kuze, who wrote the answers down. Ren’s replies were plain. They were also easy to repeat later. That was not proof of anything except that he had chosen his words carefully.

Then Ishikawa closed the sleeve over the invitation and put it away.

“I have one question for Aizawa,” he said.

Kagami’s chair shifted. “You can ask it here.”

“I can.” Ishikawa looked at Ren. “Do you know what Lantern House kept under the school?”

Ren’s face did not change. “No.”

“Do you know where the old archive stairs lead?”

“I know where the archive stands.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“No,” Ren said. “It wasn’t.”

The silverware quieted around them. The headmistress kept her eyes on her plate.

Ishikawa sat back. “That is all.”

Kagami glanced toward Ren, waiting for a reply. Ren lifted his glass and drank.

Kuze wrote the question down exactly as it had been asked.

After Ishikawa sat down, Wataru moved his chair so he could see both the inspector and the service corridor. Himeno noticed and asked if he was guarding the door.

“I’m watching it,” he said.

“Those aren’t the same thing.”

“No. Guarding it would mean I could stop anyone.”

She glanced toward Kagami. “Could he?”

“He can tell people not to enter. He can’t know everyone who already has.”

Wataru folded his napkin. “You make every answer worse.”

“I’m trying to make it accurate.”

Across the room, the headmistress asked Ren to bring her the festival program. He crossed the hall, gave it to her, and returned to his chair. She did not lean close or lower her voice. Himeno saw the exchange but could not hear a word.

Kuze asked whether she wanted to record it.

“I saw a student carry a program,” she said. “That’s all.”

He wrote that down. The conversation could have been about the schedule, the speech, or nothing that concerned the banquet. Its timing was a fact; its meaning remained unavailable.

The secretary returned with the printer order after the first course. It showed that the school had ordered forty blank cards, more than the thirty-three places set for the banquet. The printer had delivered them in two packets. The office log recorded receipt of both, but not how many remained in the supply drawer.

Himeno asked whether the blank card at the extra place could have come from an older stack. The secretary said it could. The stock was the same size and finish. Sato photographed the order and the card, then kept them in different sleeves. The order established what had been delivered; it did not establish which card had been put on the table or by whom.

# Chapter 68: The Guest Who Wasn’t Expected

---

After the meal, the committee opened the west doors to the terrace.

The sea was black beyond the garden wall. A line of small lamps marked the path, and the night air smelled of salt and wet stone. The guests came out in clusters, carrying their cups. Students cleared the plates and folded the tablecloths while the teachers gathered near the windows.

Kagami moved to the terrace entrance. He had the new guest list in his hand, with Ishikawa’s name written in the final line. The count now matched the number of people who had come to dinner.

“Did you invite him?” he asked Ren.

“No.”

“Did you know the headmistress had?”

“No.”

“Did you know he’d arrive?”

Ren looked across the terrace. Ishikawa stood near the far railing, speaking with Sato. “I knew he had received an invitation to the festival.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I did not know he would come tonight.”

Kagami watched him for a second. “You understand why that sounds convenient.”

“Yes.”

“Do you care?”

“Not about how it sounds.”

The answer was quiet enough that Kagami could not tell whether it was a refusal or simply the truth.

Himeno approached with the seating chart. “The card was blank before the lantern appeared. The committee clerk says she was told to keep one place open. The headmistress says she reserved Ishikawa. Those are two different stories.”

“Could both be true?” Wataru asked.

“They could,” she said. “The reservation might have been made without a name. The clerk might have understood the instruction correctly and still been wrong about who gave it.”

Kuze showed them the time in his notebook. “The blank card was seen at 8:26 p.m. The lantern appeared before 8:41 p.m. Ishikawa reached the door just before nine that evening.”

He turned back a page and listed the sources beside the times: a volunteer’s message, Sato’s photograph, and the hall clock. The volunteer had sent her message after noticing the card; she had not checked the time as she saw it. The camera had not been synchronized that morning. The hall clock had been compared with the kitchen clock, not the office clock.

“They’re different sources,” Kuze said.

“Which means we can’t pretend they’re one clock,” Kagami replied.

Sato requested the original camera file and asked the volunteer to preserve her message on her phone. He did not ask her to forward it through the committee account; he wanted the original timestamp intact.

Kagami looked at the card. “We know it was blank when someone saw it. We don’t know when it was placed there.”

“Or whether it was the same card the lantern message referred to,” Wataru said.

“No,” Kuze said. “We don’t know that either.”

Kagami had the clerk bring the first count sheet and the later correction. The earlier page had thirty-one names; Ishikawa’s was added after the invitation was confirmed. The seating plan had thirty-three settings. Those figures could be reconciled in more than one way, but none of the papers identified the extra place.

The clerk said the headmistress had told her a guest might arrive late. The headmistress remembered authorizing one place for Ishikawa, not an additional anonymous one. Both accounts could describe different conversations. Neither woman could say when the blank card had been placed on the table.

Sato asked each of them to write down what she remembered without looking at the other’s page. The two statements went into separate sleeves. Kagami asked the secretary not to staple them together just because they concerned the same chair.

“Someone will say the numbers prove there was a hidden guest,” Wataru said.

“Someone might,” Kuze replied.

“Would they be wrong?”

“They would be claiming more than the numbers show.”

Himeno tapped the empty box beside “assigned name.” “Then leave it empty.”

The secretary did. She wrote “unassigned” in the status column and signed beside the correction.

The headmistress asked the secretary to keep the place card separate from the lantern. The secretary wrote two evidence labels and placed them in different sleeves. No ink, thread or paper edge connected the objects. For the moment, their only shared feature was that people had been told to count.

“Who was in the hall between those times?” Kagami asked.

“Too many people to count.”

“Then we don’t claim the same person did all three things.”

Ishikawa had finished speaking with Sato. He came over and held out the invitation sleeve to Kuze.

“You should keep the pressure mark,” he said. “It’s your school’s paper.”

“I can’t take evidence from you.”

“It isn’t evidence yet. It’s an invitation.”

“Then it belongs with the committee.” Kuze handed it to Sato, who made a note of the transfer and returned it to Ishikawa.

“Good,” said the inspector. “You’re learning not to carry other people’s objects.”

“I was already doing that.”

“Then you’re learning to make it visible.”

Ishikawa turned back toward the terrace. Kagami followed him with his eyes.

“Why did you invite him?” Himeno asked the headmistress.

The headmistress had come out onto the terrace without her glass. “I did not invite him to investigate the school. I invited him to dinner.”

“Why?”

“Because I wished to hear what he would say when there were witnesses.”

“And what did he say?”

“Very little.”

“Was that what you wanted?”

The headmistress looked toward the dark garden. “I wanted to know whether he would ask the same question in public that he asked in private.”

“Which question?”

She did not answer.

# Chapter 69: The Meal Set Aside

---

The extra meal was still covered.

It sat on a small service table in the kitchen, untouched, with a folded napkin and a glass of water beside it. The place card had been left beneath the tray, its blank side facing up. The blue line in the corner was short and straight, the kind of mark a clerk might make to distinguish one stack from another.

The chef said he had prepared thirty-three meals, as the order required. Thirty-two had been served at the tables. One remained in the kitchen.

“Thirty-two guests,” the clerk said. “Thirty-three meals. One was held for the unassigned place.”

“Inspector Ishikawa’s meal was served,” Himeno said.

“Yes.”

“Then why is there still an extra?”

The clerk looked at the headmistress. “I thought the inspector was the late guest.”

“You thought,” Kagami said. “Who told you to prepare another plate?”

“No one. It was in the order.”

They checked the paper. Thirty-three meals and thirty-three places, counting the blank one. Thirty-two confirmed guests after Ishikawa arrived. One place and one meal remained unclaimed.

“Thirty-three settings for thirty-two confirmed guests,” Kagami said. “Who authorized the extra?”

The clerk shook her head. The order had been approved by the banquet committee, but no one could say who added the final portion. It was not enough to prove a hidden guest. It was enough to show that the unknown was not just a blank card.

“Could the order have been written before the seating plan?” Kuze asked.

The chef brought the original. The meal count had been written on Friday, before the guest list was finalized. At the bottom, beside the number thirty-three, someone had written “one held.” The writing was too small to identify.

“Who wrote it?” said Sato.

The chef recognized the hand as his assistant’s. The assistant confirmed it. She had been told that one guest might arrive late and had held the meal under the warmer. She could not remember who told her. It might have been the committee clerk. It might have been one of the teachers.

“Do you remember the words?” Kuze asked.

She thought for a moment. “Someone said, ‘Don’t count the last place until nine.’”

The kitchen fell quiet.

The assistant’s statement ended at nine. She could not place the phrase before or after the meal order was delivered. The clerk remembered hearing it during setup, but the setup sheet had no time beside her initials. Neither witness had discussed the wording with the other before Sato interviewed them. He recorded their accounts separately and noted the difference.

Kagami asked whether the kitchen had kept a copy of the order. The chef brought one from the office, folded through the line that listed the portions. The original had been prepared on Friday; the copy was made on Sunday afternoon. The words “one held” appeared on both, but the copy did not show who had added them. The chef could confirm the number, not the handwriting.

Himeno asked the assistant to describe the person who gave the instruction. She had been facing the sink. She could not identify height, clothing or voice. She remembered only that a tray had been set down beside her. Several people had used the passage during setup. Sato asked her not to choose a name from the list of possibilities. Her uncertainty was entered as uncertainty, not as a failure to cooperate.

“Did you recognize the voice?”

“No.”

“Man or woman?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where were you?”

“Here. I was washing trays.”

Sato wrote it down. The statement did not prove a second guest existed. It proved someone had asked the kitchen to hold a meal, using the idea of a last place.

The headmistress turned the blank card over. A small indentation crossed the center of the paper, as if someone had written on a sheet above it. No ink remained. The pressure mark might be a name, or a crease from the tray.

Ishikawa stood by the kitchen door, hands in his coat pockets. “This is how a missing person becomes a number.”

“Do you know who it is?” Kagami asked.

“No.”

“Then don’t turn it into a person before we have a name.”

The inspector looked at him. “That is the right answer.”

The headmistress returned the card to its sleeve. “No one is eating this meal.”

“Why not?” asked Wataru.

“Because it was set aside for someone who has not arrived.”

“Or for someone who never existed,” said Himeno.

“Then it can be discarded after the record is made.”

The tray went back beneath the warmer. The staff logged it as unserved and wrote down the time. No one touched the glass.

The chef asked whether the meal should be included in the count of food served. Himeno said it should appear on the preparation sheet and not on the table-service total. The secretary changed the heading from “served” to “prepared” and left the original line visible beneath the correction.

Sato asked the chef to sign both the correction and the earlier count. The chef said the extra portion had been prepared but never carried into the hall. The paper could show the difference; no witness could say whether a person had been expected to eat it.

Sato photographed the meal, the tray, and the table where it had been held. The chef signed the inventory beside the number thirty-two. His assistant signed beneath him. The headmistress added her initials, then asked the kitchen to close the service door until the banquet ended.

“Does the invitation say thirty-two?” Himeno asked.

“It says thirty-one plus one place,” the clerk replied.

“That isn’t the same as thirty-two confirmed guests.”

“No,” the clerk said. “It isn’t.”

The clerk looked embarrassed, but did not defend the mistake. She had been given an instruction with no name attached and had turned it into a guest count. Someone else might have done the same. The problem was not that the story was impossible. The problem was that it had been accepted without a person to answer for it.

Kagami asked her to write down who gave her the instruction, when she heard it, and what words she remembered. She did so on a separate sheet. Her account did not match the assistant’s memory, but the two had never spoken about the late place before.

“Could they both be remembering honestly?” Kuze asked.

“Yes,” said Sato. “People remember different parts. That’s why we take statements separately.”

The blank card stayed sealed. No one filled it in, even after the meal was logged as unserved.

The banquet continued in the hall. Guests spoke in lower voices. The students began stacking chairs. Ren carried a crate of serving bowls to the kitchen and placed it on the counter without looking at the covered tray.

Himeno watched him leave. “You didn’t ask who the last place was for.”

“I heard the report.”

“Do you think it was meant for someone?”

“I think a meal was prepared.”

“You can be infuriating.”

“That is not a question.”

“No,” she said. “It isn’t.”

The assistant later brought the envelope sleeves from the office. One held the invitation Ishikawa had returned; the other held the duplicate school copy. The seal was intact on the duplicate. The card itself carried no added name or note. Kagami asked Sato to record the transfer and return the original to Ishikawa rather than keep it with the anonymous place card.

The clerk initialed the inventory. The invitation and the place card were different objects, with different histories, even if both had left questions around the banquet. Kuze recorded the distinction before the dinner resumed.

# Chapter 70: The Count at Ten

---

At ten that evening, Kagami asked for a fresh count.

Not the count of chairs or plates. People.

Sato and the committee clerk took the guest ledger to the hall entrance. The headmistress named the teachers and staff in the room. Wataru checked the students against the committee roster. Ishikawa remained by the terrace doors, where the open air kept the windows from fogging.

“Thirty-two guests,” the clerk said when they finished. “Including the inspector.”

“And staff?” Kagami asked.

“Seven in the hall and kitchen. The driver is outside.”

“Then record guests and staff separately.”

Kuze wrote the count in his notebook. “Thirty-two named guests. One blank place. Thirty-three meals.”

“Keep the categories separate,” Himeno said.

“I have.”

“Good.”

At the entrance, Sato compared the guest ledger with the invitation list. Ishikawa’s name appeared in both, though it had been added to the seating plan after the original count. The blank place remained unassigned. The headmistress’s genuine invitation did not explain it, and the kitchen’s extra meal did not prove anyone had come to claim it.

Kagami dictated the distinction into the recorder. He asked the clerk to read the guest count aloud, then asked her to state separately how many chairs had been set. She did so. Sato noted the time and the names of the two witnesses.

“Do we need to keep counting?” Kuze asked.

“Until the guests leave,” Kagami said. “A count is only useful if we know when it was taken.”

Across the hall, the headmistress stood beside Ren. She spoke to him for less than a minute. Ren listened, nodded once and returned to his seat. Kagami watched the exchange but did not interrupt it.

Himeno came to stand by Kuze. “You’re writing everything down.”

“Not everything.”

“What did you leave out?”

“What I can’t verify.”

“Like whether she asked him to do something?”

“Yes.”

Himeno looked toward the headmistress. “I saw her talk to him. I didn’t hear the words.”

“Then that’s what we have.”

“Sometimes what we have is not enough.”

“Then we leave the line open.”

She looked at the empty place card through the doorway to the kitchen. “That’s what someone else wants us to do.”

“Maybe.”

“Maybe is a miserable answer.”

“It’s still an answer.”

At 10:08 p.m., the committee secretary returned from the office with the original seating chart. The blank place had been marked in pencil on the first draft, then left blank on the final version. No name had been erased. The chart had been amended twice; the initials beside both changes belonged to the secretary.

She laid the sheets beside each other. “I made the changes. I can explain them.”

Sato asked her to do so on the record. The first change moved a donor away from the terrace door. The second added a chair after the headmistress said one guest might attend. Neither instruction named the guest. The secretary had not added the extra meal; she had not seen the kitchen order.

Kuze checked the dates. The first draft was from Thursday. The second was from Saturday. The phrase “one guest might attend” appeared only in the secretary’s statement, not on either sheet.

“Did the headmistress use those exact words?” he asked.

“I think so.”

“Could you be wrong?”

“Yes.”

“Thank you,” he said, and wrote that down too.

The headmistress signed the statement without comment. Her signature did not confirm the secretary’s memory; it confirmed only that she had read the page. Sato made that distinction in the record.

A burst of laughter rose near the stage. The festival committee announced that the final collection had been counted and sealed. Himeno checked the total against her ledger. It matched the corrected receipt. She signed the page and passed it to the treasurer.

For a moment, the hall had a reason to applaud that did not involve the empty chair.

Kagami asked Kuze to walk with him to the service corridor. Sato remained at the entrance. They stopped where the kitchen door and the west passage met. There were no wet footprints now; the floor had dried. A narrow strip of blue thread lay near the baseboard.

Kagami did not touch it. He called Sato, who photographed it in place before placing it in a sleeve. The thread was similar in color to the lantern tie but not yet matched. It could have come from a table decoration, a coat, or the paper lantern.

“Do you think it belongs with the other one?” Kuze asked.

“I think it’s a blue thread on the floor,” Kagami said.

Kuze wrote that down.

They returned to the hall. The guest count remained thirty-two. The banquet had reached its last toast, but no one had begun to leave.

Kagami asked Sato to compare the written count with the people still in the hall. The clerk read the names while the committee volunteers checked each table. The number matched. Ishikawa was present; the headmistress was present; the unassigned place remained empty. The check established who was in the room at that moment, not who had been there earlier.

The chair asked whether the count should be announced. Kagami said it could be recorded, but a public number without its limits would invite people to treat it as a full account of the evening. The headmistress agreed to include the time and method: names read from the ledger, then checked against the room.

A donor asked if the school would publish the list. The secretary said she would publish the total, not private names. Ishikawa asked whether the blank place would be included in the report.

“Yes,” Kagami said. “As a place, not a person.”

Kuze wrote down the distinction. The record had a total and a time. It did not have a name for the space beside the last table.

At 10:15 p.m., the headmistress announced that one final toast would close the banquet. Guests began gathering near the tables, but no one had signed out yet. Ishikawa kept the invitation in his coat pocket; the committee had already logged its return to him.

Kuze watched the guests gather. “Do we count people leaving now?”

“We record the time they go,” Kagami said. “Not before.”

Sato set the departure ledger beside the entrance and asked the secretary to keep the exit clear. The banquet had not ended.

At 10:16 p.m., he checked the names once more. Thirty-two guests remained in the hall; none had signed out. Kuze’s name had no departure time.

The clerk checked the ledger against the chairs once more. Thirty-two names appeared in the guest book, and thirty-two guests were visible in the hall. The extra chair stood empty. She placed a small mark beside the count and wrote the hall clock time, then asked Kagami to witness the entry.

He did, but added that the observation applied only to that moment. The entry did not show that no one had entered or left earlier without being seen. The clerk copied his qualification into the margin rather than compressing it into a single tick.

At 10:17 p.m., a server told Kagami she had heard Kuze’s name from the west corridor but had not seen who spoke. Kagami asked her to wait for Sato, then asked the guests to remain in the hall while the passage was checked.
