# Chapter 71: The Name Called

---

The west corridor remained empty.

Kagami did not let the server go back alone. Sato walked with her as far as the kitchen entrance, asking her to repeat the words she had heard and to point to the place where she had stood. She did not know the speaker. She had not seen a face. She had heard Kuze’s name, then the service door closing.

“Was the door open?” Sato asked.

“I thought it was.”

“Did you see it open?”

“No.”

He wrote both answers down, leaving the difference intact.

Kagami asked the headmistress to keep the guests in the hall while the committee checked the west passage. He did not call it a search. He said one student had not answered when his name was called, and they were verifying where he was. The distinction calmed no one, but it prevented a rumor from becoming an announcement.

Himeno stood beside the blank place card, now sealed in its evidence sleeve. “Kuze was at the ledger a minute ago.”

“Did you see him leave?” Kagami asked.

“No. I saw the notebook on the table.”

The notebook was gone.

Wataru checked the stage seating, then the terrace doors. Ishikawa remained at the service entrance, where the staff had begun to gather. He said he had not seen Kuze leave the room. The headmistress asked whether anyone had escorted him out. No one had.

Sato checked the guest ledger. Kuze’s name remained beside the time he entered. There was no departure mark. Ishikawa’s signature was the last one on the page.

“Keep the guests inside,” Kagami told the secretary. “Don’t lock the doors. Ask them not to leave until we know whether a witness is needed.”

The secretary nodded and went to the microphone. She asked the guests to remain seated while the committee checked a safety matter. She did not mention Kuze’s name.

Himeno searched the student tables with Wataru. They checked the cloakroom, the washrooms and the small room behind the stage. Each door was opened with a staff member present. No one found Kuze. No one found his notebook.

Ren remained beside the committee table. A folded program lay under his hand. He answered when Kagami asked whether he had seen Kuze after the last count.

“I saw him by the ledger.”

“Did you see him leave?”

“No.”

“Did anyone speak to him?”

“I heard someone call his name.”

Kagami looked up. “Where?”

“Near the service corridor.”

“Who called him?”

“I couldn’t see.”

“Did you hear anything else?”

“No.”

Ren’s account matched the server’s in one detail and added nothing that could identify a speaker. Kagami asked Sato to record it separately.

The hall clock showed 10:17 p.m. The committee began checking the rooms again, this time with staff assigned to each passage. Sato took the service keys from the coordinator and recorded which one opened the west door. The maintenance door beyond it was not on the banquet plan.

The guard who had been stationed nearby was not at his post. His supervisor said he had been asked to inspect the stage cable. No one could say who gave the instruction.

Kagami looked at the empty corridor. “Who last saw the guard?”

The supervisor checked the duty sheet. “I did. At 10:05 p.m.”

“Did he say he was leaving?”

“He said he’d check the cable.”

“Did you send him?”

“No.”

Sato recorded that too. The blank place, the extra meal and the missing student still had no established connection. But the service corridor had now produced two separate absences, and the banquet could no longer pretend its only uncertainty was a chair.

Himeno returned to the table. Kuze’s pen was still beside the ledger. She picked it up, then put it down without writing.

“Don’t let them move anything,” Kagami said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know.”

She stared at the pen. “He was just here.”

“I know.”

At 10:23 p.m., a staff member reported that the west maintenance door stood ajar. Sato went with the coordinator to check it. Kagami kept the guests in the hall. Himeno stayed by the entry, where she could see both the tables and the corridor.

A voice came from beyond the service door. It was not Kuze’s.

Sato called for the hall medic.

The headmistress asked the technician to stop the film and keep the room lights on. The guests were told not to cross the west passage or discuss what they had heard until Sato had taken their names. He asked the server and Ren to wait in separate rooms with a staff member present, not because he suspected either of them but because their accounts had not yet been recorded.

Kagami checked that the main entrance remained available for the medics. He asked the secretary to note who left the hall and when, but not to prevent anyone from leaving. A parent representative objected that keeping people in place would look like a search. Kagami agreed and clarified the instruction: stay if willing, give contact details if leaving, and do not enter the service corridor.

The hall clock reached 10:24 p.m. Sato wrote the minute beside the call for medical help. No one yet knew who was on the stairs, how the guard had been injured, or where Kuze had gone. The three questions remained separate in the log.

The server stayed with the secretary until Sato returned. She was asked not to repeat her account to the guests waiting by the stage. She had heard Kuze’s name, but could not tell whether the person who spoke was calling for him or speaking about him. She had not heard a reply.

Ren remained in the committee room with the headmistress. He agreed to give his account to Sato and asked whether his statement would be shown to the other students. Sato said not while he was still collecting accounts. Ren nodded and waited. No one asked him to speculate about the voice.

Kagami asked the coordinator to mark the service doors on a copy of the floor plan. The west door opened into the service passage; the maintenance door at its end led to the stairs. A second route connected the kitchen to the loading bay, but it did not meet the stairwell. The plan showed where the doors were, not whether they had been locked at any particular time.

Sato asked the technician to identify which camera covered the passage and which only covered the ballroom entrance. The technician pointed to the camera label on the plan and said the system had been configured for motion recording. He could not tell from the control panel whether the clock had drifted before the banquet. The calibration slip would have to be compared with the original file.

A reporter asked Kagami whether the figure had been Kuze. Kagami said the recording did not show a face and the person on the stairs had not been identified by him. The reporter asked if the clothing matched Kuze’s. Kagami said he had not checked Kuze’s clothing before the banquet, and could not make a comparison. Sato wrote the exchange into his log, including the question that produced the answer.

The guests were given paper and asked to write down where they had been when the call came. They could leave if they wished. Several wrote that they were at their tables; two had gone toward the terrace; one had been in the washroom. The times were estimates unless a person had checked a clock. Sato collected each page without asking the writers to compare memories.

# Chapter 72: The Open Door

---

The maintenance door opened onto a narrow stairwell.

Sato stopped at the threshold and asked the coordinator to stay behind him. The security guard was on the landing, conscious and breathing, one hand pressed to his forehead. He said he had slipped. He did not know who had left the door open. The medic reached him first and called for a second team.

Kagami arrived with Himeno and Wataru, but kept them outside the stairwell. “No one goes down until the medic says it’s safe.”

“Where’s Kuze?” Himeno asked.

No one answered.

The guard blinked against the light. “There was a person at the lower door.”

“Did you see their face?” Sato asked.

“No.”

“Did they speak?”

“I heard something. I can’t remember what.”

“Did you fall before or after they left?”

The guard closed his eyes. “I don’t know.”

Sato stopped the questions and let the medic work. The guard was carried up on a stretcher, still awake. He could not identify anyone or explain how he had been hurt.

A second medic went down the stairs. The landing below turned out of sight. Kagami stayed at the doorway. Himeno could see only the medic’s shoulder moving beyond the wall.

The ballroom had gone quiet. The headmistress stood at the microphone and asked everyone to remain where they were. She did not say there had been an assault. The guests began to whisper anyway. Phones came up. The technician switched off the stage screen at Kagami’s request.

“Please don’t photograph the service corridor,” Kagami said to the nearest reporter. “We don’t know what happened.”

The reporter lowered the phone but did not put it away.

After several minutes, the medic called up that another person was below. Sato went down only after the medic confirmed the stairs were clear. Kagami followed as far as the landing and stopped there.

Kuze lay at the foot of the final flight. His notebook was several steps above him, open to a page of names and times. The cover had been bent at one corner. No one touched it.

Himeno reached the top of the stairs. Kagami turned toward her. “Stay here.”

She gripped the rail. “Is he alive?”

“The medic is checking.”

The answer was not reassurance. It was all Kagami had.

Sato called the ambulance and asked the hall staff to preserve the door, the stairwell and the camera recordings. The security system showed the service door opening once after the banquet began, but its clock was four minutes behind the clock in the hall. The footage showed a figure crossing the corridor. The face was outside the frame.

“Can you identify the clothing?” Kagami asked.

“Dark jacket,” Sato said. “That’s all I can say.”

“Don’t call it Kuze until someone can.”

Sato looked at the figure again. “Understood.”

The hall coordinator brought the key log. The maintenance key had been signed out at 6:12 p.m. and returned at 6:29 p.m. The signature belonged to the technician, who had used it to check the stage lighting. No one had signed it out after that. The door had either been left unsecured or opened another way. The log could not distinguish between them.

The medic called for more space. The guests were moved back into the ballroom. Himeno stayed at the threshold until the stretcher began its climb. She saw Kuze’s hand, then the sheet drawn over him. She did not see his face.

A volunteer asked Himeno whether the person under the sheet was Kuze. She answered that she had seen a hand and recognized the sleeve, but had not seen the face. The volunteer asked if the sleeve was distinctive. Himeno said it was a dark school jacket like several others worn that evening.

Sato recorded her exact answer. He did not change it to “identified” or “unidentified.” The distinction belonged in the statement until a more reliable identification could be made. Himeno had recognized an article of clothing, not the person beneath it.

Wataru stood beside her. “They’ll tell us when they know.”

“Who will?”

“The hospital.”

“Not the people in that room?”

Wataru had no answer.

Kagami asked Sato to seal Kuze’s notebook where it had fallen. The open page remained visible through the clear evidence bag: times, initials and one line that had been written twice. The final word was cut off at the edge of the paper.

The ambulance crew carried Kuze through the service hall, not past the banquet tables. The headmistress stood by the stage and told the guests that a student had been injured and was receiving medical care. She asked them not to speculate about his condition.

Kagami accompanied the ambulance. Sato stayed to collect the witness names. Himeno waited by the doors with the envelope Kuze had given her before the banquet. She had not opened it. Its seal was unbroken.

At the curb, Kagami turned back once. “Stay with Sato. Don’t go home alone.”

“Tell me the truth when you have it.”

“I will.”

He left with the ambulance. The ballroom doors closed behind him, but the guests did not return to their meal.

At the hospital entrance, Kagami gave the ambulance crew the name written in the guest ledger and told them what the responders had observed at the stairs. He did not describe the covered figure as identified. The crew checked the wristband against the name he provided and asked whether anyone had seen the face. Kagami said that Himeno had not; he could not answer for the medic.

The receiving clerk took the time of arrival from the ambulance form. Kagami asked for a copy of the transfer receipt and wrote down the name of the person who gave it to him. The receipt confirmed that a patient had been delivered under Kuze's name. It did not answer the questions about the corridor or the cause of the emergency.

When he returned to the committee room, Sato had sealed the notebook, key and camera export separately. The door log was still open because the coordinator had not yet located the spare key. Kagami asked him to write that the key had not been found, rather than that it was missing from the building. The difference would matter if it turned up later.

Before the hospital called, Sato asked Kagami to dictate the sequence as he remembered it. Kagami began with the server’s report, then corrected himself: he had not heard the first call, only learned of it from the server. He remembered seeing the west passage empty before Sato went to check it. He could not say who had opened the maintenance door.

The correction altered the first sentence of the draft report. Sato left the original wording visible and added Kagami’s clarification beside it. He asked whether Kagami could estimate how many minutes separated the last count from the door report. Kagami gave an estimate, then said the hall clock had not been synchronized with the camera and he had not checked his watch.

Himeno asked whether the written guest count would be treated as proof Kuze remained in the building. Sato said it showed that Kuze had not signed out. It did not prove where he was. She asked him to include that exact limit. He did.

The blank place card stayed in its sleeve on the committee table. The medical response had not made it more relevant, but people kept looking at it. Himeno asked the secretary to cover the sleeve with a plain folder while the statements were taken. The card remained preserved without becoming the center of every conversation.

When the first call from the hospital came, Sato noted the caller’s department, the time and the wording. He did not let the room interrupt. He asked the caller to repeat the information, then ended the call without adding a conclusion to the log.

# Chapter 73: The Witnesses

---

The banquet ended without a closing toast.

Sato recorded statements in the committee room, one person at a time. The server who heard Kuze’s name said the voice came from the west passage. Ren had heard it too, but could not identify the speaker. The technician said he had not been near the maintenance door after 6:29. The guard remembered a figure on the lower landing, then a fall. His account stopped there.

No two statements described the same view.

The secretary asked whether that meant the statements contradicted one another. Sato said they conflicted only where they described the same moment from different positions; in several places, the witnesses were describing different moments altogether. He marked the time each person began speaking and noted that none had read another person’s account before giving one.

Himeno asked for a copy of the plan when the witnesses finished. Sato agreed, but left the names off the version for the committee. The original retained their locations and signatures. The copy could show the limits of sightlines without exposing details the students had not agreed to share.

Himeno sat outside the committee room with the sealed envelope on her knees. It contained Kuze’s letter and a key to the cabinet where he kept his copies. She had agreed to open it only if he did not return. The ambulance had left less than an hour ago. She did not know what counted as “not return.”

Wataru brought her a cup of water. She held it but did not drink.

“Could you call the hospital?” she asked.

“Sato’s doing that.”

“Then why are we waiting?”

“Because they haven’t answered.”

She looked at the envelope. “This is ridiculous.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t mean the letter.”

“I know.”

Across the room, Ren sat with the other students. He had given his statement and had not asked to leave. His program was folded in half on the table. A blank strip of paper protruded from its center; no one had yet recorded it as evidence.

The headmistress asked Himeno whether she wanted to go to the infirmary. Himeno said no. She wanted the guest list, the door log and the original footage preserved. The headmistress asked Sato to put those instructions in the record.

“Do you think that will help him?” Wataru asked.

“It will help whoever has to find out what happened.”

“You think there’s a difference?”

“I have to.”

The committee secretary returned with a clean copy of the guest count. Thirty-two people had signed in. Thirty-one had signed out before the corridor was closed. Kuze’s line had no departure time. The blank place card remained unassigned. The extra meal had been photographed and logged, then removed from the kitchen under supervision.

“Don’t join these counts,” Himeno said. “The guest book says who entered. The meal sheet says what was prepared. The missing departure mark says only that he hadn’t signed out.”

Sato nodded and wrote down her correction. The secretary initialed it.

At 11:14 p.m., the hospital called. Sato answered, listened, and asked the caller to repeat the name. He wrote down the time, department and the words used. Then he asked for a written notice and the name of the official authorized to release it.

He did not say anything to the room until the call ended.

“What did they say?” Himeno asked.

“They’re still assessing him. They won’t give me more by phone.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s all they told me.”

The headmistress asked him to read back his notes. He did. The hospital had confirmed Kuze’s arrival and said he was receiving emergency care. It had not given a prognosis. Sato’s record showed what he had been told, not what he feared.

Himeno closed her fingers around the envelope. “I’m not opening it yet.”

“No one asked you to,” Wataru said.

The second call came at 11:38 p.m. This time, the headmistress took it. She repeated the caller’s name and the hospital’s department before listening. Her pen paused over the paper. She asked for the message to be stated once more, then wrote the time beside it.

When she hung up, the ballroom remained silent.

“They’ve told us he has died,” she said. “The written notice is being sent.”

The words were heard by the students, the committee, the remaining guests and two reporters waiting near the doors. No one in the hall had seen the hospital record. Sato wrote down the announcement and the source as the headmistress’s account of a telephone call.

Himeno stood. The sealed envelope slipped from her hand and landed on the floor. She picked it up before anyone else could move.

The headmistress asked the guests to remain while the police were notified. The banquet staff began collecting untouched glasses, then stopped when Sato asked them to leave the tables as they were. A camera light blinked from the far end of the room.

Kagami had not returned.

The technician asked to correct one detail in his first account. He had said he was away from the west passage after 6:29, but that referred to the maintenance key, not to his own location. He had carried a cable spool past the passage later, though he had not entered it. Sato added the clarification as a second statement and left the first version visible in the file.

A server also remembered that a tray had been removed from the kitchen at some point after the toast. She could not say whether it was the held meal, and no one had asked her to identify it before she left. The chef confirmed that the one unserved meal was logged and disposed of after the police had photographed it. The server's memory did not change the meal record.

Himeno asked for the clock times from the hall, kitchen and camera to be listed on one page with their sources. Sato did so but refused to sort them into a single sequence until the technician supplied the calibration file. The page was useful because it showed the disagreement, not because it solved it.

The headmistress asked Kagami to sit with her before the statement was read. She wanted to know whether he had seen the hospital paper himself or heard its contents from someone else. Kagami told her he had been given the folded notice at the hospital and had read the name and time on it. He had not spoken to a doctor or seen the medical file.

“Then I should not say the hospital confirmed more than the page says,” she said.

“Correct.”

The secretary typed the statement while Sato watched the wording. They removed the phrase “died after the attack,” because the paper did not state a cause or establish a sequence beyond the recorded time. The statement said that the headmistress had been informed Kuze had died and that the investigation remained ongoing.

A trustee asked whether this would alarm the parents. The headmistress said the news itself would alarm them; a more confident sentence would not make it less true or more verified. She signed the version they had agreed on. Kagami did not sign it, because the document was the headmistress’s announcement, not his personal account.

The reporter who had recorded the first call asked for the school’s source. Sato said the headmistress had received a telephone message and that the written notice had arrived afterward. He refused to give the caller’s private number. The reporter accepted the distinction but asked whether the school could prove the call came from the hospital. Sato said that was being checked.

The room did not grow calmer. It did become clear which sentences belonged to the school and which remained unverified.

The headmistress asked the secretary to preserve the first draft of the statement as well as the signed version. The secretary marked each page with the time it had been saved and the name of the person who approved it. The reporter’s recording was listed separately, because it captured the announcement but not the call that preceded it.

A parent asked whether the notice would be posted online. The headmistress said the school would publish its own statement, but not an image of a document containing private information. The parent objected that withholding the paper would invite suspicion. Sato said the police had custody of the original and the school could not release what it no longer held.

The parent left without agreeing. Her objection was recorded as an objection, not as confirmation that the notice was false.

# Chapter 74: The Public Record

---

The headmistress made the announcement again when Kagami returned.

He had come from the hospital with a written notice folded inside his coat. He placed it on the committee table and asked Sato to record who had handled it. The document stated that Kuze Minato had died at 11:31 p.m. Kagami did not say what he believed about the notice or the cause of death.

The guests heard the time. They heard the name. The press cameras recorded the headmistress reading the public statement from the page.

“Can you confirm that the death occurred at the hospital?” a reporter asked.

“The notice records the time and location,” Kagami said. “The investigation is ongoing.”

“Was he attacked here?”

“We don’t know.”

“Was the extra place connected?”

“We have no evidence that it was.”

“Was the guard attacked?”

“He was injured. He has not identified anyone.”

The questions arrived faster than the answers. Kagami kept the notice folded and did not offer details it did not contain. He asked the reporters to distinguish the hospital’s statement from the police investigation. One reporter asked whether he was trying to protect the school.

“I’m trying to state what the records say.”

“That sounds like a way not to answer.”

“It is an answer about the records.”

The headmistress asked the remaining guests to leave through the main entrance in groups. Sato recorded their names and departure times. The blank place was still unassigned. The extra meal had been discarded. Kuze’s name remained in the guest ledger without a sign-out time.

Himeno asked to see the written notice. Kagami let her read it at the table, with Sato present. She looked for a second page or an attached physician’s signature. There was none in the envelope.

“This says the hospital reported his death,” she said. “It doesn’t say what happened on the stairs.”

“That’s right,” Kagami said.

“Then don’t let anyone say it does.”

He asked Sato to add her objection to the event record. Sato did. He wrote that the notice had been received, its contents read aloud, and its limits noted. Himeno signed beneath the entry, not as a witness to the death but as a witness to the document.

At midnight, the police took custody of the service-door key, the two camera recordings and the sealed notebook. Kagami gave them his statement without removing the parts that made him look careless. He had allowed the guests to remain in the hall. He had not closed the outer doors. He had asked the server to stay with Sato. Those facts appeared in the transcript.

A reporter asked whether he regretted not shutting down the banquet earlier.

“I don’t know that closing the hall would have prevented what happened,” Kagami said.

The clip that appeared online removed the question. Its caption read: KAGAMI: “I DON’T KNOW.” The quotation was exact. The implication was not.

The last guests left at 12:26 a.m. Ishikawa signed his name on the departure sheet and wrote, “Statement to follow.” He did not make a public accusation. Before leaving, he asked Sato for a copy of the event timeline and the written notice. Sato said the police would decide what could be shared.

Himeno remained until the committee room was empty. She had kept Kuze’s envelope sealed. The headmistress offered to hold it in the office safe. Himeno declined.

“It was given to me,” she said. “I’ll keep it until I understand what he asked.”

“You don’t have to decide tonight.”

“I know.”

She went home with Wataru. The banquet hall’s lights remained on after they had left. Through the glass, the blank place card was still visible in its sleeve, separate from the death notice.

Before the final guests left, Sato asked each reporter to identify which parts of the statement they had recorded directly and which had been repeated from the headmistress. One reporter had filmed the announcement; another had arrived after it. Neither had heard the hospital call. The headmistress agreed to let the school publish the text she had read, but not the private contact details on the notice.

The statement named Kuze and repeated the reported time. It did not name a cause, identify a person responsible, or connect the death to the blank place. Kagami asked the headmistress to read the last sentence once more before the cameras stopped. She did. The reporters wrote it down, though one asked whether the school was hiding a second page.

“No second page was given to us,” Kagami said. “That is what I can attest to.”

Sato logged the recording as a record of what the public had been told, not as independent proof of the hospital's account. The distinction was added to the copy that went to the police.

The next morning, Himeno called each person who had been present when Kuze gave her the envelope. One had seen him hand it over; another remembered the envelope on her desk but had not watched the exchange. She wrote both recollections in the custody note and asked each person to sign only the words they had supplied.

Sato photographed the sealed edge before Himeno opened it. He placed a ruler beside the flap for scale and recorded the camera’s date and time. The envelope had no return address. A faint crease crossed the back, but neither witness remembered whether it had been there the night before. Himeno did not treat the crease as a mark of tampering.

After she read the letter, she asked Sato to make two copies: one for the inventory and one for the police file. The original returned to her. She wrote the transfer time on a separate receipt and kept the paper with the notebooks, not inside Kuze’s final volume. The letter’s instruction applied to the collection; it did not establish who had written every loose page in it.

The cabinet held a second set of labels, each showing the date on which one notebook had been added. Four dates matched the committee archive log. Two did not. The secretary said those volumes had been stored before the log began. No one could confirm who placed them there.

Himeno asked whether the board could inspect the inventory without taking the notebooks. The headmistress said she would ask the board’s clerk to make a written request. Himeno agreed to provide the titles and page counts, but not to lend an unlogged volume. The distinction left the cabinet closed while allowing the board to know what it contained.

Before the room was closed for the night, Sato asked the secretary to read back the event log. It listed the 10:17 p.m. report, the 10:23 p.m. door check, the ambulance departure, the two hospital calls and the headmistress’s announcement. He asked her to keep the source beside each time: hall clock, caller’s account, or written notice. The times did not all describe the same event, and the list did not turn them into a single sequence.

Himeno checked that Kuze’s name remained on the guest ledger without a departure time. She checked that the unassigned place remained labeled as a place, not a guest. The school could close the hall; it could not close the questions with it.

# Chapter 75: The Inheritance

---

By morning, the first headline had already become the version most people remembered.

STUDENT INVESTIGATOR DIES AFTER QUESTIONING ASTER’S MISSING PLACE.

The article beneath it said the extra chair might have been intended for Kuze. It cited no source for the claim. A second report copied the headline and dropped the word “might.” By noon, a third called the blank place the cause of the attack.

Himeno read each version at the committee table. “The place wasn’t his. We don’t know who it was for.”

Kagami sat opposite her with the signed notice and the event timeline. “I’ll request a correction.”

“Request?”

“I can’t make them publish it.”

“You can publish the full record.”

“After the police approve what can be released.”

“They’ll use the gap while we wait.”

“Yes.”

He did not defend the delay. He wrote down the request and sent it through the proper channel. The answer came back that the incident remained under investigation. No additional record could be released yet.

A second message arrived from the newspaper. It asked Kagami whether he had known Kuze was in danger. The reporter quoted his earlier warning that Ren had invited him to be a witness. The line was accurate. In print, it looked like evidence that Kagami had sent him into the corridor.

Himeno pushed the article away. “You warned him.”

“I did.”

“You didn’t tell him to go.”

“No.”

“They don’t care about the difference.”

“They asked what I said. I’ll show them the whole exchange.”

The full transcript included Kuze’s answer: “I’m going as a witness.” It also included Kagami’s warning that a witness could be placed where someone wanted him. The article used only the first sentence.

Kagami did not ask the newspaper to erase the quote. He sent the complete transcript and asked them to correct the implication. They published a link to the transcript below the original story. The headline remained unchanged.

Himeno put Kuze’s sealed envelope between them. “He said I decide.”

“Then you decide.”

She broke the seal. Sato photographed the envelope, front and back, before she removed the letter. The note inside was brief:

The notebooks are yours if I cannot return.

Do not publish a page before you check the pages beside it.

If they tell you that I knew, ask them what I knew.

At the bottom was Kuze’s signature and the date, written three days before the banquet.

Himeno read it twice. Then she used the key to open the committee cabinet. Six notebooks sat inside, along with copied receipts, a list of people who had handled the festival records, and one thin folder marked “Unconfirmed.” The first page of the folder listed the false sister, the missing guest-book page, the blue thread, the two messages, and the blank place. Beside every item, Kuze had written what was known and what was not.

“He didn’t leave an answer,” Wataru said.

“He left the questions in order.”

A loose page was filed inside the final notebook, dated the morning after the banquet. Its first sentence stopped halfway through: If they use the recording, compare the clock before—

The ink had not been compared with Kuze’s other notes. No one knew when the page was written or who had placed it among the notebooks. No one filled in the rest.

Sato catalogued the notebooks and made a copy of the inventory. Himeno kept the originals in the locked cabinet. She did not let the police take them without a receipt, and she did not let the reporters photograph the pages.

At the bottom of the cabinet, a folded list recorded who had held the key in the previous month. The final line had no name, only a date: the afternoon before the banquet. The access log did not say who had opened the cabinet that day.

Himeno copied the line into her own notebook. Then she closed Kuze’s book on the unfinished sentence.

Before Himeno left the committee room, the secretary asked her to initial the inventory for the six notebooks. Himeno checked each cover against the list and found that the fourth volume had a torn corner not mentioned in the earlier copy. The tear was old; paper fibers had darkened along the edge. She did not know when it happened.

Sato added the condition to the inventory as an observation made that morning. He did not write that it had been torn at the banquet. The notebook had been handled at least twice since then, once by the police and once during the cabinet count, and the order of those transfers was written on separate receipts.

Wataru asked whether the notebooks were safe in the cabinet. Himeno said they were safer than loose on a table, but the lock did not identify everyone who had used a key. She requested that the headmistress authorize a new access sheet and that anyone with a duplicate key list it. The headmistress agreed to check the office register.

They did not find a key missing. The register showed three authorized copies, one with the headmistress, one with the committee secretary and one in the office safe. It did not say whether anyone had made an unauthorized copy. Himeno wrote “three recorded” rather than “three exist.”

The cabinet shut with a soft click. The receipt and inventory went into a separate folder, so neither could be mistaken for Kuze’s notes. The notebooks stayed where he had kept them, and the questions stayed open.

The empty place had not acquired a name. The record of who had opened the cabinet had not acquired one either.

Himeno asked the secretary to compare the remaining pages in the folder with the inventory. The blue-thread note was present, but the separate photograph of the lantern was not. The secretary remembered sending the photograph to Sato and found the sent message on the committee account. She wrote the time of the message on the inventory and did not print the image again.

The folder’s contents could be accounted for, though not every person who had handled them could be named. Himeno marked the photograph as transferred to the police file and asked Sato for a receipt number. He gave her one. It was a small answer, but it kept the image from becoming an item that had simply vanished.

Before leaving the cabinet, Himeno and Sato counted the notebooks by their covers and checked each title against Kuze's inventory. Six volumes were present. The folder marked Unconfirmed was present. The sealed envelope had been returned to Himeno after the letter was photographed. Sato wrote the time and the names of both people who checked the shelf.

Himeno asked him to sign a receipt stating that the notebooks had not been transferred to police custody. He wrote that the police held photographs and copies, while the originals remained in the committee cabinet under Himeno's key. She signed beneath him. The receipt did not make her the owner of every page; it made her responsible for keeping the collection in order until the authority to move it was clear.

When she closed the cabinet, she left the inventory sheet on the outside of the door. Anyone requesting a volume would have to write a name, time and reason. The request did not resolve the blank place, the corridor or the unfinished sentence. It established who would be accountable for the next copy.
