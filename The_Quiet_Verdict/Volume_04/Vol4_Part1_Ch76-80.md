## Arc 2 Recap: The Hunt

*At Aster’s festival banquet, thirty-two guests signed the ledger; one of thirty-three places remained blank, and one meal was left unclaimed. Kuze was reported missing from the hall and later reported dead by the headmistress, who cited a hospital notice. The service corridor and its records remain under investigation. Kuze’s friend Himeno has his sealed letter and notebooks. Kagami’s careful public statements are already being quoted without their context.*

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event follows within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the writer’s own handwriting and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not state how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows.*

# Chapter 76: The First Correction

---

The newspaper corrected one sentence and left its headline unchanged.

On Saturday morning, the front page still read: STUDENT INVESTIGATOR DIES AFTER QUESTIONING ASTER’S MISSING PLACE. Beneath it, a new line said that police had not established a connection between the place and the incident in the service corridor. The old version remained in the paper’s archive, and a copy of it had been sent to every parent on the school list.

Himeno laid both printouts side by side in the committee room. “They corrected the paragraph. They kept the accusation in the largest type.”

Kagami read the two versions before answering. “They corrected what they were willing to correct.”

“That’s not enough.”

“No.”

He had brought a folder of transcripts, a copy of the guest ledger with private addresses covered, and a statement from the Foundation that the event records had been preserved. He had not brought the hospital notice. Sato had told him the police had custody of the original and were not releasing it.

“Then we say only what we can show,” Himeno said.

“That’s what I’m doing.”

“What does the school get to say?”

“The same.”

The headmistress sat by the window with a list of parents who had asked for a meeting. Some wanted the school to suspend the festival committee. Others wanted Kagami removed from the investigation. A few wanted the full incident report, which did not yet exist.

“Can you tell them the hospital notice was genuine?” she asked.

“I can say I saw a notice and that it was delivered to the committee,” Kagami said. “I can’t authenticate a document the police have taken.”

“Can you tell them what it said?”

“I can say the headmistress read it aloud. The police have not confirmed the details to me.”

The headmistress looked at the page in front of her. “That will sound evasive.”

“It is narrower than what I want to tell them.”

The answer made Himeno look up. It was the first time Kagami had said what he wanted. He did not explain further.

A parent called through the open door. She had seen a clip of the briefing in which Kagami said the school had no evidence an unidentified person entered the hall. The caption beneath it read: INVESTIGATOR CANNOT RULE OUT UNINVITED GUEST.

“Can he rule it out?” she asked.

“No,” Kagami said. “But that isn’t what I said.”

“Then why not say there wasn’t one?”

“Because I don’t know that.”

The parent’s face tightened. “You don’t know who was in the room you were guarding?”

“I know who signed the guest ledger. I know what the staff list recorded. I don’t know whether someone entered without signing.”

“Then the school wasn’t safe.”

Kagami did not answer at once. “I can’t promise the hall was safe just because I was present.”

The parent left without taking the transcript.

The next parent waited until the corridor had cleared. She had a copy of the article folded in half, with the headline facing inward.

“My son was at the banquet,” she said. “He told me he saw Kuze leave the room.”

Himeno looked at the printed list. “Did he see which way Kuze went?”

“He said toward the service corridor.”

“Did he see him enter it?”

The parent hesitated. “He saw him stand up. Then the lights went out for the film.”

“That’s different.”

“I know.” She unfolded the article. “I just want to know whether that matters.”

“It matters if someone says he saw Kuze in the corridor. It doesn’t make your son’s memory useless.”

The parent asked whether the school would take a statement from him. Sato said the police already had a list of witnesses and would contact the students through their families. He would not question the boy in the hallway or ask him to repeat his account for a camera.

“Will you tell me what he said?”

“Not without his permission,” Sato replied. “But I can make sure the right person asks him.”

The parent folded the article again, more carefully this time. She left without asking for a promise about what the statement would prove.

Kagami watched her go. “You could have told her the distinction earlier.”

“I could have,” Himeno said. “She needed to know why we were asking.”

The parent returned at the door. “If the police ask him, I want to be there.”

Sato wrote that request down. No one called it evidence. It was a parent asking to sit beside her child.

The headmistress closed the door. “You could have said the list was complete.”

“It was complete as a list of people who signed in.”

“You could have used the more reassuring sentence.”

“It would not have been true.”

Himeno put the two newspaper versions into the folder. “And now the true sentence will be used to make the worse one sound true.”

“It already is.”

At noon, the school posted the full transcript and a list of records being preserved. The post separated four categories: signed-in guests, staff, places set, and meals prepared. It did not call the blank place a guest. It did not call the hospital notice a verified medical record. The school included the exact correction the newspaper had printed, then linked both versions of the article.

A student from the festival committee asked whether the school was making the story bigger by posting it.

“It’s already bigger,” Himeno said. “This tells people where the edges are.”

“Will they read it?”

“Some will.”

“And the rest?”

“We can’t write their version for them.”

Kagami watched the post go live. He checked the numbers one last time: thirty-two names, thirty-three places, thirty-three meals. One meal had gone unserved. The categories remained separate.

A notification appeared on the school’s account. The paper’s social post had been shared hundreds of times, with the correction cropped out of the image. Below it, a new comment named Kagami as the person who had “let Kuze walk into a trap.”

The comment quoted his warning from the week before: I won’t stop you from going, but I won’t pretend the choice is safe.

Himeno read it over his shoulder. “You said that privately.”

“I said it in the committee room. There were four people there.”

“Who told them?”

“I don’t know.”

“Was it on the record?”

“No.”

The comment had been posted by an account with no name and no photograph. It did not say who had supplied the quotation. Kagami saved a screenshot, along with the time and the link, then closed the page.

The correction had appeared. The warning had appeared too.

That evening, Himeno read the replies to the school’s post without responding to them. One parent asked whether Kuze’s name had been removed from the official guest list. Another asked if the school had changed the count after the incident. The secretary drafted answers from the signed copies: Kuze’s name remained in the entry ledger; the total of signed guests had not been changed; the departure column had a blank beside his name.

Himeno asked that the school not answer the second question with “no one changed anything.” The committee had amended the list before the banquet, and the paper trail should show that. The reply instead said that the original and corrected copies were preserved, with the date and reason for each change noted.

Some commenters called that evasive. The secretary saved those replies too, but did not treat the criticism as evidence that the records were false.

Before the school closed its post for comments, the secretary exported the public responses. Some repeated the newspaper headline; others quoted the correction. Several posts used the same cropped image, though the accounts had different names. The school could not tell whether people had copied one another or received a common source.

Himeno asked for a list of the posts that contained the altered warning. The secretary made one, preserving the links and times. She did not attempt to identify the anonymous account from its profile picture, which was a blank circle. The page showed circulation, not authorship.

Kagami read the list and asked that the school not contact the accounts directly. The board had not authorized an investigation of the posters, and the police had not asked him to identify them. The saved material could be handed over if requested. For now, the school had only a record of what had been published.

# Chapter 77: The Selective Quote

---

The board asked Kagami to attend without Sato.

The meeting was held in the old music room, where the windows looked down on the courtyard and the chairs were arranged in a semicircle. The headmistress sat beside the Foundation chair. Three trustees and a parent representative occupied the other seats. No one had brought a recorder. Kagami asked whether the minutes would be kept.

“They will,” the chair said.

“Then I’ll answer on the record.”

The chair nodded. The secretary opened a notebook.

The first question concerned the warning he had given Kuze. The parent representative had printed the sentence from the anonymous post: I won’t stop you from going, but I won’t pretend the choice is safe.

“Did you tell him that?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you stop him?”

“He was an adult participant in a public event. I had no authority to detain him.”

“You could have told the headmistress.”

“I told her the event needed a clear staff roster and access log.”

“That isn’t the same as saying a student was in danger.”

“I had no evidence that Kuze was specifically in danger.”

The parent representative looked at the chair. “He admits he knew the choice wasn’t safe.”

“I said I wouldn’t pretend it was safe. That was a warning about uncertainty, not knowledge of a threat.”

“Couldn’t you have said that more clearly?”

“I could have said more. I did not know what more was true.”

The secretary wrote steadily. The chair asked whether Kagami had personally checked every door in the hall. He said no. He had checked the entrances assigned to him. The security team had checked the others. The maintenance door’s key log showed one checkout at 6:12, but the door had later been found open. The hall’s camera did not cover the stairwell.

“Then you didn’t know who entered.”

“I knew who signed in. I didn’t know whether someone else entered.”

“Would you use that language in a public statement?”

“I already did.”

The chair folded his hands. “Kagami, people are using your honesty to suggest you failed to do your job.”

“It may be true that I failed. The record should be able to show that.”

The parent representative shifted in her chair. “You’re not defending yourself.”

“I’m answering you.”

“That isn’t enough.”

“No,” Kagami said. “It isn’t.”

The meeting ran for forty minutes. Kagami corrected two errors in the minutes before signing: he had not described the maintenance door as secured, and he had not said the guest list was complete. The secretary initialed both changes. The board thanked him, then asked him to surrender his temporary access badge while the school reviewed its procedures.

He placed it on the table.

The decision did not remove him from the police inquiry, but it barred him from the committee room and the archive office.

Before the board concluded, Kagami asked the chair to state the restriction precisely. Would he be barred from school grounds, from the committee records, or from all contact with the students?

“Only the rooms listed in the notice,” the chair said. “You may still speak with the police.”

“May I contact witnesses who have already given statements?”

“Not on behalf of the school.”

“May I answer questions if they contact me?”

The chair looked to the solicitor. She said the board could not prevent a voluntary conversation, but Kagami should not use school records or imply that he was acting for Aster.

“Put that in the minutes,” Kagami said.

The secretary wrote it down. He asked her to read the sentence aloud. It said he could speak with police independently, but could not access school records or represent the institution. He corrected “could not investigate” to “was not authorized to investigate on behalf of the school.” The board accepted the narrower wording.

The parent representative said the distinction would confuse people.

“It may,” Kagami said. “But it is the distinction you decided.”

The chair read the final notice and asked if Kagami understood. He said he did. He did not ask the board to withdraw it. He asked for a copy with the time it took effect and the name of the person who held his badge.

The secretary signed the receipt. The board’s decision took away his access, not his right to give an account. A trustee walked him to the main entrance and asked him to leave the campus until the board met again.

On the walk to the gate, the trustee asked Kagami whether he would tell the reporters that the board had suspended his access. Kagami said he would state the restriction as written and would not describe it as a finding about the banquet. The trustee said that distinction might be lost in the coverage.

“It may be,” Kagami replied. “But the notice is still specific.”

At the gate, he asked for permission to take a photograph of the badge receipt. The trustee agreed. Kagami saved the image with the board’s notice and did not post it. He wanted the record available if anyone later said he had returned the badge voluntarily or been dismissed from the police inquiry.

In the courtyard, reporters waited by the gate. One asked whether he had been removed for withholding information. Another asked whether he had admitted responsibility for Kuze’s death.

“I was asked to surrender my school access badge during a review,” Kagami said. “I have not been told that I am responsible for what happened.”

“Do you think you should have prevented it?”

“I think I should have asked for a clearer door check.”

The answer appeared in the evening report as: KAGAMI ADMITS SECURITY FAILURE.

The words were not wholly false. He had said the door check should have been clearer. The article did not mention that the maintenance-door log showed a single checkout and no later entry, or that he had not controlled the camera system.

Himeno found him outside the gate, where he was waiting for Sato to collect him. “You gave them the sentence.”

“I gave them the answer.”

“They’ll use it.”

“They already did.”

“Does it bother you?”

He looked at the school badge in his hand. “It changes what I can do here.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“It’s the part I can act on.”

She walked with him to the curb. “If you tell them every weakness, they’ll call the weakness the whole story.”

“If I hide it, they’ll call that the story.”

“You can still choose what to tell them.”

“I did.”

Sato’s car pulled up. Kagami opened the passenger door, then turned back to Himeno. “Keep the original notes separate from the copies. If anyone asks to see them, write down who asked and when.”

“I know.”

“I’m not giving you an order.”

“I know that too.”

The car pulled away. Himeno watched it turn at the gate. On the board’s notice, her own name appeared below Kagami’s: requested to provide copies of Kuze’s committee notes. The request had no explanation or deadline.

She folded the notice and put it in her bag.

The board secretary gave Kagami the signed minutes before he left the campus. The copy included his corrections, the access restriction and the name of the trustee who had received his badge. It did not include the parent representative's summary of his answers; that had been entered separately as her view.

He read every page at the gate. One line described him as having failed to secure the maintenance door. He had said he did not know whether the door had been secured, and asked for that wording to replace the sentence. The secretary checked the recording and agreed to amend the minutes. The revised page was dated, initialed and attached without removing the earlier version.

Himeno kept a copy of both. The correction did not make the first page disappear. It showed what the board had changed and when.

# Chapter 78: The Cost of a Fact

---

The school’s statement had two versions by the end of the day.

The first was the one the board approved. It said the investigation was ongoing, the event logs had been preserved, and the school would cooperate with police. The second was a draft that had been sent to the parent mailing list before the headmistress could review it. It said the board had “removed Kagami from the investigation.”

The board had not done that. It had taken his campus badge.

Himeno printed both versions and circled the difference. The second email had been forwarded from an account used by the festival committee. The sender said she had copied the wording from a message in the parent group. No one could tell who had written the original line.

“Do we correct it?” the headmistress asked.

“Yes,” Himeno said. “And we say exactly what changed.”

The secretary drafted a correction. The headmistress wanted to remove the phrase “temporary access restriction,” because it sounded like punishment. Himeno asked her to leave it in. The board had restricted access; a softer word would not undo the decision.

The final statement said Kagami was not removed from the investigation, but his school access had been suspended during a procedural review. It did not say whether the board trusted him. No one had asked it to.

The parent mailing list accepted the correction. The forwarded version kept circulating.

The headmistress asked Himeno to help compare the two emails. The first correction had gone to the entire parent list. The forwarded version contained a line that was not in the original: Kagami had been dismissed from the case after admitting he failed to secure the hall.

“Who added that?” Himeno asked.

The sender showed the message on her phone. It had come from a parent group, forwarded three times. The first sender’s address was hidden by the app. No one could tell whether the wording began as a deliberate lie or a careless paraphrase.

The headmistress wrote to the group administrator and asked for the correction to be pinned beside the forwarded message. The administrator agreed, but said she could not delete screenshots already saved to phones.

“Then ask them to add a note when they share it,” Himeno said.

The administrator did. Some parents added the correction. Others reposted the earlier image without it. By midafternoon, both versions were being shared under the same school crest.

Himeno created a table with four columns: original wording, altered wording, source, and what could be verified. She left the source field blank when no source could be traced. She would not fill it with a guess just to make the row look complete.

Sato glanced at the table. “That’s a lot of work for a rumor.”

“It’s a lot of work because the rumor keeps changing.”

He signed the receipt for the copies and took the table to the police liaison. The paper trail did not stop the next version. It did show which parts had changed.

At lunch, two students argued over whether Kagami had been fired. One said the board had confirmed he was at fault. The other said he had only lost his badge. Their phones showed different screenshots.

Himeno sat down between them. “The original notice says his badge was suspended. It doesn’t say he was fired.”

“Why would they suspend it if he did nothing?” one student asked.

“We don’t know that he did nothing.”

“So he might be guilty?”

“He might have made a mistake. That’s not the same as being guilty of causing what happened.”

The student looked at the screenshots. “Everyone keeps saying they don’t know.”

“Then keep asking what each person actually knows.”

The other student turned his phone face down. “That takes longer.”

“Yes,” Himeno said. “It does.”

That afternoon, Sato brought her a receipt for the notebook copies. Each volume had been photographed with Himeno present, then returned to the locked cabinet. The digital files were stored by the police. The paper originals remained with her, as Kuze had asked.

“Did they request the originals?” she asked.

“Not yet. If they do, we’ll take a signed receipt.”

“Will the copies include every page?”

“They include every page in the six notebooks. The sealed inner envelope is separate.”

“There was an inner envelope?”

Sato paused. “A folded sheet, fixed inside the back cover of the last notebook. The officer found it during the inventory and photographed it. It has not been opened.”

“Why wasn’t I told?”

“It was found after I left. The officer is preserving it.”

“Can I see the photograph?”

“I can request it.”

He wrote down the request and gave her a copy. Himeno watched the time go onto the page. “You’re learning.”

“I’ve been doing this for a while.”

“You’re learning to make it visible.”

Sato recognized the words from Ishikawa and gave a tired smile. “He says that about everything.”

The photograph arrived an hour later. The folded sheet was held shut with a narrow band of clear tape. No writing could be read through it. The tape’s edge was marked with a date from the day before the banquet.

Himeno asked who had taped it. The inventory had no name beside that action. The police would check the original for fingerprints, but Sato cautioned that a result might not identify when the tape had been applied.

“Could the sheet be a letter?” Wataru asked.

“It could.”

“Could it be a list?”

“It could.”

“Could it be empty?”

“It could.”

Wataru looked at her. “That answer is contagious.”

“It’s in the photograph.”

She wrote three possibilities beside the request number, then underlined: unknown. She did not ask the police to break the seal for her. The notebook’s warning was clear: check the pages beside it before publishing a page. The sheet belonged with the sequence, not by itself.

At five, a messenger delivered a formal request from the board. It asked for access to Kuze’s notes “relevant to the safety of the school.” The request did not say who would read them or whether they would be copied.

Himeno signed for the envelope and wrote the time. She did not hand over the notebooks.

The request had an authorized seal. It had no named investigator.

Himeno took the request to the records room and checked the date against the previous day’s mail log. The seal matched the Foundation’s, and the delivery signature matched the office clerk’s. No page showed who had drafted the letter or which trustee had approved the request.

She asked the clerk to call the board office for the authorization minute. The office said it would locate the record and send it later. Himeno wrote the clerk’s name and the time of the call beside the request. A valid seal showed where the letter had come from; it did not answer who would take responsibility for its scope.

The headmistress asked Himeno whether Kuze had told her where he kept the notebooks. Himeno showed her the dated letter, but not the pages themselves. The letter assigned responsibility if he could not return; it did not establish why he had expected the possibility or who else knew of the cabinet.

The headmistress read the sentence twice, then returned the letter without asking for a copy. She said the board might request one. Himeno asked that the request be made in writing, with the name of the person who would receive it. The headmistress agreed.

Wataru asked if that meant the headmistress was a suspect. Himeno said it meant the headmistress had read the letter. A reader was not an author, and an authorized request was not proof of access. She wrote down the meeting time and added the letter's location to the inventory.

# Chapter 79: The Pages Beside It

---

Himeno did not open the sealed sheet.

She started with the pages around it. The last notebook had sixty-three numbered leaves, but only sixty-one pages of writing. Two blank pages separated the entries from the folded sheet. The first blank page was clean. The second carried a faint crease that continued beneath the taped edge.

She photographed the sequence with Sato present and wrote down the page numbers. The sheet was not removed. The police had said it should remain sealed until they completed their examination. Himeno agreed, but asked that the adjoining pages be included in the record.

The notebook’s entries were not a secret diary. They were lists of who had handled a document, what time it had been moved and whether a statement had been checked.

Before turning the page, Himeno checked the notebook’s binding and the numbered tabs. One tab had been moved from the sixth leaf to the seventh. Kuze’s index still referred to the sixth. The tab might have slipped during handling; it might have been moved when the pages were copied. She did not decide which.

Sato compared the order with the photographs already in police custody. The overview images showed the tabs but not their edges clearly. He asked for the original-resolution files rather than enlarging a compressed copy. Until those arrived, Himeno kept the page references as Kuze had written them and added a note that the physical tab was in a different position. Kuze had crossed out his own mistakes in red pencil. Beside several uncertain times, he had written “estimate,” then added the source in the next column.

“He made a record of being wrong,” Wataru said.

“He made a record of what he knew when he wrote it.”

“Which is different?”

“It’s the difference between a correction and a lie.”

She found the entry for the service-door camera. The hall clock was four minutes ahead of the camera clock, not behind it. She checked the copy against the event technician’s calibration slip. The slip showed the camera at 8:41 when the hall clock read 8:45.

Kuze had written the comparison two hours before the banquet ended. He had not been working from the footage afterward. The notebook noted the discrepancy and gave the technician’s name.

“Then the clip time is earlier than the hall time,” Wataru said.

“Yes.”

“Could that make it look as if someone crossed the corridor before the door opened?”

“It could make the clocks disagree. We need the original file and the calibration.”

Sato confirmed the police had both. The committee’s copy was not enough to reconstruct what the camera saw. The footage had been exported once, and the export record did not specify whether its timestamp came from the camera or the server.

Himeno wrote that down, then closed the notebook. “No conclusion until we have the original.”

The page before the service-door entry contained a list of names who had touched the guest book.

Himeno checked the list against the sign-in sheets. A volunteer’s name appeared at 8:31, though she had told Sato she arrived at 8:40. Kuze had marked the earlier time as “from door log,” and the later one as “from memory.” Neither entry had been crossed out.

“She could be wrong about when she arrived,” Wataru said.

“Or the door clock could be wrong.”

“Could the log be right?”

“It could.”

They found the volunteer in the library and asked if she was willing to clarify. She remembered arriving after the first course, but could not remember whether the wall clock had shown 8:31 or 8:41. She had signed the sheet before putting on her armband. The armband had been handed to her by a student whose name she did not know.

Himeno added a note to the record: the sign-in time was written, the remembered time was approximate, and the person who gave her the armband was unidentified. She did not change either time.

“Does that help?” the volunteer asked.

“It tells us which part you remember.”

The volunteer glanced at the open notebook. “I thought I was supposed to tell you what happened.”

“You did.”

“But I don’t know which time is right.”

“Then the honest statement includes that.”

The volunteer thanked her, though she looked no more certain than before. The contradiction did not place her near the service door. It made the sign-in sheet less exact than its ruled columns suggested. Kuze had marked each one with a time and a reason. The clerk’s name appeared twice. The headmistress appeared once. Ishikawa’s name was followed by a note: invitation returned to recipient; no inference from this alone.

“He knew people would read into the phrase,” Wataru said.

“He knew they might.”

“Why not write what he thought it meant?”

“Maybe he did somewhere else.”

“Or maybe he didn’t know.”

Himeno looked at the next page. It listed the blank place card, the unserved meal, the false sister and the girl who delivered the lantern. Each item had a source column and a question column. The question beside the lantern read: Who knew I would be the one to receive it?

The question beside the blank place read: Was it an instruction, an error, or an invitation whose name was withheld?

No answer followed either line.

At the bottom of the page, Kuze had written, “Do not let matching numbers make separate events into one event.” The sentence referred to two count sheets from different days. It did not say what the counts were meant to conceal.

Himeno copied it into her notebook. Wataru watched her add the source and page number.

“You’re following his method.”

“I’m following his instruction.”

“Is that different?”

“I don’t know yet.”

They came to the leaf immediately before the folded sheet. It contained no names, only a series of times: 8:44, 8:47, 8:49, 9:26, 11:14, 11:38. The first three had a question mark beside them. The later three were in a different ink.

“Those last times are after he was taken to the hospital,” Wataru said.

“They are the times recorded by people in the hall.”

“Who added them?”

“The ink is different. That doesn’t tell us who held the pen.”

The public report had placed the notice at 11:31. The committee record showed the first hospital call at 11:14 and the headmistress’s announcement at 11:38. The notebook did not reconcile them. It simply held both times beside each other.

Himeno checked the signature at the bottom. It was Kuze’s. The final line had been written in the same blue ink as his earlier notes: If I cannot check the next entry, do not let the time become a fact by repetition.

Wataru read it over her shoulder. “That sounds like him.”

“It sounds like something he wrote.”

“Those aren’t the same?”

“They can be. They don’t have to be.”

She closed the notebook and placed it back in the cabinet. The folded sheet remained inside the back cover. She locked the cabinet and recorded the time, then wrote down who had watched her do it.

In the hallway, the school secretary waited with the board’s request for the notebooks. Himeno gave her a written reply: access could be arranged for a named reviewer, with a receipt, and no pages could be removed or copied without recording the request.

The secretary read it and asked, “Who are you protecting?”

“The record.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No,” Himeno said. “It wasn’t.”

# Chapter 80: The Review

---

The board sent a named reviewer the next morning.

Her name was Ms. Kagawa. She was an outside solicitor retained by the Foundation, not a police officer. She brought a letter authorizing her to inspect the festival committee’s records, but not to take them. Himeno read the letter, copied its first page and asked the headmistress to confirm the seal.

The seal was genuine. The scope was not clear.

“Which records do you want?” Himeno asked.

“The material relating to the banquet and the incident afterward.”

“That could include every notebook.”

“It could.”

“Do you want the pages about the false sister, the missing guest-book sheet and the lantern?”

“If they bear on the incident.”

“Who decides whether they bear on it?”

“I do, after I see them.”

Himeno turned to the headmistress. “The request names a reviewer but gives her the power to decide the scope after she sees the material.”

“That is why she is here,” the headmistress said. “We need someone independent.”

“Independent of whom?”

Ms. Kagawa folded her hands. “I understand the concern. I can list every page I inspect and explain why I considered it relevant.”

“Will the board get copies?”

“Only if the board requests them.”

“Will you tell us before you provide them?”

“I can agree to notify you.”

“Can you agree not to remove a page?”

“Yes.”

Himeno wrote the conditions on a separate sheet. Ms. Kagawa read them and signed. The headmistress witnessed the signature. The review began with the guest-count records, not Kuze’s notebooks.

The count sheets agreed on thirty-two signed guests, thirty-three set places and thirty-three prepared meals. The empty place had no assigned name. The meal had been left unserved. The extra seat and meal remained unexplained, but the numbers themselves were not disputed.

“Do you consider the place an unidentified guest?” Ms. Kagawa asked.

“No,” Himeno said. “It is an unassigned place.”

“Could it have been for a person who never arrived?”

“It could.”

“Could it have been a bookkeeping error?”

“It could.”

“Could it have been deliberately left blank?”

“It could.”

Ms. Kagawa looked up from her notes. “You don’t have a preferred explanation?”

“I do. I’m not putting it in the record as a fact.”

“What is your preferred explanation?”

“That someone wanted us to notice the blank.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know.”

The reviewer wrote the answer down without comment. She then examined the list of people who had handled the seating plan. The initials were legible. The instruction about the possible late guest appeared only in the committee secretary’s statement. No document named a person for the extra place.

When she reached the notebook inventory, Himeno did not unlock the cabinet immediately. “The last notebook contains a sealed sheet. The police have photographed it but not opened it. It remains in place.”

“Can I see the notebook?”

“You can inspect the outside and the adjoining pages, under the conditions we signed.”

Ms. Kagawa agreed. Himeno opened the cabinet, removed the notebook and laid it on the table. The reviewer examined the cover, the page numbers and the entries surrounding the folded sheet. She did not touch the tape. Every page she read was recorded on the inspection log.

At the list of times, she stopped. “Why does the hospital notice appear at 11:31 when the first call was at 11:14?”

“The public notice came later,” Himeno said. “The headmistress announced the report at 11:38. The paper we saw records 11:31 as the time of death, but it has not been released for independent review.”

“Do you believe the times conflict?”

“I believe they describe different events. The call, the time written on the notice, and the announcement are not the same thing.”

“Can the hospital confirm that?”

“Not to me.”

Ms. Kagawa wrote that down and asked to inspect the police copy. Himeno said she did not have it. The notebook stayed open at the page with the times, each entry separated by a line.

The reviewer’s phone rang. She stepped into the corridor to answer.

Before opening the notebook to the final leaf, Ms. Kagawa asked Himeno to turn the page herself. She wanted the movement recorded and the spine supported. Himeno placed a clean sheet beneath the cover and turned it by the corner, without touching the folded insert.

The page numbers ran in order. The paper was not torn. A pale strip of adhesive crossed the inside of the back cover where the folded sheet met it. Ms. Kagawa photographed the spine, then asked whether the notebook had been opened before it was sealed by police.

“Not by me,” Himeno said. “I saw it on the stairs, then the officer bagged it.”

“Did you see the last pages?”

“No. I was kept at the corridor entrance.”

“Did anyone tell you it was open?”

“Sato said it was found open.”

The distinction went into the log: Himeno had not seen the notebook open at the scene; she had heard that it was open. The officer’s photograph would be needed to establish the position of the pages before recovery.

Ms. Kagawa asked that no one write directly on the inspection sheet. She numbered the images and recorded the camera used. When she stepped out to answer her call, the notebook remained open beneath the clear sleeve, with Himeno’s hands on the table and away from the page.

Nothing moved. The log recorded the pause anyway. The door remained open. Himeno did not look at the notebook until Ms. Kagawa returned.

The second page beside the folded sheet contained a single added line, in ink different from Kuze’s: Ask who changed the clock.

Himeno had not noticed it before.

Ms. Kagawa read the line and looked at the event log. “The camera clock?”

“Maybe,” Himeno said.

“Who changed it?”

“The technician calibrated it before the banquet. Kuze recorded the difference.”

“Could someone have changed it again?”

“We don’t know.”

The reviewer photographed the line through the open notebook, added it to the page log, and left the sheet sealed. She did not say whether the ink had been there before the notebook was recovered. The page had not been photographed in the corridor before it was bagged.

At the end of the inspection, Ms. Kagawa signed the log and returned the notebook.

She gave Himeno a copy of the inspection record. It listed the page numbers reviewed, the time spent with the notebook, and the one line whose presence had not been established in the earlier photographs. She left the question of the ink to the police examiner.

“Do you think it was added after the book was recovered?” Himeno asked.

“I don’t have a basis to say.”

“Could you tell by looking?”

“Not reliably. The paper can show pressure, but the writing material and the handling history matter. I’m not an examiner.”

She did not offer a confident impression. She did not rule one out. Her report called it “an unexplained annotation observed during this inspection.”

Himeno read the sentence and signed beside it. The reviewer’s careful wording gave the board no answer it could put in a headline. It gave them a line they could not quietly turn into a fact about Kuze’s last hours.

Ms. Kagawa returned the copy of the authorization letter. “If the police release the photographs, I can compare them with today’s images.”

“Will you send us the comparison?”

“I’ll send the board my report. You’ll receive the pages you’re entitled to see.”

Himeno wrote down the promise and its limits. Then she locked the notebook away. She asked the headmistress to authorize a second session after the police released the camera file. The headmistress agreed, pending the board’s approval.

Himeno locked the notebook away. She recorded the time, the people present and the fact that the new line had been seen only after the book reached the cabinet.

Wataru stood in the doorway. “The clock, the blank place, the unclaimed meal. One of them is a message.”

“Maybe.”

“Do you think the line was there before?”

“I think we have a new line.”

“You’re not going to guess?”

“I’m going to find the photograph taken when the notebook was sealed.”

She called Sato. He said the police had photographed the cover and the open page where it had fallen, but he would check whether the adjacent sheet was visible. He would not promise that the angle showed it.

Himeno wrote down his answer and the time of the call. She underlined the question about the clock.

Then she added a second question beneath it: Who had the book before the photograph?
