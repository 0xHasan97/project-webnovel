# Chapter 81: Four Minutes

---

The first photograph did not show the line.

Sato sent Himeno a copy from the police evidence desk, then called to explain the angle. The image showed the notebook open at the stairwell, one page visible, the cover bent beneath it. The facing page was mostly hidden by the spine. The words “Ask who changed the clock” could not be seen.

“Does that mean it wasn’t there?” Wataru asked.

“No,” Himeno said. “It means the photograph doesn’t show it.”

They sat at the committee table with the image on a laptop. Sato had sent the file as an attachment marked for review, not as an official release. Himeno did not save it into the notebook folder. She printed one copy and wrote the sender, time received and file name along the bottom margin.

Wataru leaned toward the screen. “Can you enlarge it?”

“I can enlarge the pixels.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“It won’t make a covered page visible.”

He sat back. The image showed Kuze’s handwriting on the open sheet: short times, initials, a vertical line that might have been a divider. The right edge of the paper was cut off where the photograph ended. It could not establish what the next page contained, or whether the page had been turned before the camera arrived.

Sato joined them by phone. He said the officer had taken three photographs, but the other two showed the bag label and the notebook cover. None recorded the whole spread. The protocol was to photograph the visible page, not rearrange the object to make a better image.

“Was the notebook moved before the photographs?” Himeno asked.

“I don’t know,” Sato said. “I wasn’t at the scene when it was sealed.”

“Who was?”

“I’ll get the name from the property log.”

Himeno wrote that down. She did not ask him to guess from the image. The line noticed in the later inspection remained unexplained. The photograph neither confirmed nor disproved that it had been present when the notebook was found.

Wataru tapped the printed time on the evidence sheet. “Four minutes.”

“The hall clock and the camera clock differed by four minutes,” Himeno said. “That’s what the calibration slip says.”

“And the line asks who changed it.”

“Yes.”

“Could be about the camera.”

“It could.”

“Could be about the hall clock?”

“It could.”

“Could it be unrelated?”

She looked at him. “That’s also possible.”

The review agreement with Ms. Kagawa allowed her to inspect the committee’s records, but not to take them. Himeno sent the solicitor a brief note asking whether she had made a record of the new annotation and whether the original notebook had been closed between inspections. Kagawa replied that her report would include what she had personally observed. She could not certify what had happened before she arrived.

The answer was careful and unsatisfying. It was still an answer.

At noon, Kagami called from outside the school grounds. He asked whether the line was visible in the first photograph. Himeno told him it was not, but that the page itself was largely obscured.

“Then don’t call it new,” he said. “Call it newly noticed.”

“That’s what I wrote.”

“Good.”

He was not allowed inside the records room. The board’s notice listed the committee archive and the east office as restricted areas. The library and courtyard were not named, but the headmistress had asked him not to contact students on school business.

“I can still give a statement to the police?” he asked.

“Yes,” Sato said through the phone. “That restriction is from the board, not us.”

Kagami asked if he could receive a copy of the notebook inspection log.

“Not through the school,” Himeno said. “You’d need to request it from the police.”

“Understood.”

He did not ask her to send a photograph around the restriction. Before ending the call, he asked whether Wataru had eaten. Wataru said he had, then reached for the untouched packet of crackers on the table.

After the call, Kagami sent the board a written request for the exact rooms and records covered by his restriction. He asked that the answer distinguish school property from police evidence and personal documents. The reply did not arrive that day. Without access to the east office, he could not check his own notes against the committee’s copies, and he could not verify which version of his warning had been circulated first.

Himeno asked whether he regretted giving Kuze the warning at the banquet.

“I regret that he had reason to need it,” Kagami said. “I don’t regret telling him what I knew.”

“You knew he could be placed where someone wanted.”

“I knew it was possible. I didn’t know where he would go or what he would find.”

Wataru looked up from the sugar. “If you’d told him not to go, would he have listened?”

“I don’t know.” Kagami paused. “He might have gone anyway. He might have stayed and watched me instead. I can’t use either possibility to make my choice sound harmless.”

He took the restriction notice back and folded it along the printed edge, not through the signature. The school had limited his access; it had not made him innocent of every consequence of his advice. He left the question of his responsibility open rather than asking them to absolve him.

The next morning, the technician arrived with the calibration slip and the maintenance log. He placed them side by side and said the camera had been set four minutes slow on the day of the banquet. The correction was made at 4:15 that afternoon. The line in Kuze’s notebook, however, did not carry a date or identify which clock it meant.

“Was there a later change?” Himeno asked.

“Not in my log.”

“Did you change the hall clock?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to?”

“No.”

Sato recorded the answers. The technician checked the camera’s current display, then the hall clock. The difference was now less than a minute. That did not prove the earlier clocks had stayed in sync throughout the event.

He took a breath and pointed to the form. “This is my correction. If somebody changed the system after I left, it wouldn’t be here.”

Himeno asked him to sign beneath the sentence. He did, then asked if that meant they believed him.

“It means you signed the statement,” she said. “Belief is a different entry.”

He looked at the paper, then nodded. “Fair.”

After he left, Wataru compared the maintenance log with the photograph. The log established that the camera clock had been adjusted before the banquet. It did not establish whether the annotation referred to that adjustment, nor who had written the words in the notebook.

Himeno added the calibration slip to the evidence index as a separate document. The line remained on a page whose full condition at recovery could not yet be established.

A message came from Sato in the afternoon. The officer who sealed the notebook had taken a fourth photograph on a department phone. It had not been included in the initial packet. The image was dated 10:41 p.m., nearly twenty minutes after the ambulance left.

The photograph showed the notebook closed.

On its cover, beside the bent corner, someone had written a number in black marker: 4.

Sato called again before the end of the day. The officer’s log showed that the fourth photograph had been taken after the notebook was placed in an evidence bag. It did not say whether the marker number was on the cover before the bag was sealed or added to identify the exhibit afterward. The image itself showed the number, but no hand, label, or timestamp close enough to settle the sequence.

“Was the number written on the notebook or the bag?” Himeno asked.

“The angle makes it look like the cover,” Sato said. “The plastic is reflective. I won’t certify that from this copy.”

“Who took the picture?”

“I have a name now. I’m asking the officer to confirm the procedure and send the original file.”

She wrote down both answers. Wataru read the line about the number again, then turned the paper over as if there might be another image beneath it.

“Four notebooks before it?” he asked.

“There were six in the cabinet,” Himeno said. “That doesn’t tell us what the four means.”

“Could it be a sequence?”

“It could. It could also be an exhibit number.”

The photograph narrowed the question: the mark was visible by 10:41 p.m. It did not tell them who made it or when. Himeno added the file to the index as a police-supplied image, not as proof about the notebook’s earlier condition.

# Chapter 82: The Owner’s Copy

---

The board’s letter asked for the notebooks by noon.

It arrived at the school in a gray envelope, addressed to the headmistress and copied to Himeno. The language was firmer than the earlier request: “All materials held on behalf of the committee that concern the incident are to be made available for review.” The letter named Ms. Kagawa as the reviewer, but did not say whether she had authority to remove or reproduce pages.

Himeno read the sentence twice. The secretary sat across from her with the original request, the reply and the inspection conditions in date order.

“They’ve answered the question we asked,” the secretary said.

“No. They’ve repeated the request.”

The headmistress came in and closed the door. She had spoken to the Foundation chair. The board expected cooperation, and Ms. Kagawa was still willing to work under the signed conditions.

“Then the conditions still apply,” Himeno said.

“The chair says the board owns the school’s records.”

“Does it own Kuze’s notebooks?”

“The committee paid for the cabinet.”

“That isn’t the same question.”

The headmistress did not answer. She asked Himeno to arrange a second session for the reviewer. Himeno said she would, once the officer’s full photograph set and the chain-of-custody log had been requested. She would not hand over the books without a receipt specifying what was handled and by whom.

“You’re making this difficult,” the secretary said.

“It was already difficult. The letter made it official.”

The second session took place in the library, not the committee room. The library was not listed among Kagami’s restricted spaces, and the board had asked for the notebooks, not the school’s other files. Himeno brought the six volumes in a locked case. Ms. Kagawa brought the signed inspection conditions and a fresh page log.

Before opening the case, Himeno read the serial number of the seal aloud. Kagawa read it back. The headmistress witnessed the match. Sato was not present; the police had not released the original scene photographs for general distribution.

Kagawa examined the outer covers and confirmed that the case seal was intact. She asked to see the notebook containing the clock annotation. Himeno placed it on a clean support and opened it to the relevant page.

“Have you received the fourth photograph?” Kagawa asked.

“Not yet. Sato described it.”

“What does it show?”

“The notebook closed. A number four on the cover.”

“Was the number there when you first saw it?”

“I didn’t see the notebook at the stairs.”

“Did Kuze mark his notebooks?”

“He used numbered labels inside the covers. I don’t know whether he wrote on the outside.”

Kagawa recorded the answers. She did not claim the number was significant. The photograph had not been reviewed by her, and the mark could have been an evidence number, a property-room label or something else. The person who had taken it would have to explain what the number represented.

Himeno asked whether the officer had written it.

“I haven’t seen the image,” Kagawa said. “I can’t answer that.”

The board solicitor joined by video call. She said the notebooks were to be treated as school-held materials until ownership was determined. Himeno asked whether the board claimed authorship or merely custody.

“Custody,” the solicitor said. “The school is responsible for safeguarding them.”

“Then the receipt should say that. It should not say they belong to the board.”

The solicitor paused, then agreed to revise the form. Kagawa wrote the proposed wording on the inspection log: physical custody remained with the school; the reviewer could inspect listed pages under the agreement; originals would not be removed.

The headmistress asked whether that satisfied Himeno.

“It answers what’s being transferred. Nothing is being transferred today.”

The inspection took two hours. Kagawa reviewed the page with the annotation, its facing page, the inventory and the three related time records. She asked Himeno to show where each copy had been stored. Himeno did, keeping the police image separate from the committee’s own photograph.

Kagawa compared the inventory dates with the first written request. The board had asked for “incident records” before naming any particular volume. The notebook had entered the review only after Himeno disclosed the sealed sheet and the adjoining pages. Kagawa added both steps to her log. She would not claim the board had known what was inside when it made the first request.

The solicitor asked whether the school would certify that the six notebooks had remained untouched. Himeno said she could certify that she had not removed pages and that the cabinet log recorded each inspection she knew about. She could not certify that no one else had handled them before they came into her custody.

“Then write exactly that,” the solicitor said.

Himeno did. Kagawa read the sentence back, and the secretary witnessed the correction. The board received a narrower assurance than it wanted, but one no one had to retract later.

At the end, Kagawa signed a receipt for the pages she had viewed. She did not take a copy of the notebook. Himeno signed beneath her, then locked all six volumes back into the case. The case remained in the cabinet, with its seal number written on the outside log.

The revised custody letter arrived at 4:07 p.m. It described the Foundation as custodian, not owner. It also added a new condition: the school could not publish or destroy any material while the board’s review remained open.

Himeno read the last line aloud. “That includes Kuze’s letter?”

The solicitor said the board’s scope was limited to records concerning the incident. The letter was among the materials submitted for review.

“That letter was given to me,” Himeno said. “It wasn’t submitted by the board.”

“Then list it separately as a personal document in your custody.”

The secretary added a separate line. The letter was not placed in the school cabinet. Himeno kept it in a sealed sleeve and logged the condition of the envelope, the date it had been opened and the number of copies made.

The board had gained access to the pages it asked to inspect. It had not gained the right to call every item its own.

The secretary prepared two copies of the receipt. Kagawa signed both, and Himeno compared the signatures before placing one in the cabinet log. The second went into the Foundation’s outgoing correspondence file. The inspection itself had been orderly, but the documents did not state whether the board could quote the pages in a public statement. Himeno asked the solicitor to clarify that point in writing.

“The board can describe its review,” the solicitor said. “It cannot present an unresolved annotation as an authenticated statement by Kuze.”

“Will that restriction be in the minutes?”

“I’ll ask the chair to include it.”

Himeno noted that the answer was a promise to ask, not confirmation that the minutes had changed. The secretary initialed the note as a witness to the call. Their record grew more exact while the board’s authority remained unsettled.

When Kagawa asked about historic plans, Himeno requested the reference number rather than relying on the oral description. Kagawa wrote it on a card and marked the source: city archive index, relayed by the Foundation reviewer. Himeno copied the code into the correspondence log. The paper trail had begun with an insurance query; it now had a number they could verify independently.

Before leaving, Ms. Kagawa asked whether the school had any documents about the original building that became Aster Institute.

“Why?” Himeno said.

“An insurance query came in through the Foundation’s records office. It asks for an old site plan.”

“Who asked for it?”

“The request came through the city archive. I don’t know who initiated it.”

“What site plan?”

Kagawa checked her notes. “A property number. I can send it to you when I have the reference.”

She left without the notebooks. The library table was empty again, except for the seal log and a card with the property number written on it.

Kagami returned to Aster for the badge receipt.

He had arranged to meet the secretary at the front gate, not inside the school. The board’s notice allowed him to collect personal property, but the secretary was required to bring it out. She arrived with a paper bag and a clipboard. Kagami checked the bag before signing: one notebook, two pens, a phone charger and the sugar packet he kept in his coat pocket.

“She may.”

“Then I shouldn’t give her a reason to repeat that.”

He asked the secretary to add one line to the receipt: the bag had been handed over unopened, and Kagami had confirmed its contents in her presence. She amended the form, initialed it and gave him a copy. His caution cost him a warmer explanation; the parent left with a suspicion he could not control. The written receipt protected a smaller truth.

He would have to submit any document request as a private citizen, and the board would decide whether the material could be shared. Kagami asked the secretary to confirm that the restriction did not cover his personal notes or his own communications with Kuze. She could not answer without asking the solicitor.

“You’re not angry?”

“I’m glad it says what it means.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

He put the paper in his folder. “I know.”

At the cafe across the road, Himeno had reserved a table near the window. She had asked Kagami to meet outside Aster because she did not want their conversation mistaken for an official interview. Wataru was already there, with five sugar cubes lined along the edge of his saucer.

“Those are not all going in the coffee,” Himeno said.

“They’re a control group.”

Wataru put four cubes in. “Then we’ve learned something.”

“Yes.”

“An old site plan was requested through the city archive. We don’t know by whom.”

“What is it?” Wataru asked.

“Because the school asked for the site plan. I want to know why.”

The cafe door chimed. Kagami looked down at the folder, then at Ishikawa. “The school didn’t ask you for it.”

# Chapter 84: Twelve Names

Hoshina Aya did not bring the list to the school.

She met Ishikawa in the public records room at the central library, where the archive terminals faced the staffed counter and all visitors signed in. Himeno came with him. Kagami had been invited, but the board’s solicitor advised him not to appear in a meeting concerning school records while his access restriction was under review.

He sent one line by text: Ask what she can verify, not what she suspects.

Himeno showed it to Ishikawa. “He has a habit of writing instructions that sound like criticism.”

“He has a habit of being right about what people will do with a sentence.”

“That doesn’t mean he knows what we should ask.”

“No,” Ishikawa said. “It means we should ask it.”

Hoshina arrived with a document case and a recorder. She turned the recorder on only after everyone agreed. She had chased the Lantern House story for six years, and the list she carried had twelve names. She would not put the list on the table. Before they began, she checked the recorder’s battery and said she would share the recording only with those present unless her source consented. Ishikawa asked whether the restriction applied to his notes as well. Hoshina said it applied to the identity and route by which the information reached her; the public filing on the table could be copied. She drew a line between those categories in her notebook.

“The twelve entries came from three different files. They were not a roster in the original documents. I matched them by date, not by handwriting.”

“R?”

“I’ve seen references to it.”

“No.”

“All three are possible,” Hoshina said. “The list doesn’t say.”

“Because twelve is how many entries there are,” she said. “Not because I know there were twelve children.”

“Could one person appear twice under different spellings?”

“Yes. I found one likely duplicate, but I haven’t merged it. The name might refer to two different people.”

Hoshina opened her case enough to show the tabbed source index without exposing names. Each tab carried a file date and archive reference. She had kept the possible duplicate separate because collapsing it would make a cleaner list than the records justified.

Hoshina signed as a journalist; Himeno signed only as a witness to the request.

“No.”

“I won’t.”

At the library entrance, Ishikawa checked the receipt again. The supplemental file had a separate date from the original property index. Its location was not listed. Beside the request number, the clerk had written a note in pencil: “Held under Aster Foundation deposit.”

# Chapter 85: The Survey

---

The supplemental file was not a map.

It was a survey request, a payment form and a receipt for a set of architectural drawings. The sheets had been held in a city archive envelope, folded into thirds. The oldest page was dated six years ago, two weeks after the Lantern House fire. The newest was the request placed by Aster’s Foundation the previous month.

Ishikawa read each page without touching the originals. The clerk turned them under a clear sheet and logged the time. Hoshina took notes by hand; the camera on her phone stayed in her bag.

The first request asked for “a measured plan of the north wing and lower service level.” It did not identify Lantern House by name. The property number matched the charitable trust’s closed file. The applicant was a contractor hired to assess fire damage.

The receipt showed that a copy of the plan had been delivered to the trust’s treasurer. A second line said: “duplicate to Aster Educational Foundation.” No signature appeared beside the transfer.

“Could that be a separate foundation with the same name?” Himeno asked.

“It could,” Ishikawa said. “We need the registration number.”

The archivist found the number in the ledger. It matched Aster’s present Foundation. The entry confirmed the recipient, not the reason for the transfer or who had accepted it. The receiving signature had been left blank.

Hoshina asked the clerk whether the blank was usual. The clerk said some older forms recorded dispatch but not receipt. She could not say whether the copy had physically reached Aster. The archive had no courier log for the period.

The recent request was clearer. Aster’s Foundation had asked for the same drawings by parcel number. The requester’s name was masked in the public copy, but the filing stamp showed it had come through the school’s legal office. The request listed a purpose: “site reconstruction and records review.”

“Who approved that?” Ishikawa asked.

The clerk checked the index. The original authorization was in the Foundation’s files, not the city archive. The city had only logged the request and prepared the envelope. The prepared copy had not yet been collected.

“Then we don’t know who is looking at the plan now,” Himeno said.

“We know the Foundation requested it,” Ishikawa said. “We don’t know who inside the Foundation initiated the request.”

He asked the clerk to confirm whether the current request had been submitted in person or by post. It had come through a registered courier, but the public index did not show the sender. The clerk could request the sealed intake slip; she could not release it without the archive supervisor’s approval. Ishikawa filed a second request rather than press her to disclose information she did not have authority to provide.

Hoshina turned to the six-year-old transfer form. “The copy was requested two weeks after the fire. If it reached Aster, someone there may have had a plan of the building before the public inquiry ended.”

“May have,” Ishikawa said. “The receipt is not signed.”

“What would you need to establish it?”

“The courier record, a receiving log, or a copy in Aster’s own archive.”

Himeno glanced at the envelope. “The board’s solicitor asked us whether the school had old site plans.”

“Did she say why?”

“An insurance query.”

Ishikawa wrote that in his notebook, then added “reported by Himeno” beside it. He asked her to confirm the phrase. She did. He did not record it as a direct statement from the solicitor.

The clerk finished the supervised review and placed the pages back in their envelope. Ishikawa asked for certified copies of the public forms and a note confirming that the receiving signature was blank. The archive clerk said she could certify the copy of the filed record, not the truth of the delivery.

“That’s enough for the next question,” Ishikawa said.

Outside, Hoshina asked whether he would confront the headmistress.

“I’ll ask her whether she has the plan.”

“And if she says no?”

“I’ll ask whether anyone else in the Foundation has it.”

“And if she says she doesn’t know?”

“Then we’ll know what she says.”

Hoshina shook her head. “You still think the answer is the cleanest part.”

“It’s the part we can quote.”

Himeno asked if she should tell the board about the old transfer. Ishikawa said the board already had the recent request in its own records. She should ask the Foundation to preserve the authorization and any copy of the drawing. He would make the same request through the police channel.

“Two requests,” Himeno said.

“Two separate records,” Ishikawa replied. “If one disappears, the other may remain.”

They parted at the station entrance. Hoshina had the list of twelve. Ishikawa had certified copies. Himeno had the request number, the blank delivery signature and a new question about Aster’s archive.

That evening, the Foundation replied to her preservation request. It confirmed that a set of historic site plans existed, but said the file was “not presently held at the school.” It did not identify where it was.

Himeno read the sentence to Wataru. He asked her to read it again.

She did.

“Not at the school,” he said. “That doesn’t mean they don’t have it.”

“No. It means they say the file isn’t there.”

She wrote the statement’s source and time in the notebook index. Then she set the Foundation’s reply beside the archive receipt. Both referred to the same drawings. One said a copy had been sent to Aster six years earlier. The other said the file was not presently held at the school.

Neither document said where the copy had gone.

On the reverse of the Foundation letter, in the space reserved for the recipient’s initials, someone had written a single number: 88.

No one had initialed it.

Himeno did not read the number aloud as though it answered anything. She photographed the back of the letter with her phone, then checked that the Foundation’s message and the attached document remained saved in the original email. She forwarded neither to the school list nor to the press. The archive receipt, the Foundation’s reply and the number were logged as three separate items with three separate sources.

Wataru stood beside her while she wrote. “Eighty-eight is the Lantern House stairs.”

“That’s a count in the Codex and our earlier records,” Himeno said. “This is a number written on a letter. We don’t know who put it there or what it refers to.”

“It could be a page number.”

“It could.”

“An office?”

“It could.”

“Or the stairs.”

“It could. None of those is established.”

She called the Foundation’s records office using the number printed on its public website, not the return number in the email. The clerk confirmed that the letter had been sent from their office but could not say who had written the handwritten mark. The physical original had been mailed, and the outgoing scan showed the back as blank. The clerk promised to preserve the routing envelope and check who had handled the paper before dispatch.

That was the first new fact: the number was absent from the scan and present on the mailed copy. It still did not identify the writer. Himeno added the clerk’s name, the call time and the exact limitation of the comparison to the log.

“Could someone have written it after the scan?” Wataru asked.

“Yes.”

“Could the scan have missed it?”

“The clerk says the back was blank in the outgoing image. We need to see that image before we can check.”

She put the letter in a sleeve and sealed it with a dated label. The number remained visible through the plastic, separate from the archive code and from every other reference in the file.
