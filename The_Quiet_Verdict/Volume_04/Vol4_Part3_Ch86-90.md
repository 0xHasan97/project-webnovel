## Arc 2 Recap: The Hunt

At Aster’s closing banquet, one of thirty-three places remained unassigned and one meal went unserved. Kuze was reported missing, then reported dead after the headmistress received a hospital notice. No banquet witness has established the cause or manner of death. Himeno holds Kuze’s notebooks and a sealed-page sequence; a later inspection found an annotation about a clock, but the first recovery photograph does not show the page clearly enough to date it. A Foundation request for old site plans shares a property reference with a Lantern House file, but delivery and custody remain unverified. Kagami’s exact warnings are being circulated without context, and the school has restricted his access.

The witnesses’ times remain qualified by their sources: clocks, records, and memory do not always agree. A fourth print-shop transaction establishes that a festival sheet was copied, not that it was a seating plan. The Foundation’s old envelope may have traveled to Aster, but its contents and delivery remain unproved. An archive cut through a name field establishes removal, not whose name was there. Kagami can still speak about what he personally observed, though he cannot inspect the records needed to test his account.

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event follows within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the writer’s own handwriting and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not state how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows.*

# Chapter 86: The Full Exchange

---

The complete recording was six minutes and twelve seconds long. The clip on the parent forum was eleven seconds.

Himeno watched the clip once, then stopped it before the loop began again. Kagami’s voice said, “I won’t stop you from going, but I won’t pretend the choice is safe.” The crop began on the word “stop” and ended before Kuze answered. The caption called it a warning delivered after Kagami had already sent him toward the corridor.

The full recording came from the festival committee’s fixed camera. It showed the room, the clock, and the people seated around the table. It did not show the service corridor or the banquet itself. The recording began after Kagami and Kuze had already been speaking for several minutes.

Himeno set the clip beside the transcript. “This is the part they left out.”

Kuze’s voice, on the full file, was quiet but clear: “I’m going as a witness.” Then Kagami said that he could not promise the choice was safe. Kuze answered that a witness who stayed away would only hear what others chose to tell him.

The exchange did not establish what either man knew about the banquet. It established what they said in that room.

The committee secretary checked the camera index. The file was created automatically at 3:18 p.m. and copied to the school server at 3:32. A later export had been made on a staff computer, but the log did not identify the person who selected the eleven-second segment. The original remained read-only on the server. The copy circulating online had no metadata beyond its upload time.

Himeno asked the secretary to preserve the export history before anyone made another copy. The secretary printed the system log and signed each page. It established when the full file had been saved, not who had trimmed the clip or whether the online audio came from the committee camera.

Kagami listened to the original once through the speakerphone. He recognized his own voice and the room’s clock chime, but said recognition was not authentication. He had not made the recording and could not vouch for the camera’s settings. The secretary confirmed the file was the one stored on the committee server. Ishikawa asked for a formal transfer request before he would treat it as evidence.

“Do we have permission to release it?” Wataru asked.

“The committee owns the camera,” Himeno said. “The recording also includes two students who weren’t part of the conversation.”

They had blurred the students’ faces in a review copy, but the audio still caught one of them saying a name in the background. The committee secretary said she could mute that portion. She could not promise that the editing would preserve every pause or overlap.

Kagami joined them by phone. “Don’t release a file you can’t authenticate from the original.”

“Then the cropped clip stays the only one online,” Wataru said.

“No,” Kagami replied. “The committee can publish a transcript and say the full recording is held for police review.”

Himeno looked at the paper. “The transcript won’t show the pauses.”

“Then say that too.”

It was not a satisfying substitute. It was the version the committee could stand behind without making the recording appear to prove more than it did.

Himeno called the parent representative who had posted the clip. The woman answered from her car. She had received the eleven seconds from another parent and had not checked the source. The file had been forwarded several times; the original sender was not shown on her phone.

“I thought people should hear what he said,” the woman explained. “I didn’t cut it.”

“Do you know who did?”

“No. It was already that length.”

Himeno asked her to remove the caption claiming Kagami had sent Kuze into the corridor. The woman said she would correct the post but did not want to delete it; people had already shared it.

The transcript went up that afternoon with a note that the full recording was not public and that the transcript could not reproduce the sound or timing. It included Kuze’s answer and the statement that neither man described a specific threat. The note also said the school could not identify who made the eleven-second clip.

The correction did not travel as far as the crop. By evening, the clip had been shared on three neighborhood pages. The transcript appeared in one reply beneath it. A user copied the sentence “I won’t stop you” and left off the rest.

A local reporter called Himeno and asked who had first circulated the eleven-second file. She checked the committee’s export log before answering. It recorded a full-length export on a staff computer but did not record a user name. The forum post showed only the person who uploaded it, not the sender who had supplied it. Himeno gave the reporter those limits and offered to send the log after the headmistress approved its release.

The reporter asked if Kagami had access to the camera system. He had been on the festival committee and had been present in the room, but the system log did not show him exporting the recording. Himeno would not turn absence from the log into proof he had not touched it. She said the log did not identify the operator.

By afternoon, the story’s online update said the clip’s source was unknown. Its headline still described Kagami as the last person to warn Kuze. The reporter had included the transcript link, but the preview image remained the cropped quote. The correction was present; the first impression remained stronger.

The student whose name could be heard in the background asked the committee to remove the transcript until the audio could be redacted. Her parent did not want the school to publish any version that might identify her. The secretary apologized and explained that the transcript had been checked against the recording, but not against the family’s privacy request.

Himeno took down the transcript link while the secretary prepared a version without names. The change took forty minutes. During that time, the original transcript remained downloadable from the school’s cache, and one parent had already printed it. The secretary recorded the removal and replacement times instead of claiming the first copy had been erased.

The redacted version preserved the two men’s words and marked the background speech as [inaudible]. It also stated that the full recording had not been released. The student approved the correction but did not want her name attached to the request. Himeno logged the request without identifying her in the public record.

The corrected transcript went back online. It offered more context, but it could not prove who edited the clip or what the editor intended.

Kagami read the thread at the committee table. He did not reply. He saved the posts with their dates and sent the links to the police contact, asking that they be preserved if relevant.

“You could tell them the truth,” Wataru said. “You didn’t send him.”

“I’ve said that.”

“They’re saying you did.”

“They’re repeating a line that can mean either thing without the rest.”

“Then make them read the rest.”

“I can make it available. I can’t make them read it.”

Himeno closed the laptop. “Your honesty leaves them room to lie.”

Kagami looked at the transcript. “It also leaves a record of what I actually said.”

The committee secretary printed the final post and logged the time it went live. Beneath the transcript link, a new comment appeared: Kagami’s full version was “too careful to be innocent.”

No one had signed it.

# Chapter 87: The Access Notice

---

The board’s new notice removed Kagami from the festival review group.

It did not call him a suspect or say he had mishandled evidence. It said his participation might create “a perceived conflict” because he had advised Kuze and later helped preserve the committee record. The restrictions on the archive, east office and staff files remained. A new clause required him to route questions about the banquet through the headmistress.

He read the notice at the front gate, where the secretary had brought it out with his personal mail. “Does this apply to my police statement?”

“No,” she said. “The solicitor confirmed that it applies to the school review.”

“Can I still speak to parents who ask me about the event?”

“The notice doesn’t say.”

“Then it needs to say.”

The secretary took the paper back and made a note. “You could just avoid them.”

“I could. That would still leave them guessing what I’m allowed to answer.”

He asked her to obtain a written clarification. She agreed, though she said the board might not reply quickly. Kagami signed for the notice, writing beside his signature: received; scope of clause three queried.

The secretary watched him. “They’re trying to keep you from making this worse.”

“Possibly.”

“You don’t think that’s what they’re doing?”

“I don’t know what each trustee intends. I know what the notice says.”

At the committee room, Himeno had sorted the correspondence into three folders: board access, police requests, and public statements. She had placed Kagami’s notice in the first folder. The school secretary said the headmistress wanted to see him, but the restriction required that any meeting be attended by a board representative.

Kagami declined the meeting until the representative was named and the purpose written down. That delay angered the headmistress, who sent a note saying the school needed cooperation, not procedural objections.

Himeno read it to him. “You could meet her and keep your own notes.”

“I could. The notice says questions about the banquet go through her. If she is asking me about the banquet, I want the meeting recorded.”

“She might just want to talk.”

“Then she can say that in the invitation.”

The secretary believed him. The headmistress did not. She replied that the meeting would be postponed.

Wataru came in carrying two copies of the same printed article. One used the cropped quote in its headline; the other had replaced it with a correction. Both pages carried the same publication date and the same photograph of Kagami leaving the school.

“The first copy was handed out at the station,” Wataru said. “The second was on the website by afternoon.”

Himeno checked the website’s timestamp. The correction appeared seven hours after the print edition. It acknowledged that Kuze had said he was going as a witness, but added that Kagami had not told him to stay away.

“That’s accurate,” Kagami said.

“It still sounds like you approved the decision,” Wataru replied.

“I didn’t say I approved it.”

The reporter had called Kagami before publication and asked whether he had tried to dissuade Kuze. Kagami answered that he had warned him of uncertainty, not ordered him to stay. The reporter’s notes quoted both clauses. The headline used only one.

Himeno asked whether they should publish the full call transcript. Kagami said the reporter had not agreed to release it, and the school had no copy. He would not claim what the notes contained beyond the excerpt he had seen.

That evening, the parent representative requested a private meeting with Kagami. Her son had been questioned by police, and she wanted to know whether his statement matched the time on the guest ledger. The school notice required Kagami to route the question to the headmistress. He wrote back that he could not compare a witness’s account with a police-held statement, and suggested she contact the assigned officer.

The parent replied that she had already done so. The officer would not disclose the statement to her. She asked Kagami to tell her what he remembered from the banquet.

He had not been there when the lights went out. He had heard two students arguing about whether Kuze left before or after the film began. He could not remember which student had said which time. He wrote that in his reply and added that it was not a witness statement.

The parent thanked him, then asked if he believed her son.

Kagami did not answer by email. He asked the police officer whether he could speak with the parent and student together about the narrow question of timing. The officer said he could not discuss an active witness statement with a school employee who was no longer on the review group. He would contact the family himself.

The parent never received the comparison she had asked Kagami to make. The notice had not silenced him, but it had removed the role that made his answer useful to her.

Kagami asked the headmistress for a copy of the board’s written restriction, so he could distinguish what he was permitted to say from what he merely preferred not to discuss. The secretary provided the signed notice but could not supply the board minutes approving it. Those minutes were not yet finalized. Kagami marked the copy “received” and wrote the date beside his signature; he did not mark that he agreed with its scope.

At lunch, a student approached him near the front gate and asked whether Kuze had been scared. Kagami said he could not answer for Kuze. The student asked if he had been. Kagami said he had been worried, but did not know what would happen. He neither denied the fear nor offered it as evidence of a specific threat.

The student repeated the answer to a friend as “Kagami knew something bad would happen.” The friend posted it in a class chat. By the time the secretary showed him the screenshot, the original message had been deleted. Kagami asked the student not to be disciplined for repeating a conversation adults had already made public. His restraint protected the student but left the claim unanswered in the chat.

She later sent the headmistress a copy of her son’s own account. The statement said he saw a person rise from a chair when the film began; he could not see the face from where he stood. He remembered hearing Kuze’s name afterward, but could not place who had said it. The form had one time written beside the event, with no indication whether the student meant the wall clock or the time on his phone.

Himeno did not compare the account with the guest ledger. She asked the parent to preserve the original and let the police decide whether to take it. The parent replied that she had hoped Kagami would explain whether the account helped. Himeno had no basis to do that, and Kagami had no access to the statement.

At the next staff meeting, the headmistress read the board’s restriction aloud. Kagami was permitted to speak about his own observations, not documents held by the school or police. The parent representative asked whether his memory of Kuze leaving the room counted as an observation. The headmistress said she would seek a written ruling.

The meeting ended without one. Kagami could answer questions about what he had seen, but the school would not tell him what the other witnesses had said. His honesty remained available to the public; the records needed to test it did not.

The next morning, the board sent a clarification. Kagami could speak to parents about his own observations, but could not discuss documents held by the school or police. He could attend the public memorial but not enter the records room.

The clarification did not say whether he could answer questions about a conversation recorded on the committee camera. The board solicitor told him that because the school held the file, he should not quote it until the police completed their review. Kagami asked whether he could correct a public misquotation using his own memory. The solicitor said he could describe what he remembered but should not represent that as a verified transcript.

He wrote the distinction down and kept the email with the access notice. In a statement to the parent representative, he said the posted excerpt was incomplete as he remembered the exchange, while the committee’s transcript included Kuze’s reply. He added that the full recording had not been independently authenticated or released.

The representative replied that the qualification made it sound as if Kagami was retreating from his own words. He could either repeat the sentence plainly or leave it to the school’s transcript. Kagami chose not to repeat it without its context. The statement was technically available to anyone who asked, but no longer fit into the short version circulating among parents. He folded it and slid it into his folder. Then he turned to Himeno. “I can tell them what I saw.”

“Can you tell them why you let Kuze go?”

“I didn’t let him. He chose to leave.”

“That will sound like you’re shifting blame.”

“It may.”

Himeno put the clarification in the public-statement folder. It gave Kagami a narrow right to speak and a wider reason to be misunderstood.

# Chapter 88: The Counted Copies

---

There were four copies of the seating plan, not three.

The committee inventory listed the original, a kitchen copy and a copy sent to the headmistress. The fourth appeared in a print-shop invoice filed with the festival expenses. It was charged as an “emergency replacement,” paid in cash, and collected at 5:20 p.m. on the day of the banquet.

Himeno found it while checking the expenses against the board’s request. The invoice named the shop and the student who picked up the envelope. It did not say which version had been printed or who paid the cash.

“Why didn’t we see this before?” Wataru asked.

“It wasn’t in the seating-plan folder. It was under expenses.”

“Can we ask the printer?”

“We can ask whether they still have the file.”

The shop owner remembered the job. A student brought a paper copy and asked for one clean replacement. The owner did not retain customer files; the print machine’s cache had been cleared at the end of the month. He still had the receipt, but not the page itself.

“Was the student’s name on it?” Ishikawa asked over the phone.

“The invoice says ‘M. S.’” Himeno replied. “The pickup line has initials only.”

“Could be anyone with those initials.”

“The school has two students and one staff member.”

“Don’t send me the names until you confirm the form.”

She looked at the paper. The invoice had a handwritten signature beside the initials, but the signature could not be read clearly. She emailed Ishikawa a scan of the invoice only, with the names of unrelated students covered. He replied that he would check the original after obtaining the committee’s permission.

The seating plan’s extra place had never been labeled. The invoice did not say it was a plan for the banquet. “Emergency replacement” could refer to another festival document. The shop owner remembered a folded sheet, not its contents.

Himeno set the invoice beside the guest count. Thirty-two people had signed in; thirty-three places had been set. The extra copy did not establish an extra guest, or an instruction to leave a blank card.

The headmistress asked why Himeno was sending records to the inspector without board approval. Himeno said she had sent only an invoice relevant to a police inquiry and would provide a copy of the transmission log. The headmistress asked her to stop until the Foundation reviewed the request.

“Did the inspector ask for it?”

“He asked whether another copy existed.”

“That is not the same as a formal request.”

“No. I described it as a lead and asked before sending the page.”

The headmistress wanted the email recalled. Himeno said it could not be recalled from the recipient’s system, but she could ask Ishikawa not to circulate it. She called and documented the request. Ishikawa agreed to preserve it as received and not pass it to the Foundation without a formal basis.

That afternoon, the student listed on the invoice came to the committee room. Her initials were M.S. She remembered collecting a print envelope, but not what was inside. A teacher had asked her to pick it up because the shop was on her walk home. She did not know who had paid.

“Did you open it?” Himeno asked.

“No. It was sealed.”

“Did you bring it to the banquet?”

“I gave it to the teacher in the office.”

“Which teacher?”

The student paused. “I don’t remember. I was late for the bus.”

Himeno wrote that the student remembered a sealed envelope and a handoff, but not the recipient’s identity or the contents. She did not show her the invoice signature. The student asked if she had done something wrong.

“No,” Himeno said. “We’re trying to find out what the receipt can establish.”

The student left with her own statement in her words, not a copy of the incomplete invoice. Wataru asked whether the initials meant anything to Himeno.

“Not until we compare the pickup record with her writing,” Himeno said. “And even then, it only tells us who collected the envelope.”

The shop owner agreed to meet Ishikawa and confirm the receipt. He recognized the student’s coat from the counter camera, but the recording had overwritten itself. The owner could identify the day from the cash register’s daily close report; he could not identify what she carried away. His memory of the envelope came from the fact that it had been sealed with brown tape rather than the shop’s usual white strip.

The secretary checked the committee’s supply cabinet for brown tape. Two rolls had been purchased that month. The purchase form did not say who had used them. The school had no policy requiring festival volunteers to use one color. The detail could be checked, but it did not connect the envelope to the seating plan.

Ishikawa asked the shop to keep the original carbon invoice in its own envelope and not mark on it. The owner agreed. He would provide it only against a written receipt. That left the committee with a scan and a memory, while the shop retained the paper source.

Himeno added the owner’s terms to the inventory. The envelope might have held a plan, but the evidence now pointed only to a sealed sheet collected for the festival.

“They match her name. That verifies the pickup line only if the handwriting is hers. She hasn’t confirmed the signature.”

The print shop had kept a duplicate carbon receipt in its monthly envelope. Ishikawa collected it under a written request. The student’s initials were typed, not signed. The payer field was blank. The line item said “one replacement sheet,” with no title or size.

The extra copy had become a real transaction, but not necessarily a seating plan. Himeno added it to the inventory as “unidentified festival sheet,” cross-referenced to the expense ledger. She struck out the phrase “fourth seating plan” in her draft notes and wrote “possible additional copy” above it.

The correction created a disagreement with the headmistress, who had already told the board that a fourth seating plan existed. Himeno showed her the invoice and explained that it established only a replacement sheet. The headmistress said the committee’s own recollection was that a seating chart had been reprinted after a spill. No one had recorded that recollection at the time.

“Did you see the spill?” Himeno asked.

“No. The secretary told me.”

“Did she see the reprint?”

“I don’t know.”

Himeno asked the secretary separately. She remembered a damp chart near the kitchen, but did not remember a spill or the replacement being printed. The two recollections might refer to the same event, but neither confirmed the envelope’s contents.

The headmistress agreed to amend her statement to say that she had heard a seating chart was reprinted. She disliked the correction. The board’s earlier summary now had to be amended as well, and the school would have to explain why its first account had named a plan when the invoice named only a sheet.

Wataru said they were becoming less certain with every document.

“More precise,” Himeno replied. “That isn’t always more comfortable.”

The board’s review chair asked to see the corrected inventory. Himeno sent it, along with the earlier version and the reason for the correction. The chair acknowledged receipt and did not ask her to remove the uncertainty.

That evening, the school secretary found the old print envelope in the locked supply cabinet. It had no page inside. It bore a red stamp from the print shop: “COPY 4.”

# Chapter 89: The Witness Form

---

The witness form had two boxes for time.

One asked when the witness arrived. The other asked when they saw Kuze last. Neither box specified whether the answer should come from the wall clock, the camera, or memory.

Ishikawa brought a blank copy to the library and asked Himeno to review the wording. He had not asked her to provide names or compare it with the student’s statement. The police form had been approved for use before the banquet, but the event had exposed a weakness: different witnesses had used different clocks without being asked which one they meant.

“Can you change it now?” Himeno asked.

“I can add a supplementary question. I can’t rewrite a form already submitted.”

He wrote in the margin: Identify source of time estimate, if known. He showed her the change before adding it to the master copy. A small box appeared beneath each time field: clock, record, memory, other.

Himeno checked that he had not added an option called “verified.” The time source could be documented; the time itself might still be wrong.

Wataru sat at the next table with the duplicate print-shop invoice. “This is an awful lot of care for one banquet.”

“It’s an awful lot of records for one banquet,” Ishikawa said.

“Does that mean there was a plan?”

“It means people recorded things.”

Ishikawa asked about the Foundation’s site-plan request. Himeno said the records office had not yet located the outgoing scan or the routing envelope for the letter marked 88. The city archive had certified that the old transfer form contained a blank receipt line. It had not certified delivery.

“Then we have an entry saying a copy was sent, but no proof it arrived.”

“Yes.”

“And a present-day reply saying the file isn’t at the school.”

“Yes.”

“Could it be in another Foundation office?”

“It could.”

“Could the number 88 point to where?”

“It could. It could also mean nothing about the drawings.”

Ishikawa wrote the alternatives in his notes without choosing among them. He had been asking about Lantern House since before the banquet, but he did not force the school’s records to answer a question they had not been made to answer.

At Aster, the headmistress gave Kagami permission to attend the parent meeting as a witness, not as a reviewer. The distinction appeared in the invitation. Kagami accepted and asked that the meeting have a written agenda. The secretary sent one: timeline, public statements, school access, questions from families.

The meeting began with the signed count. Thirty-two guests; thirty-three places; thirty-three meals. A parent asked whether the extra place had been for Kuze.

“The seating chart does not assign it to him,” Himeno said. “His name appears separately in the guest ledger.”

“Could it have been intended for him anyway?”

“It could have. There is no document that says so.”

Another parent asked whether Kagami knew the banquet was dangerous. He read his statement from the transcript: he had warned Kuze that going as a witness was not safe, but had not known of a specific threat or destination.

The parent representative interrupted. “You still allowed him to go.”

“I had no authority to detain him.”

“You could have told the headmistress.”

“I told her the event needed a staff roster and an access log. I did not tell her I knew Kuze would be harmed. I didn’t know that.”

The statement was exact. It did not make him sound less responsible to the people who believed he should have done more. One father asked why Kagami had not called the police before the event. Kagami said he had no specific crime to report and had not asked for police protection. He did not add that he wished he had.

The parent representative asked the school to provide a written account of who had approved the service-door key checkout. The headmistress said the maintenance log showed the key was returned at 6:48, but did not identify who had checked the door afterward. The representative asked whether the blank sign-out line meant the key had been returned by the same person who took it.

“It means the return was recorded,” Ishikawa said. “It doesn’t establish who made the entry.”

The headmistress promised to request the original log from facilities. She would not speculate about the handwriting. Kagami, whose name appeared nowhere on the key sheet, did not volunteer a theory.

After the meeting, the representative asked him whether that restraint was another way of avoiding responsibility. He said she could read his statement and decide. She replied that she wanted an answer, not a document.

“I don’t know who made the return entry,” he said. “If I guess, I’ll give you a name without evidence.”

She left the room dissatisfied. Kagami stayed to collect the chairs, though the secretary told him he was not required to help. The headmistress later removed his name from the review group roster. The change was administrative, but the empty line remained visible on the printed copy.

Afterward, the headmistress thanked the parents and asked them not to share the students’ names. She gave Kagami the attendance sheet to initial. He checked the names and returned it without reading the private notes in the margin.

The parent representative stopped him in the hall. “You keep saying you didn’t know. If you’d said you did, would that have changed anything?”

“I won’t invent knowledge to make an answer simpler.”

“It would make you look guilty.”

“Yes.”

“And you still won’t say it?”

“No.”

She walked away before he could ask whether she meant he should lie or confess to something he had not done. The secretary told him the answer had made two parents angrier. He said he understood.

That evening, the board circulated a summary of the meeting. It quoted Kagami’s sentence “I had no authority to detain him” and omitted the next sentence about what he had told the headmistress. The summary was not false. It was shorter than the statement.

Himeno sent a correction request with the complete paragraph attached. The board agreed to add a link to the transcript but did not alter the summary. Kagami did not ask them to do more.

A student found the link and printed the full transcript. She left it on the library desk, where the names of two witnesses were visible in the footer. Himeno noticed before the next class and covered the names with paper. The transcript’s accuracy had created a new risk: the school’s attempt to correct the public record could expose people who had not chosen to be public.

She asked the secretary to replace the file with a redacted version and keep the original in restricted storage. The school removed the link for twenty-three minutes while the corrected copy was prepared. During that gap, several users downloaded the first version.

Himeno added the exposure to the incident log. The record corrected Kagami’s quote and endangered two witnesses. Both facts stayed in the same entry.

The headmistress asked her to take down the corrected transcript permanently. Himeno said the school could restrict access while it reviewed the exposure, but deleting the record would erase the correction along with the names. She proposed keeping a redacted public version and preserving the original in restricted storage, with the replacement date attached.

The headmistress approved that plan, then asked Himeno to initial the change. Himeno signed as the person who prepared the redaction, not as the person who had authorized the first transcript. The public page returned with a note explaining that the earlier copy had exposed identifying details and was no longer available from the school.

Two parents complained that the school had hidden evidence. One of them had downloaded the unredacted transcript before it was removed. The secretary asked whether to contact them and request deletion. Himeno said they could ask, but could not claim the file had been recalled. The school sent a notice and recorded who received it. A correction could reduce harm; it could not make an earlier disclosure disappear.

# Chapter 90: The Initial

---

The name field on the oldest register had been cut out.

Himeno learned that from the archive technician, who had been asked to inspect a water-damaged admissions ledger. The page belonged to Aster’s earliest intake records, but the file was restricted because it contained information about minors. The technician could describe the damage; she could not show Himeno the page or identify the student whose entry had been altered.

“Is the paper missing, or was the line left blank?” Himeno asked.

“The paper is missing. There is a narrow cut where the name field should be.”

“Can you tell when it was cut?”

“Not from the scan. The original needs examination.”

The school had requested preservation of the ledger after a parent asked whether Kuze’s records had been altered. The parent had confused the admissions book with the banquet notebook, but the question prompted a check of both. The archive technician would not say whether the missing line belonged to Kuze, Ren, or another student. The request did not authorize her to identify the entry.

Himeno wrote the answer down as reported by the technician and asked for a formal condition note. The technician promised to file one with the archive supervisor. No one had yet established when the cut was made or why.

At the school, the headmistress met with Himeno and Ishikawa in the front office. She had been told that a city archive contained a transfer request for old site plans bearing Aster’s Foundation number. She said she knew of no plans in the school building, and no one had shown her a copy from Lantern House.

“Did you request the recent plan?” Ishikawa asked.

“I did not submit the request.”

“Do you know who did?”

“The Foundation’s legal office handles property records. I have not asked them for the name.”

“Will you?”

“I will ask for the authorization form.”

She said she had once seen an old envelope among the Foundation’s files, but could not remember its contents. She did not know whether it held a plan or whether it had come from the city. Ishikawa asked her not to search the school cabinet without a witness and a written inventory. She agreed.

A reporter waited at the curb after the meeting. She called out to the headmistress, asking whether Aster had concealed a floor plan of Lantern House. The headmistress stopped but did not answer the question as phrased.

“The archive records an unsigned transfer line,” she said. “It does not establish delivery. The Foundation has been asked to preserve any related file.”

The reporter asked if she had known about the fire. The headmistress said the school had known the public account of the fire; she could not say what the Foundation’s former officers knew. She entered the car without adding anything else.

The next morning, the Foundation sent a copy of its routing envelope. The scan showed a mailing label to Aster’s former administrative office and no handwritten number on the reverse. The envelope was postmarked six years earlier. It had not been signed for. The current records clerk confirmed the scan came from a box transferred from the old office, but could not say when the box had been opened.

Ishikawa asked for the box-transfer inventory and the date the scan had been made. The clerk provided an index entry and a scan timestamp from the current records system. The inventory listed “historic correspondence, mixed,” without naming the property plan. The time the box entered the new office was recorded, but not who had sealed it at the former office.

The Foundation’s secretary said the envelope had been discovered during a routine filing audit. She did not remember seeing it before. The headmistress said the same: she recalled an old envelope, but could not identify this one from its scan. Neither statement established that the sealed envelope in her cabinet was the one from the archive.

Ishikawa asked both women to avoid comparing the envelopes in private. He wanted the item logged where it had been found, then photographed in place before opening. The headmistress agreed to leave it in her office safe until he could collect it with a receipt. The Foundation secretary sent written confirmation that the old box would not be rearranged pending review.

The additional paperwork did not move the plan closer to the school. It made the route of the surviving envelope more specific and left the drawings themselves unaccounted for.

The envelope made delivery more plausible, not certain. No one could confirm what had been inside it or who had received it. The label did not show the architectural drawings themselves.

Himeno added the scan to the site-plan file and marked the number 88 as absent from the envelope image. The number on the earlier letter and the routing envelope were separate objects. It would have been easy to put them together; the records did not.

That afternoon, she and Wataru reviewed the admission ledger’s condition note. The technician had documented a narrow rectangular cut through the name column on one page. The next page contained a list of transfers, but the index did not identify which entry corresponded to the cut line.

Wataru asked if it could be Ren’s record.

“It could.”

“Could it be Kuze’s?”

“It could.”

“Could it be someone else?”

“It could.”

The technician’s report said the cut had been made with a blade, but did not date it. The edges were too clean to match the water damage. That established a deliberate removal at some point, not who had done it or what name had been there.

The headmistress returned with the old envelope she remembered. She did not open it. The outer label showed the same Foundation routing address as the archive scan, but the postmark was smudged and there was no contents list. She had found it in a cabinet in her office, behind old inspection forms.

“Was it there six years ago?” Ishikawa asked.

“I don’t know. I took over the office four years ago. The cabinet was locked when I arrived.”

“Who had the key before you?”

“The previous headmistress and the Foundation secretary. There may have been a spare.”

She set the envelope on the table. The flap was still sealed. Its weight was too slight to hold a full set of plans, but it could contain a letter or a receipt. The headmistress asked whether the police wanted it.

“I do,” Ishikawa said. “But I’ll give you a receipt and leave it sealed.”

She agreed. The receipt recorded the envelope’s condition, location, and the three people present. It did not state what the envelope contained.

On the way out, Ishikawa asked the headmistress one final question. “Did you know the name on the original admission record?”

Her fingers rested on the closed ledger. “I saw a sealed file from the Matron years ago. It included a name, but I was told not to repeat it.”

“Do you remember it?” Himeno asked.

The headmistress looked toward the corridor. “I think I remember the name—”

The office phone rang. The secretary answered and said the archive technician was on the line. The headmistress listened, then asked Ishikawa to hold the question until the technician had finished describing the cut page.

When she returned, she did not complete the sentence. She said the sealed file was not in her office and she could not certify that her memory was accurate. Ishikawa did not press her to guess.

The afternoon’s archive note arrived by email. The cut name field had been assigned an internal locator code: A-208.

Himeno called the archive technician to ask whether the code identified a page, a box or an individual entry. The technician said it was an internal locator for the damaged section, not a name or a date. The ledger’s catalog described the section as one of several pages requiring conservation review. It did not establish which student had been listed there.

The technician confirmed that the name column had been cut away from the paper. She could not determine whether the cut had been made while the book was in use, during a later records transfer, or after it entered the archive. The detached strip was not among the stored fragments. No handwriting sample remained beneath the missing field.

Wataru asked if that made the old admission record useless.

“No,” Himeno said. “It establishes a deliberate absence in one field. It doesn’t tell us whose absence it is.”

She added the locator code to the archive correspondence. It was separate from the number on the Foundation letter, and there was no evidence that the two references were related.

At the office door, the headmistress picked up the sealed file she had mentioned earlier. She had found its cardboard sleeve in a drawer but no document inside. The sleeve was labeled only “intake—restricted.” Its flap had been cut, not opened along the fold. She could not tell whether the name she remembered came from that file or another one.

“I may be remembering a conversation,” she said. “Not a page.”

Ishikawa asked her to write that distinction into her statement. She did. The empty sleeve went into a separate evidence bag, its contents listed as none found. No one called it proof that a name had been removed.

When Ishikawa closed his notebook, the headmistress looked once more at the blank space in the archive scan. She did not resume the interrupted sentence.
