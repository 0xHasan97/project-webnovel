# Chapter 91: The Envelope

---

The envelope was opened at the archive, not at Aster.

Ishikawa asked the records clerk to photograph it before anyone broke the seal. The envelope had been found in the headmistress’s office safe, according to her statement, and matched the address on a six-year-old routing scan. Its postmark was blurred. The stamp named the Aster Educational Foundation, but did not show the parcel reference or a contents list.

The clerk set it on a neutral backing board and recorded the condition of the flap. The seal had been cut along one edge, though the paper under it was still intact. The headmistress said she had not opened it. She had found the cut as it was.

“Can you tell if it was opened and resealed?” Himeno asked.

“Not from this view,” the clerk said. “The adhesive may have failed, or someone may have lifted it. We’d need an examiner to say more.”

Ishikawa signed for the envelope, then asked the clerk to open it in view of the camera. Inside lay a single cover sheet, folded once. No plan was present. The paper named a survey contractor and listed a parcel code matching the Lantern House fire file. It did not name Aster Institute or describe the work completed.

The cover sheet had a routing mark: “duplicate requested by A.E.F.” The initials resembled the Foundation’s name, but the form did not state whether the duplicate had been sent. A handwritten date appeared beside the mark. The year was legible; the day was not.

“Does this establish the plans came here?” Himeno asked.

“No,” Ishikawa said. “It establishes that a cover sheet tied to the survey file was kept in an envelope addressed to the Foundation.”

“Could someone have mailed only the cover sheet?”

“Could be.”

“Could the plan have been in it before?”

“Could be. There’s no inventory to say.”

The clerk logged the empty envelope and the sheet separately. She did not staple them together. The envelope was not proof of delivery, and the cover sheet was not proof that the drawings had accompanied it.

A records examiner arrived to look at the cut flap. She photographed the torn fibers and compared them with the fold. The flap had been opened at some point, but the adhesive residue could not distinguish a careful opening from a failed seal. There was no stamp or mark showing that the city archive had opened it. The examiner would not date the cut from a photograph.

The cover sheet carried two different inks. The printed text was a standard form; the routing note appeared to have been added by hand. The examiner could say only that the note was handwritten, not who wrote it or whether it had been made before the envelope was sealed. Ishikawa requested a comparison with the Foundation’s surviving clerical samples, but the examiner said any comparison would require originals and a qualified handwriting analysis.

Himeno asked whether the blue pencil circle on the parcel code had been there when the envelope was found. The headmistress said she had not examined it closely. The archive camera had photographed the sheet immediately after opening, and the circle was visible in the first image. That established it existed by the time of opening, not when it was written.

The clerk added the examiner’s note to the chain-of-custody record and returned the sheet to its sleeve. She created separate entries for the envelope, the cover sheet and the photographs. No one wrote “plan received” on the label.

Ishikawa asked for the original survey order. The archive had a scanned copy but not the signed contractor’s set. The scanned form listed a project number in the margin that did not appear in the public index. The clerk checked the paper ledger and found that it had been entered under the contractor’s business name, not the parcel. Ishikawa copied the number and asked for the contractor’s archived job book.

The clerk warned that the business had merged twice and that the old project files might have been transferred to a storage company. She gave him the name of the current records custodian and the form required to request a search. Ishikawa completed it at the desk rather than assuming the city could produce a file it no longer held.

Himeno watched the clerk write the request number on the receipt. The project number might lead to another copy of the plan, or only to a bill. It was a new search route, not a new fact about what had been delivered. The city index showed it had been transferred to a closed property file, which required a different approval. He completed the request and noted that the envelope had been found in Aster’s safe. He did not write that the Foundation had received the plan.

Hoshina arrived after the opening. She waited until Ishikawa finished photographing the cover sheet before asking to see the public portion of the record. The clerk allowed her to read the scan on the terminal but not to copy the restricted attachment.

“The parcel code matches the fire file?” she asked.

“Yes,” Ishikawa said.

“Does it match the Aster site?”

“Not yet.”

Hoshina marked that distinction in her notebook. She had spent six years following Lantern House records; she did not pretend to have the link before the document did.

The clerk brought the land-index book from a locked cabinet. It listed parcels, owners and transferred documents. The Lantern House parcel had been split years before the fire. One portion carried a note: “associated plan filed under institutional property.” The institution was not named on that line. A cross-reference directed readers to another volume.

Ishikawa requested the second volume. The clerk said it had been moved to an off-site archive. The request would take three working days.

“Could the note mean Aster?” Himeno asked once they were outside.

“It could mean any institution named in the missing volume,” Ishikawa said. “We don’t have that volume.”

Hoshina shut her notebook. “The envelope is a lead, not the answer.”

The headmistress signed a receipt confirming that the envelope and sheet had been taken for examination. She asked whether the police had found a floor plan inside.

“No,” Ishikawa said. “We found a cover sheet.”

“Then the plan may be somewhere else.”

“Yes.”

She asked him to tell her if he found it. He said he would record the request and respond through the Foundation’s legal office. The answer disappointed her, but she signed the receipt without changing the wording.

As they left the archive, the headmistress asked whether the Foundation could search the old correspondence room. Ishikawa said the police could not order a search of records without a legal basis, but she could preserve the boxes and arrange an inventory with a neutral witness. She asked the secretary to suspend disposal of any files connected to the property code. The secretary sent the instruction before they reached the car and received an automated acknowledgment.

Himeno asked whether the acknowledgment named which boxes were included. It did not. The secretary followed up with a list of the rooms and file series covered by the hold. The first message had protected a category; the second made the category specific enough to check.

The Foundation did not promise the plans would be found. It promised not to discard records while the review was active. The difference went into the log.

Before leaving, Himeno asked for a copy of the archive photograph. The clerk said she could provide one after the evidence review. Himeno wrote the request number in her own notebook and dated it. She left with no plan, only a cover sheet, a split-parcel note, and a second reference that no one had read.

# Chapter 92: The Boundary

---

The map office found the second volume under an older parcel number.

The city clerk placed it on a foam cradle and opened it to the index page. The entry for Lantern House pointed to a boundary adjustment filed seven years earlier. The map showed two adjoining lots, one labeled with the charitable trust’s name and one listed as municipal land. A narrow strip between them had been transferred to a private educational foundation.

The receiving entity was not written out in full. It was identified by a registration code. Ishikawa requested the corporate registry record before treating the initials as a match.

The clerk turned to the boundary sheet. A dotted line followed the edge of the trust’s property and continued beneath an unmarked slope. The sheet did not show buildings, only measurements and corner markers. One corner marker corresponded with the rear boundary of Aster Institute’s current site.

“Does that make these the same property?” Himeno asked.

“No,” Ishikawa said. “It makes one boundary point common to the two surveys.”

The clerk agreed. The map was a survey reference, not a title deed. It could show where a measured point had been located; it could not establish who owned the parcels now or what structures stood there.

Hoshina compared the old map with a public street map. The scale differed, and the old sheet lacked a north arrow. She wrote down the map’s survey bearings instead of tracing a line over the modern map.

“Could the old drawing be rotated?” Wataru asked.

“It could,” Hoshina said. “That’s why we need the bearings.”

The city surveyor brought out the field notes. They contained three measurements and the initials of the surveyor who took them. The notes had been made two months before the fire. The corner marker itself had since been replaced. The current survey used a reference nail on the retaining wall by Aster’s east access road.

“Can we inspect the nail?” Ishikawa asked.

The surveyor said it could be viewed from the public side of the road. It was not a school entrance and did not require entry to the grounds. He offered to accompany them so they would not confuse the old marker with the newer one.

At the east road, the surveyor pointed to a small brass disk set in concrete. The stamped letters were worn, but the number matched the current survey record. He showed them where the older marker had been, on a section of retaining wall now covered by ivy.

He measured from the disk to the curb using the current field notes, then compared that distance with the old survey. The measurement fell within the tolerance stated on the older sheet. He wrote down the result and the instrument used. The match placed the historical corner near the present boundary; it did not prove that every wall or fence between them had stayed in the same place.

The surveyor explained that a boundary plan could include easements and utility corridors that crossed parcels without transferring ownership of the land beneath them. A line on the map might mark a right to reach or repair a pipe. It would not necessarily mark a corridor large enough for a person to pass through. Until the deed and the service-level drawing were reviewed, he could not tell which kind of route the line represented.

Himeno looked past the wall toward the school grounds. “Does the line run underneath the building?”

“I can’t say from this point,” the surveyor said. “The old plan is not a building plan.”

The school’s present boundary fence had been moved after a road repair. The city record showed the work but not whether the change followed the original parcel line. A painted mark on the curb could be a utility reference, a contractor’s mark or an old survey point. The surveyor would not identify it without a measurement.

Ishikawa asked whether the fire-damaged parcel had been surveyed again after the fire. The city index listed a later boundary check, but the actual field sheet was missing. The archived entry gave the date and the surveyor’s name. It did not give the result.

“Could the later check have been the source of the plan request?” Himeno asked.

“It could,” the surveyor said. “A survey can be ordered for many reasons.”

They took no photographs over the wall. Ishikawa recorded the visible marker and the public road address. He did not write that the two properties were physically connected. The point established that their mapped boundaries met at one surveyed corner.

Back at the archive, the registration code arrived from the corporate registry. It belonged to the Aster Educational Foundation. The record showed the Foundation had been registered under that name before the fire. It did not establish that it owned the adjoining land at the time of the boundary survey.

Hoshina asked the clerk for the title record. It required a separate request. Ishikawa filled out the form and included the reference number from the map office. The clerk accepted it and estimated a week for the certified copy.

The Foundation’s current counsel sent a short letter that afternoon. It said Aster’s property department had no building plan for Lantern House in its present archive. It did not answer whether the Foundation had received a plan in the past or whether another office held one.

Himeno asked the facilities manager to define “present archive.” He said he had checked the school’s maintenance files and the property office’s current cabinet, not the Foundation’s off-site storage or the legal department. Counsel confirmed that the phrase referred only to those two collections. The search had not included the correspondence boxes placed under preservation that morning.

The manager returned a signed search note with the cabinets and file dates he had checked. It listed the date range and the two locations. The note gave the school a verifiable limit, not a broad claim that no plan existed anywhere under Aster’s name.

Wataru tapped the letter. “They could have used the word ‘Foundation’ if they meant it.”

“They didn’t,” Himeno said. “So we don’t.”

The next day, Hoshina brought the public copy of the property index she had obtained six years earlier. Its stamp matched the current index, but her notes showed a different parcel number. She had not recognized the connection at the time because the later renumbering cross-reference was not attached to her copy.

Ishikawa compared her copy with the certified registry correction. The codes matched after the renumbering. That gave Hoshina a new link between two documents she had held separately, but it did not add a new name to her twelve-entry list or prove who had occupied the building.

She marked her old notes with the correction date and source. “I’m not going to say I found the Aster connection six years ago.”

“You had one of the records,” Himeno said.

“I had a code I couldn’t place. That’s not the same as the connection.”

She asked Ishikawa for permission to quote the certified registry entry. He said the public portion could be quoted, but not the protected list of names. Hoshina wrote down the distinction and said she would verify the public record herself before publication.

Ishikawa did not ask her to reveal her sources. She did not ask him to endorse her theory. Each left with a document the other could check. Wataru asked whether “property department” meant the same thing as “Foundation.”

“No,” she said. “The letter is narrower. We shouldn’t widen it.”

The survey point joined a Lantern House boundary to an Aster site boundary. The corporate code matched. The title had not yet been retrieved. Ishikawa filed the map and the counsel’s letter in different folders, with a note explaining why they were not interchangeable.

# Chapter 93: The Fire Record

---

The first fire report said the north wing was empty.

The sentence appeared in a typed summary, beneath the time the first engine arrived. It was not in the dispatcher’s original log. Ishikawa requested both pages from the municipal fire archive, then laid them side by side on the reading table.

The dispatcher’s log recorded a call from a neighboring property at 2:06 a.m. It described smoke above the north roof and a second caller who reported a glow from the lower windows. The typed summary listed the first notification at 2:14 and called the lower level “unoccupied storage.” It cited no source for the occupancy description.

“Could the summary be rounding the time?” Himeno asked.

“It could,” Ishikawa said. “Or it could be recording when the crew was dispatched, not when the call came in.”

The dispatch sheet showed the engine assignment at 2:14. The two documents described different events. The summary had combined them under one heading.

Ishikawa asked the clerk to check whether the typewriter or template used for the summary had a revision history. The archive had the final page and a carbon copy, but no earlier draft. A stamp on the bottom showed that the summary was prepared three days after the fire. The stamp gave the date of typing, not the source of the occupancy phrase.

Hoshina asked if a newspaper could have supplied it. The clerk said the archive had no clipping attached. Ishikawa added that the phrase might have come from an insurance schedule, a staff recollection, or an unrecorded briefing. None could be confirmed from the file before them.

The clerk agreed to request the department’s old index cards, which had not been digitized. She could not promise the cards survived. Ishikawa logged the request and the date, then wrote “possible source for summary” beside it, not “source.”

The fire captain’s report said crews found no people inside the rooms they entered. A separate sentence stated that the building’s night register had not been recovered. The report did not say the entire property was empty, and it did not count the people who might have lived there.

Hoshina read the passage twice. “The newspaper account said no residents were present.”

“That isn’t what this says,” Ishikawa replied.

“Did the captain tell the paper that?”

“I don’t know. There’s no interview note in this file.”

The archive held a carbon copy of the report but not the handwritten field sheet. The report’s signature was clear. The appendix listing rooms searched had a blank line beside the lower service level. No reason was given for the blank.

Himeno asked whether the blank meant the crew had not entered. Ishikawa said it meant the copy did not record an answer. The field sheet might clarify it, if it survived.

The insurance schedule named a north wing, a kitchen annex and “subgrade service rooms.” It described the building as a former residential institution. A later public notice called it an office and storage facility. Neither document established what rooms were occupied on the night of the fire.

Hoshina did not photograph the insurance page. Its file number included a private claimant’s name, and she had not yet received permission to publish the document. She wrote the archive reference and asked the insurer for a public version.

The fire report included a second detail: the north access gate was locked when the first engine arrived. The fire crew had entered through a side door. The report did not name the person who unlocked it. A witness statement elsewhere in the file said a caretaker had arrived after the engine, but the caretaker’s time was written from memory.

Ishikawa placed the two entries together without choosing which time was accurate. One was a dispatch record; the other was a witness’s recollection. They were not a contradiction until they referred to the same event.

The water company’s log showed a pressure drop in the district during the fire response. The maintenance office classified it as a damaged main. A handwritten note on the copy said “interior valve?” but had no initials or date. The city could not identify who added it.

“Was the valve inside the building?” Himeno asked.

“The note asks that question,” Ishikawa said. “It doesn’t answer it.”

The water company had closed the complaint after the main was repaired. Its work order described a street excavation and a replacement joint. No inspection of the building’s internal plumbing was recorded.

Hoshina asked Ishikawa how long he had been following the Lantern House file. “Fifteen years,” he said. “The public fire report was one of the first documents I saw.”

“And you still don’t know how many people were there?”

“I know how many the documents identify in each category. I don’t know that the categories are complete.”

The answer was not a dramatic discovery. It was the reason he had kept the file open: every confident summary had outlived the record beneath it.

A retired fire clerk agreed to speak by phone. She remembered preparing copies of the report, but not the night itself. The summary template had a field for occupancy, and staff sometimes filled it from the property’s registered use. “Unoccupied storage” could have been copied from an insurance file, she said. She could not say who typed that line in this case.

Himeno asked if the retired clerk could identify the typist from the initials. The clerk said the initials belonged to the office pool, not a person. The typed summary was not a firsthand account of who was inside.

Ishikawa requested the original dispatch audio, the field sheet and the gate log. The archive clerk found no audio recording listed; the retention period had expired. The field sheet might be among uncatalogued fire papers. The gate log was held by a private caretaker service that had since dissolved.

Himeno wrote the three requests into her book, each with a different custodian. “If all of them are missing, does that mean something?”

“It means we can’t ask them,” Ishikawa said. “Absence isn’t a signature.”

He made a copy of the typed summary and wrote on the cover: secondary account; occupancy source unknown. Hoshina asked him to sign the note. He did, then gave her a public reference number for the dispatcher’s log.

The records did not prove the fire had been set. They did show that the phrase “the building was empty” had been stronger than the surviving report. Hoshina added that difference to her six-year timeline and left the cause of the fire open.

Before leaving the archive, Ishikawa requested that the file’s public index be amended to note the missing field sheet and the blank service-level entry. The clerk said the index could be annotated, but the original summary could not be rewritten. She made a cross-reference in the catalog and signed it. The change would help the next researcher find the limitation; it would not repair the missing record.

Hoshina asked whether the fire department had kept a list of people who were supposed to be in the building. The archive clerk found a mutual-aid roster for the neighboring district, but it listed responders, not residents. One name had been added in pencil after the incident, with no explanation. Ishikawa refused to treat it as an occupancy list. The roster belonged in a separate request.

He gave Hoshina the public reference number for the dispatch log and asked her to send any independent copy she found. She agreed, with the same condition she had used for the list of twelve: source and permission would be recorded before a name was published. The two investigators were following different routes toward the same fire; neither would borrow the other’s uncertainty as proof.

# Chapter 94: The Same Mark

---

The architectural plan arrived without its lower sheet.

The city archive released a certified copy of the north-wing plan and a separate index page. The index listed three sheets: ground level, upper level and service level. Only the first two had been scanned. The third was marked “withdrawn for transfer” beside the Aster Foundation registration number.

The plan’s title block gave the survey company, scale and date. It did not name Lantern House. The parcel code at the bottom matched the fire file. The Foundation’s name appeared only in the transfer column.

Ishikawa asked why the service-level drawing had been withdrawn. The archive clerk said the transfer form did not state a reason. The receiving signature was blank. She could certify the copy of the index, not what had happened to the missing sheet.

Hoshina compared the plan with the survey record from the boundary office. The north arrow and measurements matched. She could now say that the sheet belonged to the parcel surveyed before the fire. She could not say where the service-level drawing had gone.

A thin blue line marked a corridor on the ground-level plan. It ended at a wall on the east side. The legend identified it as a utility route, not an entrance. A pencil note read “verify on site,” with no date or initials.

“Could that be the service door?” Wataru asked.

“It could be a pipe chase,” Ishikawa said. “The legend calls it a utility route.”

“Could it lead below?”

“The missing sheet might show the service level. This one doesn’t.”

The drawing showed an old external stair on the north side, but the scale was too small to count its steps. A later photocopy had a faint pencil oval around the stair. The archive copy did not identify who had drawn the oval or when it had been added. The clerk marked the oval as part of the copy’s condition, not as an architectural notation.

Himeno asked for the source copy. The archive had received the drawing from a box transferred from the former municipal survey office. The box inventory named only “north parcel plans.” No intake photograph showed the pages before they were filed.

At Aster, a maintenance worker saw the public copy and recognized the shape of the east wall. He said it looked like the retaining wall by the school’s service road. He had worked there for nine years and had never entered the old property archive.

Ishikawa asked him to mark the location on a current campus plan. The worker placed a pencil dot near the road, then stopped before drawing a line. He was uncertain whether the modern road followed the old boundary. He signed the statement as an impression based on visible layout, not a survey.

Himeno asked the headmistress whether the school had changed the east wall. The headmistress said a contractor had repaired it after a storm, but she did not know whether the wall’s original foundation had been disturbed. The maintenance invoice listed concrete work and drainage, not an excavation of the ground below.

The Foundation’s facilities office sent the invoice. It covered a crack repair, a drainage channel and replacement of two fence posts. The work plan had a small site sketch. It showed a buried pipe leading toward the east wall, but no building or room beneath the slope.

The sketch and the old plan shared a similar turn in the line. The facilities manager said the new pipe route followed an existing service connection. He did not know where the connection originated. The contractor’s final inspection said water flowed normally; it did not include a ground-penetrating survey.

“Could the old line and the new pipe be the same route?” Himeno asked.

“They may be aligned,” Ishikawa said. “That doesn’t tell us what the older line served.”

Hoshina laid the copies side by side. The old plan’s blue line was marked “utility route.” The new plan’s line was marked “drain.” The bends were similar but not identical, and the scales differed. She noted the resemblance but did not trace one onto the other.

The service-level drawing remained missing. The archive had no proof it was delivered to Aster, and the Foundation had no copy in the school building. The public pages established that a transfer was requested and that a lower-level sheet once existed in the same file.

Ishikawa requested the contractor’s job ledger and the Foundation’s facilities file. The contractor had retired, but the company still held old invoices in storage. The facilities office agreed to preserve the current plans. Neither had yet produced a document connecting the line beneath the east road to Lantern House.

The plan offered a location to ask about, not an entrance to enter. The school’s counsel refused to authorize digging or internal access without a title review and an engineering assessment. Ishikawa did not ask anyone to cross the fence. He marked the public road viewpoint on his map and kept the two drawings separate.

The facilities manager arranged for a licensed engineer to review the current drainage sketch. The engineer compared it with the old plan’s scale and warned that the copies could not be overlaid accurately: one had been scanned at an unknown reduction, and the other was a hand-drawn maintenance copy. The similar bend could be caused by the shared route, by a later rerouting, or by the difference in scale.

He recommended a non-invasive survey from the public side before anyone considered access to the school grounds. The counsel approved the survey only if it did not disturb the wall or record private areas. The city surveyor agreed to return with a calibrated instrument and a second witness. No one would search for a hidden door based on a line whose meaning remained unknown.

Hoshina asked whether the engineer’s report would be public. The facilities manager said he would provide it to the Foundation first, then the Foundation would decide what could be released. Ishikawa requested a copy through the police channel. He did not assume either route would produce the same document.

The engineer added a caveat to his preliminary note: the old drawing’s “utility route” might refer to water, drainage, power, or a combined service corridor. The plan legend did not specify which. Until the missing lower-level sheet or another independent record appeared, no one could tell whether the route entered a building, followed its exterior, or ended at the parcel boundary.

The drawing gave them a testable location and a reason to seek permission. It did not give them permission to enter, and it did not establish that anyone could walk through the route.

Kagami sent Himeno a message after reading the public notice. He asked whether the east access route was included in the board’s restriction. She replied that she did not know and would not guess. He asked her to send him the public map, not the restricted plan. She provided the city’s public parcel sheet, which showed the boundary but not the blue utility line.

Kagami compared it with a sketch he had made during the festival, then called to say the old line did not match any door he remembered seeing at Aster. His memory was not a survey, and he did not claim otherwise. He asked if the police had checked the maintenance road during the banquet investigation.

Ishikawa said he had not asked Kagami to reconstruct the route because Kagami had been restricted from the relevant records. Kagami could still report what he had personally seen, but he had not been stationed at the east wall that night. His memory could not place a person or a door there.

The school’s secretary asked Kagami to stop sending questions through Himeno. The notice required questions about the case to go through the headmistress. Kagami acknowledged the reminder and sent no further requests. His interest in the lead remained, but the restriction made him a witness to his own recollection rather than a participant in testing it.

Himeno filed the public map with the board correspondence, not Kuze’s notebooks. The source and authority were different, and so were the questions each document could answer.

# Chapter 95: The Parcel

---

The title record arrived with a correction attached.

The land registry certified that the charitable trust had owned the Lantern House parcel before the fire. Six years earlier, the trust transferred one adjoining strip to the Aster Educational Foundation. The deed’s registration number matched the Foundation code in the survey file.

The first copy named the strip “Lot 14-C.” The current map office used a different number. A registry clerk had attached a correction explaining that the parcel had been renumbered during a city-wide survey. The correction linked the old and new lot numbers, but did not say the entire Lantern House property had become Aster’s. The deed’s registration date was seven years before the present inquiry, not six; the archive envelope’s postmark came later and did not change the date of the land transfer.

Ishikawa read the deed aloud. The transferred strip was described as a service easement and access right. The trust retained the main parcel. Aster’s Foundation received the right to maintain a boundary path and shared utility connections.

Himeno looked at the old plan’s blue line. “That makes the route more than a resemblance.”

“It makes the easement and utility connection part of the deed,” Ishikawa said. “It doesn’t tell us where the pipe goes now or what rooms were connected to it.”

Hoshina asked whether the deed had been public all along. The registry clerk said it had been filed under the old lot number, and the cross-reference was added during renumbering. It could be found by searching either number, but the electronic index had not displayed both in the same view.

The Foundation’s counsel sent a statement confirming that the current entity was the registered recipient of the service easement. It said the easement had been maintained for access and utilities and had no known connection to the interior of Lantern House. The statement did not identify who had requested the original plan or where the missing service-level sheet was now.

Ishikawa asked for the deed’s referenced maintenance schedule. It listed a route along the east boundary, a drainage line and an access gate. The gate was to remain locked except for inspection or repairs. The schedule did not state that the school had built or used any rooms beyond its boundary.

Hoshina asked the registry clerk whether the service strip had a separate street entrance. The clerk said the deed gave access from the Aster side, but the public map did not indicate whether a gate had been installed. The original field survey was missing. The access right existed on paper; present use was not recorded there.

The headmistress agreed to meet Ishikawa and Himeno in the Foundation’s conference room. She brought the old office key register and the facilities inventory. The key register listed a “west boundary gate” key held by the former secretary. The entry ended four years ago, when the office changed hands. No return date was written.

“Does the current office have a key?” Ishikawa asked.

The headmistress said the facilities manager kept several unmarked keys in a maintenance cabinet. She had asked him not to test any of them until police could inventory the set. The facilities manager arrived with a sealed bag. One key had a small paper tag reading “east access,” but the tag’s handwriting had not been compared with the old register.

The headmistress did not recognize the handwriting. The facilities manager said the key had been in the cabinet when he took the job. He had never used it. Ishikawa photographed the bag and requested a locksmith to identify the lock it fit. No one tried it at the gate.

The school’s current campus plan showed a fence and retaining wall along the same side. It did not show an interior entrance. The old deed described a gate, and the key tag named an access point. The present location of the lock had not been established.

Himeno asked the headmistress why the old site plan had been requested now. She said she did not know who made the request. The legal office had been asked to preserve the authorization; it had not yet produced it. She had learned of the survey file only after the archive inquiry.

“Did anyone at Aster know the Foundation had an easement?” Ishikawa asked.

“The facilities office may have known. The deed was not in my office file.”

“Will you ask them?”

“I have. The manager says he was told there was a boundary access, but not what it connected to.”

Hoshina asked whether the old gate could have been used during the fire. Ishikawa said the deed and the key register did not establish that. The fire report described the north gate and a side door, neither identified as the east boundary gate. The locations did not yet match.

The legal connection was now established: the same Aster Foundation had held an access and utility easement beside the Lantern House property. The plan transfer remained uncertain, and no one had shown that the present school buildings sat over the old service level. The fire report still did not say the building had been empty or that the fire had been staged.

That evening, the school board asked Ishikawa to clarify whether the easement placed Aster inside the Lantern House investigation. He replied that the deed made the Foundation a party to a property arrangement; it did not establish criminal involvement or knowledge of the fire. The chair asked whether he would remove the Foundation from the inquiry if no plan was found.

“I can’t promise that before I know what other records exist,” he said. “I also can’t treat the deed as proof of wrongdoing.”

The board’s clerk recorded both sentences in the minutes. The Foundation’s counsel asked that the second be published with the first. Ishikawa agreed to provide the exact quote but would not approve a summary written by the board.

Himeno read his reply and said the wording gave both sides something they could use. Ishikawa said the wording belonged to the record, not either side.

The board’s public notice called the easement “a routine access arrangement.” Ishikawa did not dispute that such arrangements were common. He asked the board to add that the deed’s route and present use were still under review. The correction was added the following morning, below the original sentence rather than in its headline.

The registry clerk walked them through the deed’s attachments. One schedule described annual inspection of the boundary path. Another assigned responsibility for repairs to the trust, except for the gate and utility fittings, which belonged to the Foundation. The wording created shared obligations but did not say who had exercised them. No inspection reports were filed with the deed.

The deed also reserved the trust’s right to enter the strip for maintenance, with notice to the Foundation. The clause did not name particular employees or give a key holder. Ishikawa requested any notices sent under that clause. The registry had none; those would have been private correspondence, if they existed.

Himeno asked the headmistress whether the school had ever received a maintenance notice from the trust. The headmistress checked the office log and found no such entry for her four years in the role. She could not answer for the period before she arrived. The Foundation secretary promised to search the old correspondence boxes, but the search would take time.

The deed gave a reason for both organizations to maintain a boundary route. It did not say whether the route had been used during the fire, whether the gate still stood, or whether the easement touched an interior space. Those questions remained outside the title record.

Ishikawa added the deed to his Lantern House file, along with the correction linking the parcel numbers. He wrote the source and date on the folder tab. The evidence did not close the case. It changed the question from whether Aster appeared in the records to what its registered access had been used for.

The locksmith called before they left. The tagged key fit a gate lock kept in the facilities cabinet. The lock had been removed from the east retaining wall during a repair and stored, not discarded. The work invoice said it had been taken down, but did not say whether the gate itself had been replaced or sealed.

The headmistress asked whether the gate was still there.

The facilities manager checked his inventory. The gate had not been listed. He asked the contractor for the repair photographs and work order before anyone returned to the wall. The contractor agreed to search its archive; the job had been completed four years earlier, and the crew had since changed.

Ishikawa asked the locksmith whether the key could have been copied. The locksmith said the cut was common and could not identify a particular lock from its shape alone. It fit the stored lock because they had tested it in the shop, but the key tag’s handwriting had not been authenticated. The test established compatibility between two objects, not who had labeled them or when.

Himeno asked the headmistress to keep the key and lock in separate sealed bags until the contractor’s records arrived. The facilities manager signed a receipt for both. He wrote that the lock had been stored in the cabinet and the key had been found in an unmarked set; he did not claim they had been used together before the test.

The Foundation’s counsel received the report and asked that no one approach the east boundary until the ownership and safety review was complete. Ishikawa acknowledged the instruction. The deed gave the Foundation a right of access, but he would not turn that right into an invitation to enter an uncertain site.

The gate itself remained absent from the facilities inventory. The lock and key were present, matched by a shop test, and still lacked a documented chain connecting them to the old property.
