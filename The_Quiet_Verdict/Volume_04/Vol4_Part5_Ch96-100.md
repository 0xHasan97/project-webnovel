# Chapter 96: The East Gate

---

The gate was not on the current campus plan.

The repair contractor sent three photographs from a storage drive. The images were dated four years earlier and showed a section of east retaining wall, a steel gate and a square lock plate. The first photograph was taken before the repair; the second showed the lock removed; the third showed the wall after the concrete work. The gate itself was outside the frame in the final image.

The contractor could not confirm whether it had been reinstalled. The work order said “remove defective lock; return hardware to facilities.” It did not say “remove gate.” A handwritten line beneath the description read “opening closed,” but there was no signature beside it.

The facilities manager checked the work-order system for earlier drafts. The electronic record had been scanned after the job closed; no revision history remained. A paper copy in the contractor’s archive showed the same phrase, but the lower edge was torn where a crew number might have been written. The contractor would not guess whether the tear happened before or after filing.

The invoice itemized concrete repair and drainage work. It charged for a replacement lock, but no lock appeared on the receipt for returned hardware. The facilities manager said the company often billed a standard lock allowance even when a part was repaired. The line could be checked against the company’s rate sheet, but it did not prove the lock had been replaced.

Ishikawa asked the contractor to preserve the invoice, torn copy and rate sheet as separate records. He did not call the difference an irregularity until he could compare them. The contractor sent the rate sheet that afternoon. It confirmed the allowance was standard, leaving the missing receipt unexplained but not necessarily suspicious.

The facilities manager brought the photographs to the school office and placed them beside the locksmith’s receipt. Ishikawa asked him not to match the stored lock to the gate by appearance alone. The key fit the lock, and the lock had been photographed on the wall before the repair. That supported a likely connection. It did not establish the key had been used after the photographs were taken.

“Who wrote ‘opening closed’?” Himeno asked.

“The crew lead usually completed the bottom line,” the contractor said. “He retired last year. I can ask him if he remembers.”

“Does the phrase mean the gate was sealed?”

“It may mean the repair was finished. Our work orders use the same phrase for a patched opening.”

The contractor looked again at the photograph. It showed a narrow gate set into the wall, with weeds along the lower edge. It did not show what lay on the other side. The camera’s date stamp was part of the saved image, but the contractor could not say whether the camera clock had been checked that week.

Ishikawa requested the original image files and the crew lead’s statement. The contractor agreed to preserve them. He did not agree to release the employee’s personal address; the city could contact him through the company.

The city surveyor came out with a measuring instrument and a second technician. They stood on the public road and recorded the brass boundary disk, the retaining wall and the location shown in the repair photograph. The current wall had been rebuilt in sections. One joint fell near the position of the old gate, but the surveyor could not confirm the opening’s exact width from the photograph’s perspective.

He measured the public boundary and compared it with the deed. The easement crossed the strip from the Aster side to the trust parcel. The gate’s location appeared consistent with that route. The surveyor wrote “probable alignment” in his field note, then explained that the deed allowed maintenance access; it did not establish an underground passage.

The second technician took photographs from the road. Neither entered school property or leaned over the wall. They placed a scale marker beside the visible joint and recorded the distance from the brass disk. The resulting measurements could be compared with a later survey if one was found.

Hoshina asked the surveyor whether the boundary had moved when the wall was repaired.

“The wall may have moved. The legal line did not,” he replied. “I can locate the line. I can’t tell you what the wall concealed.”

The Foundation’s counsel sent permission for a non-invasive scan of the public-facing section only. The engineer arrived the next afternoon and used a hand-held instrument to check for changes in material density. He found two areas that differed from the surrounding concrete. One was a patch consistent with the repair photograph. The other lay below the visible joint.

“Could that second area be a doorway?” Wataru asked.

“It could be a former drain, a void, a different aggregate, or a repair,” the engineer said. “This equipment cannot identify the shape or purpose.”

He did not give them a dramatic reading. He marked the location, took a second measurement from a different angle, and recorded that the result was inconclusive. Any further examination would require the owner’s permission and a structural review.

The counsel refused destructive testing until the Foundation and the trust agreed on responsibility for the wall. The trust’s former office had been dissolved; its successor was still being identified. Ishikawa asked the city to preserve the survey readings and requested the current title holder’s contact through the registry.

Himeno watched him file the request. “If the wall is only a wall, the scan tells us nothing.”

“It tells us what we checked and what the instrument could not tell us.”

“That isn’t much.”

“It is enough to keep someone from calling a gray patch a door.”

The Foundation sent an updated property statement that evening. It confirmed the easement and the former gate but said the school had no current record of an entrance through the east wall. It did not say whether the gate had been sealed or what lay beyond it.

The contractor’s retired crew lead called the next morning. He remembered a gate but not the phrase “opening closed.” He said it could refer to repairing the lock plate, patching the concrete around it, or closing the work order. He had not signed the page and could not identify who wrote the note. His memory supported the gate’s existence in the photograph, not the condition of the opening afterward.

Ishikawa asked the contractor to preserve the call record and provide the crew lead’s statement in writing. The man agreed, but wanted the question put to him by the company rather than the police. The contractor sent a form with the exact wording. He answered that he recalled the gate being present before the work and did not recall seeing it afterward.

That left the most important part of the repair undocumented. The photographs showed the lock removed and the wall repaired; the witness could not say whether the gate had been replaced, sealed, or left out of frame. The work order closed the job, not the question.

Himeno added the statement to the file. She kept the repair photographs, survey note and scan report separate. The legal access was established; the physical route remained incomplete.

On the last photograph, the gate’s outside face carried a small painted numeral. It looked like a 4, though the lower stroke was blurred by rust. The contractor said the crew had not painted it. The surveyor said it was not a boundary marker. No record identified it.

Wataru asked whether it could be connected to the black number on the notebook cover.

“It could,” Himeno said. “It could also be a maintenance mark.”

Ishikawa asked the contractor to preserve the original photograph before sending a copy to the police. The contractor did. The mark became another recorded detail without becoming an explanation.

# Chapter 97: Her Hand

---

Mio returned to Aster with Dr. Kreutz.

They arrived through the front entrance at 10:06 on a Monday morning. The headmistress had received a request for a supervised visit the previous Friday. The request said the doctor wished to consult the school about a former student and a set of archived correspondence. It did not name Mio as a visitor.

The receptionist recognized her first. Mio wore a dark coat and carried no bag. Her hair was shorter than it had been when she left. She stood beside Kreutz with her hands folded at her waist, her posture straight enough to look practiced. The doctor rested one hand on the curved handle of his cane and greeted the receptionist by name.

“Mio-san,” the headmistress said when she reached the lobby.

“Good morning, Headmistress.” Mio bowed. Her voice was calm and low. “Thank you for allowing me to visit.”

The headmistress looked at Kreutz. “You said you wanted to discuss records.”

“I do,” he said. “And Mio’s return. She has completed her work at Lantern House and asked to speak with the school.”

“I did not ask to return to class,” Mio said. “I asked to speak here.”

The correction was quiet, but deliberate. Kreutz turned toward her and waited. He did not contradict her.

The headmistress arranged a meeting in the small conference room. Ishikawa was already at Aster to collect the contractor’s latest statement. He remained outside until the headmistress asked whether the discussion concerned the Lantern House file. Kreutz agreed to let him observe, provided the meeting was not treated as an interrogation.

“I’m not here to interrogate anyone,” Ishikawa said. “I’m here because the Foundation’s property records and the Lantern House file overlap.”

“Overlap is not proof of wrongdoing,” Kreutz replied.

“No,” Ishikawa said. “It is a reason to ask what records exist.”

Mio sat beside the doctor. She placed both hands on the table, palms down, and did not touch the glass of water offered to her. Kreutz removed a slim folder from his coat and set it between them. A paper label on the folder read “M.H.—reports.”

Himeno entered with the secretary and took a seat near the door. Kagami was not invited. The board’s access notice still excluded him from school records meetings, and no one had asked the solicitor to make an exception.

Kreutz opened the folder. Inside were typed reports with dates beginning in May. Each contained observations about Ren: his class schedule, his conversations, his movements around the school, and brief summaries of what he had said. The pages were numbered but not signed.

“These came through the Matron,” Kreutz said. “She told me Mio was keeping a record.”

Mio looked at the pages. “I delivered them.”

“Did you write them?” Ishikawa asked.

“No.”

Kreutz turned toward her. “You told me they were your reports.”

“I told you I brought them.”

“You did not correct me when I said you had written them.”

“No.”

Himeno asked whether the reports were in Mio’s handwriting. Kreutz said they had been typed. He had assumed she dictated them to the Matron, who then prepared the clean copy. None of the envelopes carried a return address.

Kreutz chose a page from the middle of the stack. “The report dated June third says Ren told you, ‘A person who has chosen is more reliable than a person who has obeyed.’ Did you hear him say that?”

Mio’s fingers tightened against the tabletop. “Yes.”

“Did you write the sentence?”

“No.”

“Who did?”

Mio looked across the table. Ren stood by the conference-room doorway. He had arrived with the headmistress and had not spoken. His uniform was pressed; his hands rested loosely at his sides. He gave the doctor a polite greeting.

“Dr. Kreutz.”

“Ren.”

The doctor lifted the typed report. “Did you write this?”

“Yes.”

The room became still. Kreutz lowered the page. “All of them?”

“Every report you received from Mio after she told me she was reporting on me.”

Mio kept her gaze on the folder. “I carried the pages. I did not compose them.”

“You let me believe they were yours,” Kreutz said.

“I did.”

“Why?”

“I was told to deliver them. I believed it was my duty.”

“By whom?”

Mio did not answer immediately. Her posture remained straight. “The Matron told me to report. Ren gave me the pages already written. I carried them to her, and she sent them on.”

Kreutz looked at the typed stack. “You never wrote one sentence?”

“I wrote the dates on the envelopes. I carried them. I did not write the reports.”

Ishikawa asked whether Ren had access to the reports after they were delivered. Mio said she did not know. She had passed sealed envelopes to the Matron. She had not entered Kreutz’s office or seen what he did with them.

The doctor opened the last envelope in the folder. It contained a single sheet, folded in half. The typeface matched the other reports. At the bottom was the word “Noted.”

Ren looked at the page. “That is mine.”

“Can you prove it?” Ishikawa asked.

“The word appears in my incident logs.”

“Those logs are not here.”

“No.”

The answer gave Ishikawa nothing he could authenticate. It gave Kreutz a statement from the person who had written the reports. He turned to Mio. “Did you ever tell me the words were his?”

“No.”

“Did you tell me they were yours?”

“No.”

“You allowed me to believe it.”

“Yes.”

Mio did not plead. She did not apologize. Her refusal to decorate the answer seemed to leave Kreutz with fewer places to put his anger.

The headmistress asked whether the reports had been sent to the Matron or directly to the doctor. Kreutz said they had come through the Lantern House office. The envelopes were not in the folder; only their contents remained. He could not say who had sealed them or whether the pages had been replaced before delivery.

Ishikawa asked Kreutz to preserve the originals and their dates. The doctor agreed, but said they were his private correspondence. The Foundation’s counsel asked that they not be copied into the school file without consent. Ishikawa wrote down the refusal and requested formal preservation through the proper channel.

The reports had been presented as Mio’s observations. Ren said he wrote every report Kreutz received under her name after she disclosed the reporting arrangement; Mio said she carried his pages to the Matron but did not compose them. Kreutz said he believed the reports were hers. Their authorship was admitted and corroborated by Mio’s account; the source of each observation and the precise route of every page remained separate questions.

Kreutz closed the folder. “You have been reading your own words back to me for months.”

“I have,” Ren said.

“And you let me think she had written them.”

“I did not tell you she had.”

“You knew what I believed.”

“Yes.”

Kreutz’s thumb pressed once against the folder’s edge. The gesture left no mark. “Why?”

Ren’s expression did not change. “You read what you expected to receive.”

Mio’s eyes dropped to the table. For the first time since entering the room, she reached for the water. She drank once, set the glass down and returned both hands to the tabletop.

The headmistress ended the meeting before the questions could become an argument about intention. She asked Kreutz to take the folder with him and not leave copies at the school. Kreutz agreed. Ishikawa asked for a written inventory of the reports and their dates. The doctor said he would consider it.

At the door, Mio paused beside Himeno. “I know you thought I had gone home.”

“I did.”

“I did go home,” Mio said. “Then I came back.”

She left with Kreutz. No one asked whether she would return to class. The headmistress watched the front door close, then asked the secretary to record the visit without describing Mio as a student or a witness. She had been both, at different times, and the record could not collapse those roles into one.

# Chapter 98: The Interview Room

---

Ishikawa asked Ren the same question twice.

The first time, they were in the school’s front office, with the headmistress present. The second time, they were in a police interview room, with a recorder running and a legal representative on the line. The question was whether Ren had written the reports delivered to Kreutz.

“Yes,” Ren said both times.

“Did you see the face of anyone described in the reports?” Ishikawa asked.

“Most of the people I wrote about were known to me.”

“Did you invent any observation?”

“No.”

“Did you omit anything?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“Things I did not consider relevant.”

“Relevant to whom?”

“To the person reading the report.”

Ishikawa leaned back. The recording captured a pause, a page turning, and the scrape of a chair. Ren sat with both hands visible on the table. He did not fidget or reach for the water.

Before the second interview began, the legal representative confirmed that Ren understood it was being recorded and that he could ask for a break. Ren said he understood. The recorder’s clock was checked against the room clock, and the clerk noted the two times separately. The transcript would preserve the words and pauses, but it would not establish whether an answer was complete or whether the written reports supported it.

Ishikawa asked Ren to identify any pages he remembered writing. Ren said he could identify the topics, not the exact sheets, because he had not kept copies. When shown the inventory titles, he confirmed that he had written about the school schedule, the class meetings, and Kreutz’s questions. He did not confirm every line on each page.

“Is there anything in the collection you did not write?” Ishikawa asked.

“I don’t know what is in the complete collection.”

“You have seen the pages.”

“I have seen the pages Kreutz brought. I don’t know whether they are all of them.”

That answer made the difference between authorship and completeness explicit. Ren could admit what he had written without authenticating a stack he had not controlled.

Kreutz had supplied a written inventory of the reports but refused to surrender the originals. He confirmed the dates and said he had treated them as Mio’s reports. The envelopes had been discarded. He could not prove who had typed the pages or whether the contents had been altered before reaching him.

Mio had provided a statement. She said the Matron had instructed her to deliver reports and that Ren sometimes gave her sealed pages. She could not say whether every page in the folder had come from him. She had seen only the envelopes, not the typed contents. Her statement established how she remembered her role; it did not authenticate Kreutz’s collection.

The police had the typed pages photographed at the meeting with Kreutz’s consent. The photos showed dates and recurring phrases. The word “Noted” appeared on several pages. Ishikawa compared it with Ren’s incident logs, which had been obtained from the school. The forms used a different pen and a different surface; a handwriting examiner said the single word was insufficient for a definitive attribution.

The examiner also compared the typewriter impression with the school’s office printers. The reports were printed on a machine model used by both the east office and Lantern House administration. The model was common and the pages carried no printer serial number. The result narrowed nothing to a particular room or operator.

Ishikawa asked whether Ren had typed the reports himself. Ren said he had prepared the text and printed some pages at school. He could not say which printer produced every copy; he had sometimes handed the text to another person for printing. The statement supported authorship of the words, not physical handling of every sheet.

Kreutz asked whether the distinction mattered. Ishikawa said it mattered if they were trying to identify who inserted or changed a page after it was written. The doctor looked at his inventory and said he had never compared the pages line by line when they arrived. He had read them, filed them, and assumed they came from Mio.

“Then you have Ren’s admission,” Kagami said when Ishikawa gave him the summary.

“I have his statement. I have pages in Kreutz’s possession. I have Mio’s account of delivering some reports. The evidence supports a connection. It does not establish every page’s route.”

Kagami stood by the police station’s public counter, outside the interview area. He had not been invited into the room. The board’s restriction still barred him from school records, and the police had not assigned him to the inquiry.

“Can I read the transcript?”

“Not without authorization.”

“Can you tell me what he said?”

“I just did.”

Kagami looked at the summary. “And the pages? They are reports about a student, written by that student, sent through a girl, and read by the man who raised him.”

“That is a summary. Each part has a different source.”

“I know.” Kagami gave a short laugh without amusement. “I’m the person who tells people to separate them.”

Ishikawa watched him. “You still can.”

“Not here. The board removed me from the review, and you won’t show me the evidence.”

“I can’t.”

“I know.”

That was the cost. Kagami’s method depended on saying what he knew in front of the people who needed to hear it. Now the records that might strengthen or contradict his words were closed to him. He could tell the truth and still be unable to show why it mattered.

Ishikawa asked whether he had ever seen Ren write reports for Mio. Kagami said no. He had heard Mio tell Ren she was reporting on him, and he knew Ren had advised her to tell the Matron true things. He had not seen the pages until Kreutz brought the folder.

“Did you suspect the reports were written by Ren?”

“I suspected he could use the channel. I didn’t know he had.”

“Did you tell the board that?”

“No. I was not asked, and I didn’t have evidence.”

“Would you have told them if you had been asked?”

“Yes.”

The answer was recorded by the station clerk in a visitor note, not in the interview transcript. Kagami asked for a copy. The clerk said he would need to submit a public-record request. He filled it out before leaving.

The next day, the school received an anonymous envelope containing three pages that appeared to be copies of the reports. Two were already in Kreutz’s inventory. The third had no matching date. It described a conversation Ren had held in the library, but no source was named. The envelope had no postmark because it had been placed in the school’s street-facing mailbox.

The secretary called the police before opening the pages. Ishikawa photographed the envelope and its contents in place. The third page used the same typeface as the others, but the paper was newer. It carried the sentence: “The inspector will ask the wrong question first.”

Himeno read it once. “Is that one of Ren’s?”

“No one can say,” Ishikawa replied. “It is an unsigned page in an unmarked envelope.”

Ren was asked whether he had written it. He said he had not seen the page before it was found. The police could not establish when it had been printed or who had placed it in the mailbox. It might be a genuine report, a later copy, or an imitation.

The page was not evidence that Ren had predicted Ishikawa’s question. Ishikawa had not yet asked one. It did, however, change the school’s response: the headmistress ordered the mail slot sealed and all incoming documents routed through reception.

Kagami saw the notice on the front door. He asked whether the new procedure applied to his letters. The secretary said it applied to everyone. He handed her the request receipt from the police station and asked her to log it as delivered.

She did. “You’re still being careful.”

“I don’t want my request to become another unsigned page.”

He received no answer from the police that afternoon. The anonymous sheet was placed in a separate evidence sleeve, apart from Kreutz’s reports. The report collection could no longer be treated as a closed set.

# Chapter 99: The Wrong Question

---

The first question in the interview had not been the wrong one.

The anonymous page said Ishikawa would ask the wrong question first, but it did not identify what the right question was. The inspector refused to let the line dictate his order. He asked the school to produce the visitor register from the day the reports were delivered, not because the page named the register, but because the report envelopes had passed through Aster’s office.

The page itself had arrived folded into thirds, with no return address. The receptionist remembered finding it in the street-facing box at opening time, but the camera above the entrance had been offline during a wiring repair. The school had no way to establish who placed it there.

The paper came from a common stationery stock used by the school, the Foundation office, and local print shops. The ink did not produce a useful result in the initial examination. The sentence could have been typed from any computer; the page contained no printer code. Ishikawa kept it apart from the reports and did not call it a message from Ren.

The headmistress asked whether the page was a threat. Ishikawa said it was an unsigned statement that referenced his interview. The police would preserve it and investigate its source, but he would not label the writer or intent without evidence.

“Does it change the questions you ask?” the headmistress said.

“It changes what I make sure to document,” he replied. “It doesn’t choose the questions for me.”

The register was kept in a cabinet by reception. It showed visitors, deliveries and maintenance calls. The entry for one of the report dates listed “M.H. — personal” at 4:10 p.m. The receptionist who had written it remembered Mio bringing an envelope, but not whether she had left one or collected one. The phrase “personal” was the receptionist’s category, not a description of the contents.

“Did she meet Ren?” Ishikawa asked.

“I don’t know. She waited by the desk. A student came through the hall.”

“Did you see the student’s face?”

“No. I was taking a phone call.”

The time in the register came from the reception clock. The clock had been seven minutes fast during that month, according to the maintenance sheet. The school could state when the receptionist wrote the entry, but not the exact time Mio arrived.

Himeno asked the secretary to preserve the register and its maintenance sheet. They photographed the page under supervision, then returned the original to the cabinet. The visitor entry could support a delivery at the school. It did not show who received the envelope or what it contained.

Ishikawa requested the old mailroom log. The Foundation’s legal office said the log had been moved to off-site storage. The request receipt had a box number but no contents list. The storage company agreed to preserve the box while the Foundation reviewed access.

Kreutz came to the station with his inventory. He identified the handwriting on the dates as his secretary’s, not Mio’s. The report text had been typed. He had no original file. His former office had replaced its computers twice, and the old drive had been wiped under a standard retention policy.

He brought the filing index from his own cabinet. It listed the reports by month, but not by sender. The first entry was dated three days after Mio’s confession to Ren; the last was dated the week she left Aster. Between them, the number of sheets varied. The index had no receipt signatures, and Kreutz could not recall whether the reports had arrived weekly or in small groups.

Ishikawa asked why the first date began three days after the disclosure. Kreutz said he had never compared the index with Mio’s school schedule. The Matron might have held a page before sending it. The date could describe when he filed it, not when Mio carried it.

The index confirmed that Kreutz kept a report sequence during the period. It could not authenticate individual pages or establish that the sequence was complete. He allowed Ishikawa to photograph the index but kept the original. The doctor signed a receipt acknowledging the photographs and their date.

“Did you ever ask Mio to verify an observation?” Ishikawa asked.

“I asked her whether the reports were accurate.”

“What did she say?”

“She said they were delivered to me.”

“That was not the question.”

“No.” Kreutz folded his hands. “I did not understand the distinction.”

“Did you ask Ren whether he wrote them before today?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I believed they were hers.”

The answer might have been true. It did not explain why he had never checked. Ishikawa recorded it without offering a motive.

Kreutz asked to see the anonymous third page. Ishikawa said it was under examination. Kreutz asked if he could recognize the sentence as Ren’s. He could not. The phrasing sounded like something Ren might write, but the doctor said style was not authorship.

He asked whether the page could have come from one of the reports he had received. Ishikawa said it did not appear in his inventory and had a different paper stock, but that did not establish when it was made. Kreutz asked to view it so he could compare the phrasing. The request was denied while the examination remained open.

Kreutz did not argue. He asked for the number assigned to the item and wrote it on his own copy of the inventory, under “unmatched material.” Ishikawa warned him not to add the page to the report sequence. Kreutz said he would keep the categories separate.

The doctor had been wrong about who wrote the reports. He was not willing to make the opposite mistake about the unsigned page.

Mio submitted a second statement. She confirmed that she had carried sealed pages from Ren to the Matron’s office on some occasions. She could not identify every date. The Matron had sometimes placed envelopes in her bag without telling her who had written them. Mio had delivered those as well.

The report pages had one author by Ren’s admission. Mio remembered carrying his sealed pages to the Matron, who forwarded them; Kreutz’s file no longer contained the envelopes, so the exact date and handoff for each page could not be reconstructed. That gap did not change the central fact: the reports Kreutz had attributed to Mio were written by Ren. It did leave the source of each observation open to examination.

Ishikawa asked Mio why she had not corrected Kreutz. She said she had been afraid that refusal would send her back to Lantern House. The Matron had told her that the reports were part of her duty. She had not known whether Kreutz understood the arrangement as authorship or merely delivery.

“Did Ren tell you to let him believe you wrote them?” Ishikawa asked.

“He told me to write what I knew. He did not tell me what to say if Dr. Kreutz asked who wrote the pages.”

“Did he expect Kreutz to assume they were yours?”

“I think so.”

“You think?”

“Yes. I don’t know what he expected.”

The interview ended without a charge. The police had an admission, a collection of typed reports, and witness accounts with gaps between them. They did not have a complete delivery log, a source for every observation, or the original digital files.

Outside, Kagami waited by the station steps. He had come to collect the visitor-note copy he requested. The clerk had not prepared it. The request had been sent to records, where it would be processed in order.

“Can you tell me what Mio said?” he asked Ishikawa.

“I can’t share her statement.”

“Can you tell me whether she is safe?”

“She left with her guardian.”

“Is Kreutz her guardian?”

“I don’t know their legal arrangement.”

Kagami looked toward the station doors. “That answer is worse than nothing.”

“It’s what I know.”

Kagami thanked him and walked away. He did not ask Himeno to get the statement for him. The restriction had already taught him that a good question could become pressure on the person who had access.

That evening, the school issued a statement saying it had no verified copy of the missing lower-level plan and that an anonymous page had been referred to police. It did not mention Mio’s reports. The parent forum filled in the gap with guesses. One post said Kagami had been removed because he had written the reports himself.

He had not. He did not answer the post.

A second post quoted Kagami’s earlier warning and claimed that the report pages proved he had known Ren was using Mio. The pages did not mention Kagami, and no witness had said he wrote or received them. The post fused two separate facts: Kagami had suspected Ren could use the reporting channel, and Ren later admitted writing the reports. Neither fact established that Kagami knew the method in advance.

Himeno sent the headmistress a correction with the exact dates. The board declined to publish it, saying the reports were still part of a police inquiry. The secretary saved the post and the board’s refusal in the correspondence log. Kagami asked not to have a statement issued in his name without letting him read it first.

He drafted his own correction and left it unsent. It said he had suspected a channel could be used, not that he knew which reports had been written or how they reached Kreutz. He showed the page to Wataru, who asked if the distinction would matter to people already convinced.

“It matters to the record,” Kagami said.

“Will you publish it?”

“Not while I can’t show the documents that support the dates.”

He folded the statement and placed it with the receipt from his records request. He would not publish a claim he could not substantiate, even when silence let a false inference spread.

# Chapter 100: The Leaving

---

Kagami’s departure was announced as a change in assignment.

The board’s letter said his services as outside adviser were no longer required. It thanked him for his cooperation and directed him to return the visitor badge by Friday. It did not say he had been dismissed for misconduct. The newspaper headline said he had been forced out after “admitting he let Kuze enter the danger corridor.”

Kagami read the article in the cafe across from Aster. He had not admitted that. He had said he warned Kuze that the choice was unsafe and lacked authority to stop him. The article cited the school summary, which quoted one sentence and linked to the complete transcript below the fold.

Himeno placed the board’s letter beside the clipping. “You can challenge this.”

“I can ask them to correct it.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No.”

“You’re going to ask?”

“I already did.”

The board had replied that its letter described an assignment change, not a finding about the banquet. It would not ask the newspaper to correct its headline. Kagami could file his own complaint, but the board would not publish the full meeting minutes while the police inquiry remained open.

He had one last opportunity to attend a public forum at Aster. The headmistress invited him as a guest, not an adviser. The invitation said he could speak about his personal observations, but could not disclose school or police records.

At the forum, a parent asked him whether he believed Ren had written the reports. Kagami answered that Ren had admitted authorship in a recorded interview, according to Ishikawa’s summary, but Kagami had not heard the recording or reviewed the pages. The parent asked whether that meant Ren had manipulated Mio.

“I don’t know what she chose, what she was ordered to do, or what she feared,” Kagami said. “I can tell you that she carried reports. I cannot tell you what every report contained.”

The parent frowned. “You always have a qualification.”

“Yes.”

“Doesn’t that make the truth useless?”

“No. It makes it harder to use.”

The answer did not calm the room. Another parent asked whether Kagami could have stopped Kuze from going to the banquet. He said he could have told him not to go, and had not. He could not say whether Kuze would have listened. He did not claim that doing more would have saved him.

“Then you regret it?” someone called.

Kagami stood at the lectern with his hands resting on either side. “I regret that my warning became the only thing people remember. I regret that I cannot tell you what would have happened if I had said something different. I don’t regret refusing to invent an answer.”

A few people nodded. More did not. The headmistress ended the questions when a reporter began asking about the blank place and the meal count. The forum was not an investigation, and the school had not released the banquet records.

Afterward, Kagami returned his badge to the secretary. She wrote the time on the receipt and asked him to sign. He read the description: “outside adviser credential returned; access concluded.” He crossed out “concluded” and wrote “returned at board direction.”

The secretary watched him make the correction. “They’ll say you couldn’t even sign a receipt without changing it.”

“They asked me to sign what happened.”

“What happened is that your assignment ended.”

“My assignment ended. The badge was returned at their direction.”

She accepted the alteration and signed as witness. “You’re exhausting.”

“I know.”

He handed her the badge. The receipt separated the end of the assignment from the act of returning the credential. It could not restore his access or correct the headline.

Kagami packed his books from the borrowed office. He left the visitor transcript on the desk, the sugar packets in the drawer and the small cushion from the bench in a paper bag. Wataru came to collect the cushion. He asked whether Kagami planned to keep investigating.

“Not from inside Aster.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Kagami looked at the empty desk. “I don’t know.”

“Do you still believe telling the truth works?”

“I believe a lie gives someone else a handle. I thought the truth would leave them nothing to hold.”

“And now?”

“Now I know they can hold a true sentence by one edge and make it look like a different shape.”

Wataru took the cushion from the bag. “That doesn’t make it a lie.”

“No,” Kagami said. “But it can make me useful to the lie.”

He did not promise to return. He did not say he had stopped caring about the case. He took his coat and the board’s letter, leaving the office key on the desk. The headmistress watched him cross the courtyard and did not call him back.

At the station, he asked the records clerk once more for a copy of his visitor note. It was ready. The note recorded that he had asked whether Ren had written the reports and stated he had no firsthand knowledge of the answer. It did not include the words he had used with Ishikawa on the steps.

Kagami read it and asked the clerk to add the time he had made the request. The clerk said the note was an internal record and could not be amended after filing. Kagami wrote a separate statement with the date, the clerk’s name and the question he had asked. He submitted it for attachment, not as a correction to the original.

The records office accepted the statement and gave him a receipt. He left with no copy of Mio’s interview, no access to the notebook, and no official role in the investigation. He had one record of what he had said and another of what the clerk had written.

Three days later, Ishikawa visited him at the boarding house. He brought no case file. He told Kagami that the school had suspended the public release of the banquet transcript after the two witness names had been exposed. The redacted version remained available, but the original would not be released while the police inquiry continued.

Kagami asked whether the restriction had come from the police or the board.

“The board,” Ishikawa said. “The police asked them to preserve it. They chose to suspend publication.”

“Will you tell them to restore it?”

“I can recommend it. I can’t order it.”

Kagami poured tea into two cups. He placed five sugar cubes beside Wataru’s empty saucer, then counted them and put one back in the tin.

“You still count them,” Ishikawa said.

“It’s the only thing in this case that gives me the same answer twice.”

Ishikawa did not smile. “The answer is not always the same. Someone can take one.”

Kagami looked at the four cubes. “Then I should have counted before you came.”

“You did.”

“Did I?”

They sat without speaking. On the table, the four cubes remained in a neat row. Kagami had been removed from the school’s inquiry, but not from the facts he had witnessed. What had broken was not his habit of telling the truth. It was his certainty that truth, by itself, could make a person safe from being used.

At the school, Ren received the board’s notice that Kagami’s access had ended. He read it at the reception desk, returned it to the secretary and thanked her for the copy. His face did not change.

The notice said Kagami had left Aster.

It did not say the investigation had ended.
