## Arc 3 Recap: The Monster

Aster’s Foundation held an access and utility easement beside the Lantern House parcel. A historic plan index lists a service-level sheet withdrawn for transfer, but delivery to Aster has not been established. The former east gate appears in a repair photograph; its lock fits a stored key, though the chain of custody is incomplete. Fire records disagree on times and describe only the rooms crews entered. They do not establish that the building was empty or that the fire was staged. Mio returned with Dr. Kreutz and confirmed she carried reports Ren had written. Kagami’s school assignment ended, and he left without access to the evidence. Ishikawa has requested permission to inspect the easement. The number of children once associated with Lantern House remains uncertain, and no roster yet accounts for every name.

*The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event follows within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the writer’s own handwriting and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not state how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows.*

# Chapter 101: The Permit

---

The easement was not permission to dig.

Ishikawa said so before the first site meeting began. The deed gave Aster’s Foundation a right to maintain an access strip and shared utilities. It did not authorize police to open a wall, enter the adjoining parcel, or excavate beneath the school. The titleholder of the Lantern House land had not yet been identified as a current legal entity.

The meeting took place in the Foundation’s conference room. The headmistress sat at one end, the facilities manager beside her. A city surveyor and a structural engineer occupied the other side of the table. Hoshina had been invited as a journalist to observe the public portion. The Foundation’s counsel attended by video, with a second lawyer representing the trust’s successor office.

Ishikawa laid out the documents in separate folders: the deed, the boundary map, the gate repair photographs, and the fire report. He did not place the old floor plan among them. The service-level sheet had not been found.

The trust’s lawyer confirmed that the successor organization held the main parcel. It had not received a request to inspect the easement since the fire. The lawyer could authorize a visual survey from the public side, but entry through the former gate required a safety plan and a fresh title search.

“Does the trust have a key?” Ishikawa asked.

“Not one listed in the current inventory.”

“Could someone have one?”

“Of course. I can’t certify every key made before the office was dissolved.”

The lawyer did not claim that the trust had never possessed a duplicate. He offered to search the archived property register. The headmistress said Aster would do the same for its facilities records.

The Foundation’s counsel objected to the word “search.” The police had not served a warrant, and the school could not open restricted files for a general inquiry. Ishikawa agreed. He asked for a preservation hold, not an unsupervised inspection. The counsel said the hold could cover easement, repair, and plan-transfer records, but not private correspondence unrelated to the property.

The trust’s lawyer asked who would decide whether a document fell within the hold. Ishikawa said he would identify the records requested, and the owners could object to particular items. The lawyer wanted a joint list before the hold began. The Foundation counsel preferred a broader hold to avoid accidental disposal. The disagreement cost them half an hour and produced separate lists: one from the police and one from each owner.

Himeno read the lists against the archive references. The Foundation’s version omitted the old key register because it classified keys as facilities inventory rather than property records. Ishikawa asked that it be added, explaining that the deed named a gate and the register named a boundary key. The counsel agreed to preserve the entry without opening unrelated personnel files.

The trust’s lawyer added maintenance notices and any correspondence about access. Those records might identify who used the easement, but they could also include private information about former residents. The parties agreed that a neutral archivist would separate personal records before the police reviewed them.

No one left with broad access. They left with a defined list, a custodian for each category, and a deadline to confirm preservation. Ishikawa asked for written acknowledgments rather than relying on the meeting minutes.

Hoshina asked whether Lantern House’s former residents appeared in any of those records. The trust’s lawyer said the parcel files described ownership and maintenance, not residents. Any resident register would be held by the institution or its successor, if it survived.

“Then we keep those files separate,” Hoshina said. “Property records can tell us who controlled the strip. They can’t tell us who slept in the building.”

The surveyor presented a limited plan. His team would first verify the boundary marker and record the visible wall. If both owners agreed, an engineer could take non-invasive readings from the public road. Any entry or material testing would require a new written authorization.

The counsel approved the first stage. The trust’s lawyer approved it for the adjoining parcel. Neither approved opening the wall.

Ishikawa asked the facilities manager to retrieve the stored lock and key under separate receipts. The lock had been sealed after the locksmith’s test. The key had been resealed in another bag. Both seal numbers were checked aloud and written on the site form. The contractor’s repair photographs were added as copies, with the originals kept at the contractor’s office.

The survey began the next morning. The surveyor placed a tripod by the public road and took three readings from the brass marker. The engineer recorded the wall’s visible cracks, repairs, and drainage outlets. A fourth reading was rejected because a delivery van had blocked the line of sight. They waited until the van left rather than estimating through it.

A school caretaker came out to ask why the road had been closed for the measurement. The surveyor showed him the permit, which limited the work to the public verge. The caretaker asked whether the team was looking for a hidden entrance. Ishikawa said they were checking a boundary and a repair location. He did not tell the caretaker the measurement had found anything unusual because it had not.

The caretaker said he had worked at Aster for eleven years and had never used an east gate. He remembered an old iron door in the wall before the repair, but thought it was a utility cabinet. He had not opened it. When asked which year, he said “before the storm,” then corrected himself: there had been several storms. Ishikawa took his name and asked for a written statement, recording the uncertainty about the date.

The survey team did not use the caretaker’s memory to move the tripod. They completed the readings from the permit’s stated points. The caretaker returned to work, and the road closure ended on schedule.

The wall matched the boundary location within the tolerance on the deed’s survey. The old gate’s position fell near a concrete seam, but the surveyor labeled it “approximate from photograph.” The seam was not proof that a gate remained behind the repair.

The engineer used a hand-held scanner at four points. Three readings were consistent with the outer concrete. One showed a narrow difference in density, but the result changed when the instrument was turned sideways. He marked it as inconclusive and repeated the measurement with a second device. The second device showed no definite void.

Himeno watched from the road. “So there may be something behind it.”

“There may be a material change,” the engineer said. “That is all this test tells us.”

“Could it be the old gate frame?”

“It could. It could also be a patch, a drain, or a difference in the aggregate.”

Hoshina wrote the answer down without photographing the reading screen. The engineer’s report would identify the equipment, date, and conditions. It would not name a room.

At the site’s far end, the surveyor found a second marker that did not appear in the current plan. It was a short iron pin set into a square of older concrete. The pin had no number. The surveyor measured its position and compared it with the old boundary drawing. It fell just beyond the Foundation’s line, on the trust’s parcel.

“Could that be the gate corner?” Ishikawa asked.

“It could be a property marker, a construction pin, or something left from a repair,” the surveyor said. “I can’t identify it by shape.”

The trust’s lawyer agreed to have the pin examined by a land registrar. The Foundation’s counsel insisted that no one remove it. Ishikawa asked for a photograph and the measured coordinates, then stopped the inspection when the agreed work was complete.

No one crossed the wall. No one opened the concrete seam. The survey established where the easement ran and where a second marker stood. It did not establish a stair, a room, or an entrance.

On the following afternoon, the registry sent the current ownership certificate. The trust’s successor held the adjoining lot. Aster’s Foundation held the easement and a right to maintain the former gate. The document did not extend the easement beneath the school.

The surveyor called with one correction. The iron pin had appeared in a 1978 municipal works sketch, where it was labeled “lower access datum.” The sketch did not say what access it measured. Its line ended at the cliff face.

The archives officer checked the notation against the surveyor’s field book. “Lower access datum” was a measurement reference, not a room label. The line indicated an elevation and a point from which later measurements could be taken. It did not mark an entrance, stair, or tunnel.

Ishikawa asked why the sketch had been filed with the Lantern House parcel. The clerk said the works department often filed drawings by the parcel receiving the repair, even when the drawing covered adjacent land. The registry did not identify who commissioned the work. She could request the payment ledger, but it had not been included in the preservation list.

The city added the payment ledger to the hold. It showed that a municipal contractor had been paid to stabilize the cliff face, but the work description was a single line: “lower access—retaining support.” The contractor’s name had since changed. The ledger named no structure and no institution employee.

The phrase repeated the sketch’s terminology without explaining it. Ishikawa added both documents to the access file and marked the building interpretation as unconfirmed.

# Chapter 102: The Descent

---

The stair began behind the old gate.

The 1978 sketch was not enough to authorize entry. The trust’s lawyer required a structural assessment, and the Foundation required a joint inspection with both owners present. The city’s engineer prepared a safety plan for a narrow opening behind the repaired wall. They would remove only the loose facing panel, not cut into the load-bearing section. A surveyor, a structural engineer, Ishikawa, and one representative from each owner would enter. Hoshina could observe from the marked boundary. Himeno would stay outside with the log.

The meeting took place three days later. Before the panel was moved, the engineer photographed its edges and tested the concrete. The repair invoice described a drainage patch in that location, but the contractor’s photograph showed only the outside face. The wall’s inner structure remained undocumented.

The panel lifted from a recessed frame. Behind it was a short passage, barely wide enough for one person at a time. The first surface was brick; the next was poured concrete. A narrow iron door stood at the far end, its hinges rusted but intact. No one touched the handle until the engineer checked the frame.

The door opened inward a few centimeters, then stopped against a chain. The chain was attached to a bolt on the inside. The bolt was old, but no one could date it. The locksmith examined it with a light and said the chain might have been placed from either side during different periods. It did not show who last secured the door.

The owners authorized the engineer to remove the chain after photographing its attachment points. The chain broke at one corroded link. The broken pieces went into separate bags, labeled by side. No one called it a forced entry; rust could have weakened the metal long before the inspection.

The door opened onto a flight of stairs descending along the cliff. The first step was stone, the next twenty were concrete. After the bend, the stairs changed to brick. Water had left a dark line at the wall, but the floor was dry. The engineer checked each landing before allowing the group to proceed.

The surveyor counted aloud and marked each landing on his diagram. The stairs were narrow, and the handrail was fixed to one side only. There were eighty-eight steps from the threshold to the lower landing. The count matched the old Lantern House figure, but the surveyor would not conclude the staircase was the same one from a number alone.

Hoshina repeated the count into her recorder, then stated that the team had descended from the easement entrance. She did not claim the stair belonged to the old institution. A count could corroborate a location only when the path and its measurements were also matched.

At the lower landing, a second door faced the cliff. It had a modern latch and a narrow inspection window painted over from the other side. The Foundation’s counsel had authorized the group to reach the landing, not to open the door. The trust’s representative asked for the authorization to be read aloud. Ishikawa read it from the signed page.

The engineer tested the air through a small vent beside the frame. He detected no immediate hazard, but recommended a second test before entry. The vent carried a faint movement of air from beyond the door. It did not establish whether the space was occupied, ventilated by a fan, or connected to an outside shaft.

The stair’s stonework bore several narrow marks near the turns. They appeared at the same height on the wall, each cut into the mortar. The surveyor photographed them with a scale. There were no initials or dates. One mark was partly covered by a later patch.

“Could they be counts?” Himeno asked from the landing above, where she remained outside the restricted zone.

“They could be construction marks,” the surveyor said. “We need a specialist before interpreting them.”

The stair had no electrical lights. The team used lamps and checked the air at every landing. Their equipment logs listed the readings and the conditions. The lower door remained shut.

On the way back up, the engineer noticed that the concrete at the ninth step from the threshold had a different texture. He took a sample photograph but did not remove any material. The difference might indicate a repair. The repair could have been made before the fire, after it, or during the later gate work; the stair had no date stamp.

At the top, the owners signed the inspection log. They agreed that the lower door would remain secured until the air test, structural report, and revised permission were complete. The chain fragments stayed with the city engineer. The stair was accessible; the room beyond it was not.

The surveyor returned to the 1978 sketch. He compared the measured length of the stair and the lower landing’s angle with the line labeled “lower access datum.” The dimensions matched within the drawing’s stated tolerance. The sketch had been filed under the same parcel code as the Lantern House fire records.

He checked the calculation twice because the lower landing had a later concrete lip that could change the measured endpoint. He used the exposed stone edge instead and recorded both measurements. The older drawing’s scale allowed a margin of error. Both comparisons fell within it, though one was closer than the other.

The city’s payment ledger identified a retaining-support job, not a stair construction. The sketch could have been made to locate the cliff face during that repair. No surviving invoice named the person who directed the work, and no construction drawing showed the stair being built.

Hoshina asked whether the matching angle was enough to call the stair part of the old parcel. The surveyor said it tied the measured route to a point on the parcel drawing. Ownership of the land around it came from the deed; the age and purpose of the stair required other records.

That was a link stronger than the count: the measured route corresponded to a municipal drawing associated with the former Lantern House lot. It still did not prove who had built the stair or what lay behind the door.

The engineer finished the air test the next morning. The sample carried a trace of cleaning solvent and old dust. Neither result could date the room. A second sample taken later that day showed no measurable change. He reported both readings and recommended controlled entry with respiratory protection only after the owners approved the method.

The trust’s lawyer signed the revised permission. The Foundation’s counsel agreed, but limited the entry to a visual survey and photography. No one could remove objects, test walls destructively, or enter any side passage until a new review.

The lower door’s painted window remained opaque. Before the next inspection began, the locksmith photographed the latch. A new screw held the strike plate in place. Its head was brighter than the surrounding metal.

# Chapter 103: The Registry

---

The number seventy-one appeared in a transfer ledger.

It was not a list of children. The page was a summary of “resident placements” prepared by the Lantern House office after the fire. Seventy-one entries had been marked “transfer pending” or “destination to follow.” The last column was blank for all but nine. The report did not say whether the remaining entries represented seventy-one separate children, duplicate names, or administrative placeholders.

Hoshina found the ledger reference in an insurance inventory. Ishikawa requested the original from the successor trust. The trust did not possess it; the inventory said the book had been deposited with the old municipal welfare office. That office had been reorganized, and the current archive held a scanned register with the relevant pages missing.

“Can we say seventy-one children disappeared?” Wataru asked.

“No,” Hoshina said. “We can say a ledger counted seventy-one pending placement entries.”

“Could they have all been children?”

“Some could. The heading says residents. It doesn’t list ages.”

Ishikawa asked for the register’s intake card. The card stated that three volumes were transferred from the welfare office to a temporary records store. It named two volumes and left the third’s title blank. The receiving signature was a clerk’s initials. No page count was recorded.

The archives officer searched the store’s later inventory. The third volume was described only as “institutional placements, mixed.” A box number appeared beside the entry, but the corresponding box had been re-catalogued twice. The latest index pointed to a crate now held in a basement records room.

The first request for the crate was returned because the number on the old card referred to a storage shelf, not a box. The clerk corrected the request and asked the basement archivist to check the transfer manifest. That added a day to the appointment. Ishikawa did not ask the clerk to bypass the process; he asked that the crate remain sealed until the appointment.

Hoshina used the delay to check the public insurance inventory. Its seventy-one entry count came from a handwritten total in the margin, not from a printed field. The hand that wrote the total was not identified. The inventory listed different page ranges than the missing volume’s intake card, suggesting the count might have combined two registers.

She sent the comparison to Ishikawa with the sources marked. He replied that they should not describe the seventy-one as a confirmed number of residents until the register was open. Hoshina agreed and changed the wording in her draft notes from “seventy-one children” to “seventy-one placement entries, as tallied in a margin.”

The new phrasing was less dramatic and more exact. It also made the question harder: if the total came from more than one book, they needed to find out which entries had been counted twice.

The request for access required a written purpose and a privacy plan. Ishikawa filed both. Hoshina applied separately as a journalist and agreed not to copy protected names without permission. The archives officer gave them an appointment for the following week.

The lower staircase inspection was rescheduled for the same morning. The stair and the registry were physically connected only by the survey route; the papers did not prove that the ledger had ever been stored beneath Aster. The team did not combine the two searches into one claim.

At the archive, the crate contained tax files, two school registers, a maintenance ledger, and a thin volume wrapped in brown paper. The label read “placement register—unverified.” The cover had no institution name. The archivist photographed it before unwrapping it and recorded the torn paper separately.

The first pages contained names and dates. The protected section required a privacy screen and could be read only by Ishikawa under supervision. Hoshina and Himeno were allowed to inspect the index and totals, not the personal entries. The volume’s cover had been replaced; the binding was older than the wrapper.

Ishikawa compared the summary page with the inventory reference. The same seventy-one entries appeared, but the categories had been split into three columns: confirmed transfer, pending transfer, and no destination recorded. Nine were confirmed transfers. Thirty-one were marked pending. The remaining thirty-one had a short line through the destination box, with no explanation.

The summary did not say that all thirty-one had been lost. A line through a box could mean canceled, completed elsewhere, or a clerical closure. The ledger’s key explaining its marks was absent.

Himeno asked whether the nine confirmed transfers could be traced. The archivist said the names were protected, but the receiving institutions could be contacted through the official process. Ishikawa submitted a request for anonymized confirmation: number of placements acknowledged, not names.

The form asked how many of the nine were acknowledged by the receiving institutions. The archived copies returned only six receipts. Three had no corresponding acknowledgment. The transfer ledger had called all nine confirmed, but the receiving files supported six. Ishikawa wrote both counts and the sources beside them.

Hoshina asked whether the count of seventy-one had appeared in the public fire inquiry. The archive officer found a press summary that referred to “several transfers.” It did not provide a number. The number had appeared only in the insurance inventory and the placement ledger.

“Then it wasn’t hidden from the archive,” Wataru said. “It just wasn’t in the public account.”

“It wasn’t in the public summary we found,” Ishikawa said. “We can’t say what every official knew.”

The archivist closed the register while they discussed the next request. She did not let them photograph the protected pages. The record had enough to establish a total and a set of incomplete categories, not enough to name each resident or determine what happened to them.

Ishikawa asked the successor trust to preserve any transfer receipts it might have retained. The lawyer replied that the trust’s old office had been dissolved, but the archive search would include its records. The answer came with a file reference and no promise of a result.

Outside the archive, Hoshina explained that she had been collecting names from unrelated files for six years. Her list of twelve Quiet Ones was not the same as the seventy-one placement entries. The twelve were people connected to a program; the seventy-one were ledger entries under a broader institutional category. She had never claimed they were the same group.

“Could the twelve be among the seventy-one?” Himeno asked.

“They could. The records don’t let me match them yet.”

“Would you say that publicly?”

“I already have.”

She showed Himeno the line in her notebook where she had recorded the distinction. The list had begun with names from three files, not the placement ledger. Hoshina would not add an entry based on a similar date or initials alone.

That evening, the archive officer sent a scan of the volume’s outside cover. Under the replacement paper, an older title had left a pressure mark. The visible letters read “Lantern—” and then disappeared beneath the binding.

The mark did not identify the book’s full title, but it showed the register had once been associated with Lantern House by name. The source of the seventy-one entries had a location. The destinations still did not.

# Chapter 104: The Air Test

---

The lower door opened after the second reading.

The engineer repeated the air test with two calibrated instruments. One measured oxygen and common hazardous gases; the other sampled particulates. Both readings fell within the safe range for a brief visual inspection. The engineer still required masks and a tethered safety line. The owners’ authorization allowed entry for twenty minutes, with the door kept open and a second observer on the stair.

The latch had been tightened with a newer screw. The locksmith removed it under camera, placed it in a labeled dish, and opened the door without forcing the frame. The strike plate showed older scratches beneath the bright screw. The scratches could have come from repeated use or an earlier repair.

Beyond the door was a low room with a concrete floor. The walls were brick, blackened in patches but not uniformly. The ceiling was supported by old timber beams and a newer steel brace. A narrow shelf held empty jars, a rusted lantern, and a stack of paper that had fused together at one corner.

No one touched the objects. The engineer photographed the room in sequence from the doorway inward. Ishikawa read the time and photo number into the recorder. The surveyor marked each object’s position on a grid without moving it.

The room was not empty, but it was not an intact residence. There was no bed, table, or clothing. A drain ran through the floor toward the east wall. Beside it, a narrow stair continued down behind a half-height partition. The permission allowed a visual survey, not a second descent.

Hoshina stood at the door and asked if the lower stair might be the missing service route.

“It may be another stair,” the engineer said. “We have not measured where it goes.”

The black marks on the wall could be smoke deposits, old damp, or both. A conservator would need to sample them. The beam showed heat damage, but the steel brace was newer than the darkened wood. The room had seen fire or heat at some point. The pattern alone could not date it to the Lantern House fire.

Ishikawa asked whether anyone could identify the jars. The labels had faded; one retained a line of handwritten characters under the grime. The conservator photographed it through a scale and advised against wiping the glass. The mark might be a product label or a name. No one read it aloud as a conclusion.

The stack of papers was sealed in a protective case at the room’s threshold after the owners signed a separate collection authorization. The case would be transported to the archive, not opened at the site. The paper’s outside edge showed ruled columns and a date range beginning six years earlier. The writing was not yet legible.

The final photograph showed a small metal plate on the far wall, partly obscured by the shelf. It bore an embossed lantern symbol matching the stamp on the public Lantern House register. The design was common on the institution’s forms, but the plate had been fixed to the wall, not attached to a document.

Himeno asked whether that proved the room had belonged to Lantern House.

“It’s consistent with the institution’s materials,” Ishikawa said. “We need to establish when it was installed and by whom.”

The group withdrew on schedule. The engineer checked the stair, the door, and the air reading again on exit. The lower stair stayed unentered. The twenty-minute limit was logged, along with the fact that no object had been moved without authorization.

Before they left, Hoshina asked the engineer to photograph the visible portion of the lower stair with a scale. The first image was rejected because the tape measure cast a shadow across the edge. The engineer changed the light angle and took another. It showed five steps, a landing edge, and the top of a pipe fixed to the wall. It did not show where the stair ended.

The pipe was not touched. Its surface carried a white deposit near the joint, but the light and distance made it impossible to identify. The engineer noted the location for a later materials test. A second photograph recorded the partition’s height and the stair’s width. Both measurements could help plan a safe inspection without exposing anyone to the unknown lower area.

The tether remained attached to the observer at the threshold. No one crossed the painted limit on the floor. When Wataru asked if the lower door had been locked from the other side, the locksmith said the latch could not answer that question; it had been secured with one newer screw, and the mechanism could be worked from either side.

Ishikawa asked the team to state what they had not done: no descent, no sampling, no removal of the jars, and no attempt to test the pipe. The engineer read the list back into the recorder. The limits became part of the inspection, not a gap to be quietly filled later.

Back above ground, the Foundation’s counsel asked whether the room was part of Aster’s property. The surveyor said the room lay beneath the easement’s mapped boundary, but the deed did not describe subsurface ownership. The trust’s lawyer requested a title opinion before allowing further access.

Hoshina asked if the upper door had been used recently. The locksmith said the newer screw showed a recent intervention, but did not establish when, by whom, or whether the door had been opened. The rust on the latch could predate the screw by years. The repair might have been made to secure a door that had never been used, or to fix one that had.

The Foundation’s counsel suggested that the caretaker might know. Ishikawa said the caretaker had described an iron panel from memory but had never opened it. His account could help identify the gate, not the lower room. The counsel asked that the statement be kept outside the title review until the date was clarified.

Himeno requested the old maintenance log for the wall. The facilities manager found a gap where a binder had been moved during the school’s renovation. He could produce later inspection sheets, but none mentioned the gate. The missing binder might have gone to off-site storage. He promised to check the transfer list and report the result.

The conversation ended without a property conclusion. The room’s location could be surveyed; its ownership and history required separate records. Both owners agreed to preserve the documents while the lawyers prepared an access agreement.

The school’s facilities manager said the room was not shown on any current maintenance plan. He had no record of a ventilation fan or power supply below the east wall. The room’s air movement might come from a natural vent, but the inspection had not traced it.

Ishikawa filed for a second-stage inspection to measure the lower stair and sample the wall. The request included a conservator and an engineer. It did not ask for permission to enter beyond the next landing.

The evidence room received the case containing the fused papers. The archivist photographed the seal and recorded the handlers. The bundle remained unopened while the conservator assessed whether separating the sheets would destroy the writing.

That night, the archivist called Ishikawa. The top sheet’s corner had become visible through the case. It carried the heading “Residents moved before the fire.” Beneath it, in a different hand, someone had written: “Not all.”

# Chapter 105: Before the Fire

---

The heading was not enough to open the bundle.

The conservator placed the case in a humidity-controlled cabinet and examined the paper through its clear sleeve. The top page was stuck to the next at the corner. Pulling it free could erase a line or transfer ink. She recommended waiting until the paper stabilized, then separating the sheets under magnification.

Ishikawa agreed. Hoshina asked whether the archive could photograph the visible phrase at higher resolution. The conservator did so with a scale and color reference. The image showed the heading and the words “Not all,” but not the name of the writer or the rest of the line.

The phrase appeared to contradict the ledger’s count of completed transfers. It did not say what had not been moved, who wrote the note, or whether “fire” referred to the six-year-old event. The date on the paper was obscured by the fold.

The archive officer began a cross-reference search. The placement register listed seventy-one entries under a general category. The visible sheet appeared to concern residents before a fire, but the date remained unknown. The two records could be related; the bundle had not yet been opened, and the index did not cite the register’s volume number.

The school’s board asked Ishikawa for a briefing. He agreed to give a summary of the publicly verifiable documents: the easement deed, the survey, the existence of a lower room, and the ledger count. He withheld the protected names and the unverified writing on the fused sheet.

The board secretary asked for the exact wording of each item before the meeting. Ishikawa sent the deed’s access clause, the survey’s boundary note, and the archive’s aggregate categories. He did not send a photograph of the fused sheet because the image was still part of the preservation review.

One trustee forwarded a headline from a local site that called the lower room a “secret orphan cellar.” The headmistress asked the secretary to remove the phrase from the board’s draft agenda. The school could not control what the site published, but it could avoid repeating a claim unsupported by the inspection.

At the meeting, parents waited outside the conference room. The headmistress asked Ishikawa to stay for questions, but the trust’s lawyer limited discussion to property and safety. A parent asked if children had been held below the school. Ishikawa said no one had established that people had lived in the room. The parent asked if he could rule it out. He said he could not.

The answer did not calm the room. The headmistress moved the discussion to the next week, when the engineer’s report would be available. She also arranged an open forum for families rather than letting the rumor circulate through individual calls.

The headmistress asked him whether the room was Lantern House.

“The plate and the register mark are consistent with that,” Ishikawa said. “The title and date are not established yet.”

“Would you call it an annex?”

“I would call it a room beneath the easement.”

The board’s draft minutes used the phrase “Lantern House chamber discovered beneath the school.” Ishikawa asked the clerk to remove it. The room lay beneath the boundary strip, and the survey had not shown it extended under the classrooms. The clerk changed the sentence to “a lower room accessed from the recorded easement.”

The correction angered one trustee, who said the narrower wording made the discovery sound unimportant. The headmistress replied that the board had no right to make the wording more certain than the survey. She signed the corrected minutes.

The afternoon’s title review confirmed that the easement included a right of access to the boundary gate and maintenance of shared utilities. It did not include ownership of the lower room. The trust’s lawyer said the room might lie within the main parcel, beneath the easement, or across the property line. A measured subsurface survey would be required.

The trust’s lawyer proposed waiting for the subsurface survey before approving another entry. The Foundation’s counsel argued that delaying would leave the lower stair uninspected and the paper’s source uncertain. The school’s facilities manager objected to any work during class hours. Ishikawa asked each party to submit conditions in writing, rather than treating the meeting as permission.

A week later, the surveyor sent a cost estimate. The instrument needed to map the room from the surface would require access from both parcels and two days without vehicles on the service road. The trust could approve the work, but not the route across the school’s grounds. The Foundation could approve the route, but not drilling on the trust’s land.

The title issue therefore created two permissions, not one. The lawyers began drafting a joint access agreement. Until it was signed, no one could determine whether the room crossed the boundary. Ishikawa’s inspection request remained open, but the lower stair stayed beyond the permitted limit.

The city surveyor revisited the 1978 drawing. The stair’s measured angle aligned with the line marked “lower access datum.” The drawing’s title block identified the Lantern House parcel, though it did not identify the building or the rooms. This linked the stair to the former property record more strongly than the symbol alone.

The surveyor overlaid the 1978 line with the current boundary using the recorded coordinates. The lower landing fell beneath the east slope, on the trust’s side of the property boundary. The upper entrance was within the Aster easement. The path crossed the boundary once. It did not run beneath the school’s classrooms.

The conclusion frustrated Wataru, who had hoped the room would explain the school’s architecture. It also changed the inspection request: the trust, not Aster alone, would have to approve any further access below the landing.

Mio called Himeno from an unlisted number. She said she was back at Lantern House with Kreutz and the Matron. She had not been in the lower room during the inspection and did not know it had been opened. She had returned because Kreutz asked her to help identify old forms, not because she had been ordered to stay.

“Are you allowed to leave?” Himeno asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you choose to go back?”

“Yes.”

The answers came without hesitation, but Himeno did not treat that as proof of freedom. She asked Mio whether she wanted the school to contact anyone on her behalf. Mio said no. She would call again if that changed.

Before ending the call, Mio said the Matron had counted the Lantern House stairs every night. The old count had been eighty-eight. The stair behind the gate had eighty-eight steps too, according to the surveyor’s measurement.

Himeno did not write that the two staircases were the same. She recorded Mio’s statement and the surveyor’s independent count separately. A shared number could be a clue, a design choice, or coincidence. The route and materials still needed comparison.

Mio said she remembered the old staircase turning left at the forty-third step. The surveyor’s notes showed a landing after the first flight, but his step count restarted at each landing; his total was eighty-eight across the full descent. The two descriptions could be consistent, but no one had asked him to mark the turning point during the first inspection.

Himeno asked the surveyor to review his photographs and say whether the remembered turn could be identified. He found a bend in the handrail near the middle of the stairs, but the image did not show every step. He could not confirm that it came at forty-three. A second inspection would be needed, under the owners’ permission.

Mio did not offer to guide them. She said she had not been down the old stairs since she left. Himeno asked if she wanted to return to the site. Mio said she would decide later. The call ended with that choice still hers.

The conservator called at dusk. She had separated the first two sheets without loss. The top page was a relocation schedule dated three days before the fire. It listed thirty-one residents as transferred to named institutions. The second sheet began with another group of names, but the heading had been cut away.

At the bottom of the relocation schedule was a line in blue ink: “The east stairs remain open until the last count.”

The signature beneath it was not a name. It was the letter R.
