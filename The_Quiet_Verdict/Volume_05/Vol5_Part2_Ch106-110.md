# Chapter 106: The Joint Order

The agreement arrived with three signatures and one condition nobody had expected.

The trust would permit a survey of the lower landing. The Foundation would provide access through the east gate. The city would send an engineer and a conservator. Ishikawa could observe, but he could not remove anything. Hoshina could take notes from the marked boundary. Himeno could attend as the committee’s representative.

Mio was not on the list.

“She asked to come,” Himeno said.

The trust’s lawyer folded the request sheet. “She is not a property representative, a conservator, or an investigator.”

“She lived at Lantern House.”

“That may make her a witness. It does not make her part of the survey team.”

Ishikawa read the last page again. The language was exact: no person could enter the lower stair without written authorization from both owners. The agreement allowed one descent to the landing, visual documentation, and no handling of objects. It did not authorize a witness to guide the team or identify material.

Mio stood by the conference-room window. She had come in her own coat, without Kreutz. The headmistress had asked whether she wanted him contacted. Mio had said no.

“I don’t need to go below,” she said. “I can tell you what I remember before you go.”

The lawyer turned to her. “You understand that your account may become part of the investigation?”

“I understand that it already is.”

“Do you want counsel present?”

“I want someone who will write down what I say without changing it into what they need.”

Ishikawa looked at Himeno. “Would you sit with her?”

“If she wants me there.”

Mio nodded once.

The interview took place in the smaller room. Ishikawa asked her to describe the staircase without seeing the survey photographs. She said the old Lantern House stair turned left after a landing, then narrowed near the bottom. She remembered counting forty-three steps to the turn, but she had counted as a child, sometimes starting again after a stumble.

“Was it always forty-three?” Himeno asked.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I wanted it to be.”

Mio’s answer did not match the neatness of the surveyor’s notebook. It made the memory more useful, not less. Ishikawa asked whether she could recognize the lower room if she saw it.

“I might recognize a smell,” she said. “I won’t say I recognize a place from a photograph.”

The statement was read back to her. She changed one word: “narrow” became “felt narrow.” Then she signed the page.

Before she left, Mio asked to see the copy of her statement. Ishikawa gave it to her without the exhibit photographs. She read the last paragraph twice, then pointed to the sentence that said she had returned to Lantern House.

“I came back,” she said. “Returned sounds like I was sent.”

Himeno asked whether she wanted the wording changed. Mio said yes. The clerk replaced “returned” with “went back,” and recorded that the correction was made at Mio’s request. She signed the amended page, then asked for a copy she could keep.

The request delayed the meeting by nine minutes. No one tried to hurry her. When the copy was ready, Mio checked the correction before folding it into her coat.

Ishikawa asked whether she wanted the correction recorded as a clarification or as a disagreement with the interviewer. Mio said it was neither. She had used a word that sounded like an order when she meant a choice. The clerk added her explanation in the margin without erasing the first version.

“Will the first copy stay in the file?” Mio asked.

“Yes,” Ishikawa said. “The correction will be attached to it.”

“Then I want the receipt for both.”

The clerk made two entries. Mio checked the dates before signing. When she left, she carried the copy and its receipt separately.

Outside, Ren waited beside the reception desk. He wore his school uniform and carried no bag. The headmistress asked why he had come.

“To deliver the incident-log copies you requested.”

She looked at the envelope in his hand. “You could have sent them through the office.”

“I could have.”

“Why bring them yourself?”

“The office log would record the transfer.”

The answer did not explain whether he wanted the transfer recorded or wanted to be seen making it. The headmistress accepted the envelope but did not open it. She wrote the time on the receipt and asked him to sign beneath it.

Ishikawa came from the interview room. “You included the original pages?”

“Copies.”

“Why not the originals?”

“They include names of students who have not agreed to be part of this inquiry.”

“Then redact the names and bring the originals.”

Ren inclined his head. “That would change the record.”

“It would protect the students.”

“Not if the redaction hides who made the entry.”

Ishikawa held out his hand. “Then we’ll agree on a supervised copy.”

The headmistress asked the secretary to record who had received the packet and who held it while a supervised copy was prepared. The secretary added the receipt number to the office log. That record would prevent the selected pages from later being described as the complete incident log.

Mio emerged from the room. Ren turned toward her.

“Did you tell them about the turn?” he asked.

“I told them what I remember.”

“Good.”

“You don’t get to approve it.”

“No.”

She waited for an explanation. Ren offered none. Mio walked past him and out through the main doors.

The survey was scheduled for the following morning. Before Ishikawa left, he checked the authorization again. The lower landing was included. The room beyond it was not.

At 8:06 the next day, the gate opened with the locksmith’s key. The first photograph showed the empty path. The second showed the stair descending into the cliff.

The surveyor counted each step aloud.

At the forty-third, he stopped.

The landing was not there.

# Chapter 107: The Forty-Third Step

The surveyor counted the steps twice before he called the number.

Forty-three ended on stone, not on a landing. The turn lay four steps below it, where the wall curved inward and the handrail changed from iron to wood. The team photographed the change from above and below. No one touched the wood until the conservator had checked its surface.

The surveyor checked the tally against the marked drawing. His first count began at the threshold; the second began at the first full tread. The difference was one step, not four. The bend in the rail fell at forty-seven from the threshold and forty-six from the first tread.

Mio had remembered forty-three. She did not withdraw it or insist the survey was wrong. “I was nine. I counted when I was afraid of missing the turn.”

“Did someone tell you the number?” Himeno asked.

“No. I counted it myself. That doesn’t make the count accurate.”

The surveyor added both starting points to the diagram. The total descent remained eighty-eight steps to the lower landing, matching the earlier survey. The remembered turn did not match either count exactly.

Ishikawa asked the engineer whether the variation could come from a repaired tread. The engineer checked the photographs from the first inspection. One tread had a newer surface, but it was not at the bend. The discrepancy remained a memory against a measurement, not evidence of a hidden step.

The first inspection’s total had also started at the threshold, but its notes grouped steps by landing. The new drawing preserved both conventions instead of overwriting one with the other. The 88-step total remained consistent; the remembered turning point did not.

Ishikawa asked Mio whether the repeated number had been important to her at the time. She said she had counted because the stairs were dark and because adults praised her when she got the number right.

“Did anyone tell you what the count was for?”

“No. They only told me to keep going until I reached the bottom.”

He recorded the answer as memory, not corroboration. Mio watched him write it down and asked to read the line before she signed. He turned the notebook toward her.

Mio stood behind the marked line at the upper entrance. She had asked to watch from there, not to descend. Ishikawa had brought her statement and made sure the property owners understood she would not cross the boundary.

“Is that the turn you remember?” he called.

“I can’t see the bottom from here.”

The surveyor brought a live image up on a tablet. Mio looked at it for several seconds.

“I remember the rail being warmer,” she said. “Not the shape.”

The conservator placed a contactless thermometer near the wood. Its temperature matched the stone wall within the device’s margin. There was no evidence of recent warmth.

Hoshina wrote down the exchange. “So the memory doesn’t identify this stair.”

“It identifies what I remember,” Mio said. “You can decide what that proves.”

The team continued to the landing. The door at the end stood open by the width of a hand. The newer strike-plate screw remained in place, but the latch had not been engaged. Ishikawa stopped the group before anyone crossed the threshold.

The previous authorization allowed a visual survey of the landing. It did not explicitly cover entry into the room. The trust’s lawyer was present by telephone. The Foundation’s counsel was on the same call. They agreed to add a sentence permitting the engineer to inspect the threshold and the visible floor, provided nothing was moved.

The engineer read the sentence aloud. Ishikawa wrote the time beside it. Then the door was opened to its full width.

The room beyond was smaller than the first one and dry. A shelf ran along the left wall. On it lay a ceramic cup with a hairline crack, a brass fastener, and a strip of paper darkened at both ends. The floor beneath the shelf had a pale rectangle where dust had not settled.

No bed. No clothes. No sign that anyone had lived there recently.

The engineer photographed each object in place. The conservator recorded the paper’s visible text without touching it. Only two words could be read: “second count.”

Mio did not look at the image.

“Does that phrase mean anything to you?” Himeno asked.

“I heard it at Lantern House.”

“From whom?”

“I don’t know. Adults used it when they checked the children after meals.”

“Was it a formal term?”

“I don’t know.”

The conservator asked whether the paper could be stabilized. The lawyer allowed removal under a separate collection form. Ishikawa signed as witness; the engineer signed as custodian. The paper went into a rigid sleeve, not an evidence bag.

At the back of the shelf, a line had been scratched into the plaster. It consisted of eleven short marks and one longer one. The engineer photographed it with a scale. There was no date, name, or arrow.

“Could those be a count?” Hoshina asked.

“They could,” the surveyor said. “They could also be tool marks.”

Mio looked at the photograph on the tablet. “The long one is on the wrong side.”

“Wrong for what?” Ishikawa asked.

“For a child’s height chart. We marked the doorframe, not the wall behind a shelf.”

“Did you see a height chart here?”

“No.”

Her correction removed one easy interpretation. The marks did not become a code merely because they were deliberate.

The team moved no farther. A narrow passage continued behind the room, but the agreed survey ended at the next door. The engineers measured the opening from the threshold and checked the lintel. The passage was stable enough for a later inspection, not an immediate one.

At the surface, the property representatives signed the inspection log. The lower room’s location was confirmed within the survey boundary. The papers and objects had been collected under separate authority. The work had taken forty-one minutes.

Ren arrived before the team had packed the equipment. He had a letter from the headmistress granting permission to deliver the supervised incident logs.

Ishikawa read the letter. “This only authorizes a copy.”

“Then I brought a copy.”

The envelope contained a page-by-page photocopy with student names covered. The date, time, and writer’s initials remained visible.

“You’ve removed information,” Ishikawa said.

“I’ve covered names.”

“You’ve covered the initials too.”

Ren looked down. The black strips covered both. He had not noticed—or had chosen not to mention it.

Ishikawa returned the packet. “Make a new copy. The initials stay.”

Ren accepted it without protest. The inspector had found a limit in the version Ren offered, and Ren had no prepared answer that changed it.

“How many pages did you select?” Ishikawa asked.

“The ones that mention the boundary, the gate, or the service route.”

“Then this is not the incident log. It is your selection from the log.”

“Yes.”

“You should have said that before I opened it.”

Ren looked at the envelope in the inspector’s hand. “The request named the location. I understood that as a scope.”

“I asked for the records that refer to the east access. I did not ask you to decide which pages refer to it.”

The headmistress looked from one to the other. Ren had followed the wording as he read it; Ishikawa had assumed the office would gather the files. Neither assumption was written on the receipt.

Ishikawa asked the school secretary to freeze the full log, including pages Ren had not selected. The secretary agreed and printed a new receipt with the date range and file number. Ren signed that he had delivered a selected copy, not the complete record.

“That distinction will be in the inquiry,” Ren said.

“It should be,” Ishikawa replied.

The headmistress asked Ren to wait while the secretary prepared the complete record. He did not ask to leave. Ishikawa set the selected pages beside the new receipt and marked the envelope number on both.

“Do you want me to stay?” Ren asked.

“I want the complete file preserved. Whether you stay is your choice.”

Ren sat by the reception desk. A student came in to report a broken window latch and stopped when she saw the police forms. The secretary moved the interview into the adjacent room, where the student could make her report without being asked about the Lantern House inquiry.

When the secretary returned, Ren was still waiting. He signed the new receipt and asked for a copy. The headmistress gave him one, then sealed the unredacted incident log in the office cabinet.

On the way out, Mio passed the open gate. She did not turn toward the stairs.

That afternoon, the conservator’s first image of the paper was enlarged. Beneath “second count,” a faint line appeared in the margin. It was not a number.

It was a name, cut through the middle.

# Chapter 108: The Name Under the Cut

The conservator would not guess at the missing letters.

The paper had split along a fold. A blade or a sharp edge had passed through the name before the sheet was folded, but the cut removed only part of the ink. Under angled light, the surviving strokes suggested four or five characters. They could not establish the spelling.

Ishikawa asked the archive to compare the paper with the relocation schedule. The conservator refused to place the two objects on the same table until both had been stabilized. Instead, she compared photographs and recorded the limits of the images.

The papers were similar in weight and color. That was all.

Hoshina had already written a draft headline: “The Missing Children of Lantern House.” She deleted it before sending the story to her editor.

“You’re not going to publish?” Wataru asked.

“I’m going to publish what we know.”

“What do we know?”

“That a room exists. That a paper in it says ‘second count.’ That someone cut through a name.”

“People will say that means a child was hidden.”

“People can say it. I won’t.”

At the archive, the placement register and the recovered schedule were examined separately. The register’s seventy-one entries included thirty-one marked pending and thirty-one whose destination fields had been crossed out. The schedule named thirty-one residents transferred three days before the fire. The counts matched, but the documents did not share a reference number, author, or date range beyond the proximity to the fire.

The archivist closed the overlays before revealing any names. The privacy officer had prepared a count-only table, but even the aggregate categories carried a warning: the source pages had different purposes, and a total could not identify a person who moved between them.

Himeno asked whether the six acknowledged placements could be matched to the schedule. The archivist said the receiving records contained protected identifiers, and no consent or legal authority allowed the committee to compare them. Ishikawa submitted a formal request for an anonymized overlap count.

“If they refuse?” Hoshina asked.

“Then we report that we could not compare them,” Ishikawa said.

The answer disappointed Wataru. He had expected the numbers to line up and explain themselves. The archivist told him a matched total could still conceal duplicate entries, changed spellings, or a later transfer. He wrote that down, then crossed out the word “missing” in his notes.

The archivist placed transparent overlays over the two lists. The protected names remained covered. The category totals aligned; the written identifiers did not.

“They’re not the same list,” Himeno said.

“No,” the archivist replied. “Not on the pages we can compare.”

“Could one be a copy of the other?”

“Possibly. The schedule could have drawn from the register, or both could draw from a third source. The paper and handwriting differ.”

Himeno looked at the total again. Thirty-one names on the schedule. Thirty-one pending placements in the register. Thirty-one destination fields crossed through.

A number can repeat without being an answer.

Mio arrived for the second interview alone. She had agreed to look at the schedule’s visible categories, not the names. When the archivist showed her the heading, she moved her chair back from the table.

“Do you recognize it?” Ishikawa asked.

“I recognize the form.”

“From Lantern House?”

“From the office. The Matron used a similar one.”

“Is this the same form?”

“I can’t tell from a photograph.”

The archivist asked whether Mio wanted to see the original. She said no.

Ishikawa asked her to describe the office form. Mio said the sheets had a narrow left margin and a box for a second count. She had filled one in once, when she was nine. The instruction had been to count the children after the evening meal, then count again before the dormitory doors were locked.

“Why twice?” Himeno asked.

“To make sure the same number was there.”

“Was it always the same number?”

“No.”

The room went quiet.

“Why not?” Ishikawa asked.

“Some children were already asleep. Some were moved to the infirmary. Sometimes the Matron counted the staff with us. I don’t know what the column was for.”

Mio’s memory did not explain the schedule. It did explain why the phrase might belong to an ordinary routine, or to something more troubling. The word “count” could refer to children, paper, meals, or doors.

The archive’s privacy officer stopped the interview when Ishikawa asked whether Mio remembered the names beside her entries. The registry contained protected identities. Mio had not consented to discuss them, and the archive did not have authorization to expose them.

“I don’t want the names,” Mio said. “I want to know whether the form kept a record of who was counted.”

The archivist checked the fields. The recovered schedule listed initials beside each transfer. The second-count column had no heading on the visible portion. A separate sheet might explain it, but the bundle had not been fully separated.

Mio stood. “I’ll come back when you have the whole page.”

“Do you recognize the cut?” Ishikawa asked.

“No.”

“Could you identify the handwriting?”

“No.”

She left before anyone could ask her to look again.

That evening, Ren received a copy of the public survey summary. He read the sentence that called the lower room “a former service space.” He underlined “former,” then wrote a note to the headmistress: The survey confirms location, not period of use.

The headmistress read his note and placed it beside the engineer’s report. “You’re right.”

“The wording may become a headline.”

“It already has.”

“Then the correction should go out before the headline.”

She signed the correction and sent it to the board’s mailing list. One trustee objected that the wording made the school look evasive. The headmistress did not withdraw it.

At the archive, the conservator called Ishikawa at 9:12 p.m. The second sheet had separated cleanly. Its heading was missing, but its first line was intact.

“Do not close the east door until the second count is signed.”

The ink was visually similar in color to the “R” on the relocation schedule.

Hoshina asked for permission to publish the words without the personal names. The archive’s privacy officer said the phrase could be quoted, but the image could not be posted: the cut name and the adjacent initials remained visible even under the current redaction.

“Then I’ll quote the line and describe the limitation,” Hoshina said.

“You’ll need to say the ink comparison is preliminary,” Ishikawa told her.

“I will.”

Her editor called while she was still in the archive. He wanted a stronger headline before the evening edition. Hoshina offered “Lantern House records show a second-count instruction.” The editor said no one would click it.

“Then they can read the correction when the evidence is stronger,” she said, and ended the call.

The signature was not.

# Chapter 109: The Door That Stayed Open

The order had no date.

It appeared on the top edge of the second sheet, above a column of initials. The paper had been cut away at both corners. The ink matched the relocation schedule under the conservator’s first examination, but the writer had not signed it. A blue “R” appeared on another page, several sheets later.

Ishikawa asked whether the sheets had been written at the same time.

“Not necessarily,” the conservator said. “The ink color and paper stock are consistent. They do not give us a date.”

The line about the east door was copied into the archive inventory exactly as written. No one added the phrase “escape route.” No one called it a warning.

At Aster, the headmistress received the correction request from Ishikawa. The surveyors wanted a second inspection, this time to measure the passage beyond the room. The trust had not yet agreed to entry. Its lawyer asked whether the sentence on the recovered sheet established a connection to the gate.

“It establishes that someone wrote about an east door,” Ishikawa said. “It does not establish which door.”

The lawyer wanted a broader search warrant. Ishikawa said the police had not shown grounds to force entry, and he would not pretend that a paper fragment supplied them. The request remained voluntary.

The trust’s representative hesitated. Its director had received calls from parents, journalists, and former residents. If the trust declined, the refusal would look like concealment. If it agreed, people would treat access as proof of guilt.

Himeno sat at the end of the table. “You don’t have to choose what it looks like. You have to choose whether there is a safety reason to open it.”

The engineer said the passage could be inspected safely if the door remained open and the team used a tether. The conservator asked to accompany them. The lawyer requested a written scope, a fixed time limit, and a list of every person permitted to enter.

Ishikawa agreed to all three.

The next morning, the trust signed. The survey would proceed on Friday, for twenty minutes, with no removal of material. Mio asked to attend from the upper landing. The owners agreed, provided she stayed beyond the boundary marker.

Kreutz called before the inspection.

“I should be present,” he told Ishikawa.

“On what basis?”

“I ran Lantern House.”

“You ran it six years ago.”

“I know its records.”

“Do you know the room?”

A pause came through the line. “I know the institution.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Kreutz did not answer it. Ishikawa denied the request. He said the inspection was not a hearing and the old director had no special access. Kreutz asked if Mio would be there.

“She asked to attend. She has not been added to the entry team.”

“Then you should let her decide.”

“She has.”

Kreutz ended the call without saying goodbye.

Ishikawa wrote down the time of the call and the exact information Kreutz had been given. He had told the doctor only that the team would inspect a passage under the easement. Kreutz had not been told about a paper or a door. The police vehicles and the joint order were visible from the road.

“He could have inferred it,” Himeno said.

“He could,” Ishikawa replied. “We will not record inference as prior knowledge.”

He asked the receptionist to preserve the call log and the visitor camera, even though the camera did not cover the cliff road. The request could establish when Kreutz contacted the school. It could not establish what he had seen from outside.

The receptionist asked whether to log him as a visitor, since he had remained beyond the gate.

“Log the call and the arrival separately,” Ishikawa said. “Don’t turn one into the other.”

The receptionist created two entries. Kreutz signed neither; he had not entered the property. The distinction gave the school a record of his presence without implying he had been part of the inspection.

At school, Ren asked the headmistress whether the Foundation’s counsel had received the correction. She showed him the email receipt.

“Will it be published?” he asked.

“It’s already on the board site.”

“Then the first version remains beside it.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The headmistress watched him. “You wanted both versions preserved?”

“I wanted the correction to have a date.”

He had not asked for the original wording to be withdrawn. The board had kept it, with a note beneath it. A reader could compare the two and decide which one had arrived first.

On Friday, the team descended to the lower room. The door beyond the shelf opened without resistance. A corridor ran east for six meters, then turned sharply toward the cliff. The engineer measured the wall. The route did not point beneath the school.

At the turn, a thin line of pale paint crossed the floor. It continued under the wall and disappeared into a crack too narrow for a hand.

The conservator photographed it. “This could be a marking line.”

“Or paint from the door,” said the engineer.

They followed the corridor to a second door. It had no handle on the team’s side. The surface showed a clean rectangle where a plaque had once been fixed.

Mio stood above, just beyond the marked line. She had not descended.

“Is that the east door?” Ishikawa asked.

“I don’t know.”

The engineer checked the hinge side. It opened inward, toward a space beyond the wall. He asked for the written authorization. It covered visual inspection, not opening the door.

The team stopped. No one forced it.

Through the narrow gap beneath the door, a strip of paper had been pushed into the corridor. Its edge was fresh and white against the dust.

Ishikawa looked up the stairwell. “Nobody moves.”

The paper had not been there in the first photograph.

# Chapter 110: The Paper in the Gap

The paper did not move while they watched it.

Ishikawa asked everyone to step back from the door. The engineer marked each person’s position on the floor plan. The conservator photographed the strip with a scale. The photograph showed no writing, only a straight edge and a narrow crease.

The team had been below for six minutes.

No one had heard a door close. The surface above them remained occupied by the trust’s representative, the headmistress, two survey assistants, and Mio. All denied entering the corridor. The single camera at the stair entrance showed the team descending, then the upper landing empty. It did not cover the lower room.

The engineer asked whether the paper could have been hidden under the door before they arrived. The dust line beneath it was broken, but the corridor had been walked that morning. Their own footprints crossed the area.

“Could it have been carried on a shoe?” Hoshina asked.

“Possibly,” said the conservator. “But not pushed under the door by a shoe without leaving a different mark.”

“Could it have come from the room?”

“Possibly.”

“Could someone be on the other side?”

“Possibly.”

Ishikawa ordered the team back to the landing. He did not let anyone touch the door. The owners’ authorization did not permit an entry, and the new object changed the inspection from a survey into a potential scene.

On the surface, the trust’s representative asked what they had found.

“A paper strip under a door,” Ishikawa said. “We did not touch it.”

“Is there someone inside?”

“I don’t know.”

The representative looked toward the stair. “You can’t leave it there.”

“We can preserve the scene and request authority to open the door.”

“That could take days.”

“It could.”

The headmistress asked Mio whether she wanted to leave. Mio said she would stay until the police finished. She did not ask to enter.

Kreutz arrived at the outer gate without an appointment. The receptionist called Ishikawa. The inspector met him outside the cordon.

“You heard there is a paper under the door,” Kreutz said.

“I haven’t released that information.”

“Then someone else has.”

“Who?”

Kreutz looked through the gate toward the cliff. “You tell me.”

The statement sounded like an accusation, but he named no one. Ishikawa asked how he knew about the paper. Kreutz said he had not known until he saw the police vehicles. He had inferred that the survey had found something.

“Did you know there was another door?”

“I knew there were service doors.”

“Where?”

“I don’t recall.”

“Did you enter this passage?”

“No.”

Ishikawa asked him to remain available for a formal interview. Kreutz said he would. He did not ask to see Mio.

The legal authorization arrived at 2:40 p.m. It permitted the police to open the door, document the space, and remove any immediate hazard. It did not permit a full search. The team returned with a locksmith and a second camera operator.

The latch was on the far side. The locksmith used a thin inspection mirror to see the mechanism, then worked it through the gap without breaking the frame. The door opened inward by twelve centimeters and stopped against a chain.

The chain was taut.

No one pulled it. The locksmith photographed the links and the bolt. A narrow gap remained at the floor. The strip of paper lay on the other side, trapped beneath the door.

The engineer tested the air through the opening. The reading showed no immediate hazard. He asked for permission to widen the opening by removing the chain. Ishikawa checked the order. It covered immediate safety, not the removal of a restraint.

The trust’s lawyer was called again. The director was not available. The representative on site had no authority to expand the order.

The paper remained in the gap.

While they waited, Himeno noticed a mark on the inside face of the door: three short scratches above the latch. They were parallel and fresh enough to show pale wood beneath the paint. The camera had not captured them in the first view.

“Could the chain have made those?” she asked.

“Not from where it’s attached,” the locksmith said.

Mio stood at the top of the stairs. “Those are not counts.”

Ishikawa looked up. “What are they?”

“I don’t know.”

“Why say it?”

“Because you were about to call them marks.”

He looked back at the door. “They are scratches.”

The call from the trust came at 3:18. The director authorized removal of the chain, with each link logged and retained. The locksmith placed a shield beneath the bolt and cut the corroded section with hand tools. The chain fell inward and struck the floor once.

The door opened.

The space beyond it was empty.

No second staircase. No person. No room large enough to stand upright. Only a narrow alcove, the end of a capped drain, and a wall of old brick.

The paper strip lay on the floor. The conservator lifted it with tweezers and placed it in a sleeve. It held a single typed line:

“Count the door that stays open.”

The print was dry. The paper looked clean, but its age had not been tested.

The conservator amended her wording before the evidence label was signed. “Clean” described the visible surface. The paper had not been tested for age, and the dust beneath the door could have been disturbed by the earlier inspection.

Ishikawa asked whether the ink could be matched to the archive printer. The archive used a common toner cartridge and did not keep a page-by-page print log. The paper stock was sold by several suppliers. The line gave them a message, not an author.

The locksmith’s bright cut on the chain was made during the authorized entry. It could not prove when the chain had first been fitted or whether the door had been opened before the team arrived. The three scratches above the latch were photographed, but their age could not be determined by sight.

The only firm sequence was narrower: the strip did not appear in the first clear photograph of the threshold, and it was visible in the next image. The team compared the originals, not the compressed copies sent to the committee. There was a four-minute gap between exposures.

Four minutes was enough for a person to move through the upper entrance while the surveyors checked the stair. It was also enough for a paper edge to be missed in a shadow and noticed later. No one had logged every face in the upper passage during that interval.

Ishikawa added the gap to the report. He did not call it proof of entry. The people present were asked separately where they had stood and what they could see. Their statements would be compared only after each was signed.

The locksmith sealed the chain fragments in the order they had been removed. The inventory separated the rusted links, bolt, and section cut during the authorized inspection; the locksmith signed as the cutter, the engineer as collector, and the trust representative countersigned the seal numbers. No entry assigned an age to the chain or dated its installation.

The representative asked whether the fresh metal meant someone had recently forced the door. The locksmith said the bright edge came from his tool. He could not infer when the chain was installed from the condition of that cut.

Mio asked whether the team would return. Ishikawa said the next step depended on the order and the owners’ permission. She said she would decide whether to attend after she saw the written scope.

“You don’t have to come back,” Himeno said.

“I know,” Mio answered. “That’s why I’ll decide when I know what they’re asking me to do.”

Ishikawa looked at the chain on the floor. Its cut end had a bright edge. The other end, still attached to the bolt, was black with age.

The chain’s bright cut belonged to the locksmith. The scratches and the paper could not yet tell them who had opened the door—or when.

And someone had wanted it closed again.
