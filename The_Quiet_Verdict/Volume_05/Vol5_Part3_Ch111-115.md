# Chapter 111: The Terms

---

The order to rebuild the task force came with a page of limits.

The district chief approved a six-week records inquiry, not a general investigation into every death connected to Lantern House. The team could request municipal files, preserve school and Foundation records, and interview willing witnesses. It could not publish protected names or compel the trust to open another door without new authority. Ishikawa read the scope aloud in the station conference room before anyone signed.

“You want a task force with one hand tied,” said Detective Mori, the youngest officer at the table.

“I want a task force that knows which hand is holding the rope,” Ishikawa said.

The group consisted of Ishikawa, Mori, an evidence clerk, and an archive specialist seconded from the prefectural records office. Himeno would attend as the school committee’s representative when school records were discussed. Hoshina could observe public briefings as a journalist, but she would receive no protected material. None of them would have access to Mio’s unredacted statement unless her consent and the inquiry’s rules allowed it.

There was no place on the order for Wataru.

His old liaison credentials had been revoked when the previous Task Force was dissolved. Ishikawa had asked the district chief to consider him as an outside records adviser. The request came back with a condition: Wataru could help organize documents already public or supplied with permission, but he could not question witnesses or receive sealed evidence.

Wataru read the condition twice. “You want the part of me that can make a chronology and not the part that knew the people.”

“I want the part you choose to offer,” Ishikawa said. “The terms are written. You can refuse.”

Wataru turned to the last page. The form asked him to declare any prior involvement with the records. He wrote that he had been a liaison to the dissolved Task Force, had interviewed school staff, and had once seen copies of the incident log. He added that he had not kept copies.

“Will that be enough?” Mori asked.

“It will be enough to start,” said Ishikawa. “If it isn’t, we stop.”

Wataru signed. His name appeared under “external adviser,” not “investigator.” The distinction did not restore his old badge, but it gave him a reason to be in the room without pretending he had never been there before.

The first meeting concerned the incident logs Ren had offered in Chapter 106. The headmistress brought the complete office log, sealed in a numbered envelope. Ren’s selected photocopies remained separate. Every page had a date range; every redaction had a notation; the initials of the person who made each entry were visible. Ishikawa asked the secretary to read the transfer history aloud.

The original log had never left the school cabinet. The envelope Ren brought had contained selected copies, not pages removed from the original. The secretary confirmed that the first receipt described them as “copies.” Ren had not altered the log, but the selection still omitted the pages surrounding the entries he had chosen.

“Do the missing pages refer to the east access?” Mori asked.

“We don’t know until we review them,” the secretary said. “That is why I brought the complete log.”

Wataru kept his hands away from the envelope. “Then we review all of it. We don’t start from Ren’s selection and call the rest irrelevant.”

The archive specialist assigned a number to the complete log. The selected copies received a second number. The team would compare them only after the school’s privacy officer prepared a supervised redacted set. The original would remain in the cabinet, and the secretary would keep the key.

Ren arrived halfway through the meeting with the headmistress. He carried no bag and asked to see the receipt, not the sealed pages. Ishikawa showed him the entry documenting the selected packet.

“You recorded it as incomplete,” Ren said.

“I recorded it as selected,” Ishikawa replied. “We haven’t yet determined whether it is incomplete for the question we asked.”

“That is accurate.”

The headmistress said the full log had been preserved. Ren read the receipt once, then returned it. He did not ask to see what the other pages contained.

“Do you object to the review?” Mori asked.

“No.”

“Do you want to add anything to the record?”

“Only that the copy I delivered was selected.”

The secretary wrote the sentence on a supplement and asked him to initial it. He did.

After he left, Wataru looked at the door. “He made the distinction himself.”

“He made sure it was recorded,” said Ishikawa. “That is not the same as telling us why he chose those pages.”

The team’s first review found three entries about the east gate. One recorded a maintenance call. One listed a parcel delivery. The third named no visitor, only a time and a two-letter mark. The writing was legible, but the secretary could not identify the person who had entered it. The ink had not been compared with any other document.

The entry fell on a date when the school had no surviving visitor-camera footage. The log showed that a camera technician had signed in that morning. It did not say whether the camera was repaired or merely inspected.

Ishikawa asked the records clerk to preserve the technician’s work order. The clerk found the number in the old maintenance index. The original was stored off-site, and its box had not yet been opened.

Wataru wrote the number on a clean sheet. “We have a date, a mark, and a missing work order.”

“We have a request for a work order,” Ishikawa corrected.

The clerk called the storage office. The box had been transferred twice. Its contents list named “security and electrical maintenance,” but no individual forms. The storage office agreed to hold it while the Foundation authorized inspection.

At the end of the day, the district chief asked Ishikawa whether the team had found a lead.

“We found a record that tells us what to request next,” Ishikawa said.

The chief looked at the three entries on the board. “And the two-letter mark?”

“Unidentified.”

“Could it be R?”

“It could be two letters. It could be one letter written badly. We haven’t established even that.”

The chief left the label blank.

When the room was empty, Wataru placed the receipt for his adviser role beside the new evidence request. One document described what he was allowed to do. The other described what the team did not yet know.

The first file opened was not the one Ren had selected.

# Chapter 112: The Stopped Watches

---

The girl arrived at the archive with a guardian’s signature and four stopped watches.

She placed the watches on the table in a straight line, each face turned toward the same wall. A fifth remained on her wrist, half-hidden by a white glove. She did not wind any of them. The archive officer asked whether they were part of the evidence.

“No,” the girl said. “They’re mine.”

Her name was Nia Vale. She was fifteen and had come with Dr. Sagara, a retired investigator who now taught records analysis at a regional institute. He had recommended her for a limited youth research placement after she challenged the chronology in a publicly released cold-case packet. The placement did not make her a police consultant. It did not give her access to names, statements, or sealed files.

Ishikawa read the consent form before greeting her. Nia’s guardian had signed permission for her to observe non-sensitive material under Sagara’s supervision. The archive’s youth fellowship would cover travel and meals. The administrative copy described the fund as an unrestricted grant; the donor field was blank on the sheet the archive had received.

“Why did you ask to come?” Ishikawa said.

“Your public summary says the strip was visible in one photograph and absent in another. I want to know what you mean by ‘absent.’”

“The first image did not show it.”

“Was the threshold visible?”

“Yes.”

“Entirely?”

“The door and the floor beneath it were in frame.”

“Then that’s more useful than saying it wasn’t there.”

Sagara leaned back. “She means the report should describe what the photograph shows, not what the team assumes happened between photographs.”

Nia turned to him. “I mean both.”

The archive officer laid out a public copy of the survey summary, the two released image thumbnails, and the authorization. The photographs were low resolution and carried no embedded data. Nia asked if the original files were available. Ishikawa said they were evidence, not teaching material. She could request a supervised review of the metadata through the records unit, but not copy the files.

“Would I be allowed to write down the capture times?”

“If the unit approves it, and if you record the source and limitations.”

“Then I’ll wait for that.”

She did not ask Sagara to make an exception. He looked at her watches, then at the summary. “You could work from what’s public.”

“I could. It would answer a different question.”

The archive specialist asked what cold case had brought her to records analysis. Nia said it was an old disappearance from a coastal station. The surviving report gave a minute-by-minute timeline, but its three clocks had never been compared. A mentor had given her the published packet as an exercise. She found that one clock had been reset after the witness statement.

“Did that solve the case?” Wataru asked.

“No.”

“Did it change the chronology?”

“It changed what the chronology could prove.”

Wataru nodded. “That’s a less popular answer.”

“It was still the answer.”

Ishikawa approved a narrow task: Nia could prepare a timeline from public documents and propose questions for the records team. Sagara would supervise. Any notes would be reviewed for personal information before they entered the file. She would not speak to witnesses on behalf of the police.

Nia read the conditions. “If I find a question that requires a protected document, I mark it as unanswered?”

“Yes,” Ishikawa said.

“Not ask someone else to tell me?”

“Correct.”

She signed the acknowledgment. Her guardian’s permission was attached, not substituted for her own signature. Sagara signed as supervisor.

The watches stayed in a row beside her papers. Wataru asked whether she collected them.

“I keep the ones that stopped during cases I haven’t finished reading.”

“Do you know when each stopped?”

“Not always.”

“Then why keep them?”

“Because the time on the face is still a claim. It isn’t the time the watch stopped unless someone checked it.”

Wataru glanced at Ishikawa. “We’ve heard that before.”

“No,” Nia said. “You heard something similar. That doesn’t make it the same.”

The archivist put the public images beside the release form. The first showed the door and the threshold. The second showed the same angle, with a pale strip visible under the edge. The low-resolution copies made the dust line difficult to compare.

Nia did not touch the pages. “I can’t tell whether the paper was there in the first image.”

“You said it wasn’t,” Mori replied.

“I said the image didn’t show it. That is not the same statement.”

The correction went into the minutes. No one had to retract the observation that the strip was absent from the first clear photograph. But the word “absent” would not be used as a conclusion about the physical space unless the original files supported it.

Sagara asked Nia whether she wanted to proceed with the placement. The work might take days and might produce no answer. She looked down the row of stopped watches, then closed the case holding them.

“I want to see the records I’m allowed to see,” she said. “If that doesn’t answer the question, I’ll write that.”

The archive officer added her name to the visitor log and wrote “student observer” in the role column. Nia checked the wording before leaving the room.

“Not investigator?” she asked.

“Not under this order,” Ishikawa said.

“Good.”

On her way out, she stopped at the reception desk. The archive clerk had listed Sagara as the person responsible for her travel reimbursement. Nia read the line, then asked for a copy of the form. The clerk said it would be sent to the fellowship office.

“I’d like to keep a copy too.”

“Why?”

“So I know what I agreed to.”

The clerk printed one. The donor field remained blank. Nia folded the copy and put it inside the watch case.

At the archive door, she turned back to the two photographs.

“Do you know who was holding the camera?” she asked.

“The surveyor,” said Ishikawa.

“Both times?”

“Yes.”

“Was he the only person who could reach the threshold?”

“No.”

Nia nodded once. The question went onto her page beneath the capture times she did not yet have.

The record had named the photographer. It had not named everyone who could reach the door.

# Chapter 113: Four Minutes

---

The original photographs were opened in the evidence room under a two-person log.

Nia sat on the visitor side of the table with Sagara beside her. She wore no gloves; she was not handling the device or the files. The evidence clerk opened the read-only copies, checked their hashes against the preservation record, and displayed the metadata on a separate screen. Nia wrote the file numbers before she wrote the times.

The first image was captured at 2:17:08. The second at 2:21:12. Four minutes and four seconds separated them. The camera clock had been compared with the survey team’s radio clock at the beginning of the inspection. The difference was three seconds. No later check had been made.

“Are the files original?” Nia asked.

“The device originals were preserved,” the clerk said. “These are verified copies.”

“Was the clock reset between exposures?”

“No.”

“Can the metadata show who had the camera?”

“No. The photographer is recorded in the field log.”

“Who else had access to the threshold during the interval?”

The clerk looked at Ishikawa. He answered. “The team above the landing, the property representative, two assistants, and Mio. The log records where they were at the beginning and end, not every movement.”

Nia wrote: *Positions are sampled, not continuous.*

Wataru read the line from across the table. “That sounds like a finding.”

“It’s a description of the log.”

“Could someone have placed the paper in four minutes?” Mori asked.

“Yes.”

“Could it have been there all along?”

“Yes.”

“Could it have come from inside the alcove?”

“Yes.”

Mori looked at the page. “So you’ve told us nothing.”

“I’ve told you the log can’t distinguish those possibilities.” Nia turned to Ishikawa. “If the report says the paper appeared during the gap, that’s stronger than the evidence. The photograph shows it in the second exposure. It doesn’t show when it arrived.”

Ishikawa asked the clerk to add the clock check and the gap between captures to the evidence note. The clerk recorded the camera’s limited coverage and attached the field log. The correction did not weaken the fact that the strip was documented only in the later image. It narrowed what that fact meant.

The team reviewed the site authorization next. It listed permitted entrants by role but did not require sign-in and sign-out at each level. The visitor log had names. The field plan had positions. Neither showed continuous movement. The gap was procedural, not proof that anyone had crossed the boundary unnoticed.

“Can we fix it before the next inspection?” Mori asked.

“Only if the owners authorize another inspection,” Ishikawa said. “We can propose a live access log, fixed camera coverage, and a named person responsible for each boundary.”

“Would that guarantee nobody moves?” Wataru asked.

“No.”

“Then what does it do?”

“It gives us a better record of who moved.”

Nia wrote that down. She did not add “therefore no one could enter.”

After the review, Ishikawa asked her to prepare a two-column timeline: confirmed sequence and unknown intervals. Nia requested that the document remain separate from the police’s narrative report until Sagara checked it for protected information. The request was granted. Her status as a student did not let her bypass review; her status as a witness to the file-handling process did not make her a police employee.

The timeline began with the authorization, the team’s descent, the first photograph, the second photograph, the withdrawal, the legal order, and the later opening of the door. The chain was cut only after the director’s approval. Nia kept that entry distinct from the four-minute interval.

At the bottom, she wrote: “No continuous record covers the threshold between exposures.”

She brought it to the team. Ishikawa read the sentence, then turned to the field log. “The landing is covered by the surveyor’s notes.”

“The landing is,” Nia said. “The threshold is not.”

“That distinction matters?”

“It matters if you want to say who could reach the paper.”

Mori looked annoyed, but he checked the plan. The door sat just beyond the camera’s edge. The surveyor had noted that the team stood back while the conservator examined the hinge. The log did not identify which assistant held the measuring rod or who crossed behind the engineer.

Ishikawa wrote a question for the next witness interview. He did not change the report’s conclusion.

The archive clerk asked Nia to sign a note confirming she had seen the metadata and not copied the image files. She read it, corrected “viewed the original photographs” to “viewed verified copies of the preserved device originals,” and signed beside the correction.

“You keep correcting our labels,” Mori said.

“Only when the label says more than the thing.”

He took the form back. “That will take longer.”

“Yes.”

That afternoon, Hoshina asked Ishikawa whether she could publish the exact times. He said she could quote the public summary, which listed the four-minute gap, but not the file hashes or the camera’s serial number. She asked whether the clock was accurate.

“Accurate to the check we performed,” he said. “Not proven accurate at every second.”

“Can I say the photos were four minutes apart?”

“You can say the recorded capture times were four minutes and four seconds apart, with a three-second check at the start.”

Hoshina wrote it down. “That’s not a dramatic sentence.”

“It’s the one the record supports.”

She published it that evening. Her article said the new review narrowed the photographic sequence but did not identify who placed the paper. The comments accused her of protecting the trust. Another post claimed the police had erased a fifth photograph.

The evidence clerk checked the camera index. There were two images in the relevant sequence, not three. The device had no deleted-file marker for that interval. The team logged the check but did not issue a second statement based on an online rumor.

Nia read the article the next morning. She did not respond to the comments. Instead, she returned the timeline with a new line under the unknown interval: the public had filled it with a photograph that did not exist.

The archive officer stamped her page “student analysis—not evidence.” Nia accepted the stamp. She put it beside her watches, not on top of them.

The next inspection had not been approved. But the team’s proposed access log now included a place for every person’s position at each photograph, not merely the names on the authorization.

At the bottom of the revised form, one field remained blank: who would watch the watchers.

# Chapter 114: The Second Count

---

Mio returned to the archive because she had asked to see the form before anyone asked her to identify it.

The original remained inside its conservation sleeve. The archivist displayed a high-resolution image with names and initials masked. The heading was incomplete. A narrow column at the right had been labeled “second count.” Beneath it, the visible rows carried short marks that could be initials, checks, or damage to the paper.

Mio stood rather than taking the chair. “I don’t want to see any names.”

“They are covered,” the archivist said.

“Keep them covered if you change the image.”

The archivist confirmed that the screen would not show the unredacted layer. Ishikawa read the interview scope aloud: Mio could comment on the form’s appearance and her own memories; she was not being asked to authenticate the page or identify another child.

Nia sat across the room with her notes closed. She had requested permission to observe only if Mio agreed. Mio had said she could stay, but not ask questions.

Ishikawa turned the screen toward Mio. “Do you recognize this form?”

“I recognize a kind of form. I can’t say this is the one we used.”

“What do you remember about the second count?”

“After the meal, an adult counted us once. Later, someone counted again before the dormitory doors were closed.”

“Who was counted?”

“The children who were there. Sometimes staff were included. Sometimes someone from the infirmary came back while the second count was happening.”

“Did the numbers always match?”

“No.”

“Did a mismatch mean someone had been transferred?”

“No. It meant they counted again or asked where the person was.”

Ishikawa recorded her words exactly. “Do you remember who signed?”

“An adult. I don’t remember which one.”

“Did the children sign?”

“No.”

The archivist asked whether the short marks on the image looked like the marks Mio had seen. She leaned toward the screen without touching it.

“I can’t tell. They’re too small, and the paper is damaged.”

“Would you like to see a larger version?”

“No. The names are near the edge. I already said I don’t want them.”

The archivist closed the image. The refusal was added to the record as a limit on the review, not as an answer about the document.

Nia kept her notes shut. When Ishikawa looked toward her, she said, “I have no question.”

Mio glanced at her. “You could ask one.”

“I was asked not to.”

“That doesn’t mean you have to be silent.”

“It means the question is yours to invite.”

Mio looked back at the closed screen. “Then I’ll ask one. Why does the report call the thirty-one names a relocation schedule?”

“The heading on the recovered page says residents moved before the fire,” Ishikawa said. “The page lists thirty-one names and institutions.”

“Does it say the second count was complete?”

“No.”

“Does it say everyone got where they were supposed to go?”

“No.”

Mio nodded. “Then don’t say the count proves they left.”

The archivist entered the wording as a recommendation for the public summary. She did not amend the historical document. The schedule still recorded thirty-one people as transferred. Mio’s account did not disprove it; it established that a separate count of people present could vary for ordinary reasons and was not the same thing as a transfer record.

Himeno arrived with the committee’s draft statement. It called the thirty-one entries “unaccounted children.” Mio asked to see the sentence. Himeno read it aloud, then crossed it out.

“What should it say?” she asked.

“Thirty-one entries,” Mio replied. “Not children we’ve proven were missing.”

“Some were children.”

“Then prove which ones before you put their names in a sentence.”

Himeno wrote: “The schedule lists thirty-one residents as transferred; the receiving records have not been matched.” She showed Mio the new line. Mio read it once and said it was accurate as far as she could tell.

Nia spoke only after Himeno turned to her. “The phrase ‘second count’ is in both kinds of record?”

“It could be,” Mio said. “I don’t know if this page is the same kind.”

“Thank you.”

That was all. Nia made no note about whether Mio’s memory supported a hidden room or a routine. She recorded that the witness distinguished the terms and declined to identify the document.

The archive’s privacy officer asked Mio to sign the statement summary. Mio read every line. She changed “Mio identified the form as Lantern House’s” to “Mio recognized a similar form but could not identify this page.” She initialed the change and requested a copy.

At the door, Himeno asked whether she wanted to speak to Kreutz. Mio said no. She did not say never. She said no today.

Nia closed her watch case. The four stopped faces remained turned toward the wall. Mio noticed the one on Nia’s wrist.

“Does it always stop at that time?”

“I don’t know when it stopped.”

“Then why keep it?”

“Because I haven’t finished finding out what the time means.”

Mio studied her for a moment. “You can finish a question without finding out what you want.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Nia did not answer. She put the watch back under her sleeve.

The next day, the archive published the corrected description of the schedule. The first version remained online with a dated correction. Hoshina quoted the correction and left the names out of her story. The trust’s lawyer objected that the phrase “receiving records have not been matched” implied an obligation to match them. The privacy officer answered that it described the current state, not a legal duty.

The board asked Ishikawa whether Mio’s testimony changed the evidence.

“It changes how we describe the second count,” he said. “It does not identify the cut name, verify the schedule, or tell us where any person went.”

The board chair thanked him and asked whether the committee could still call it a missing-children case.

“You can call the inquiry what you like,” Ishikawa said. “You cannot call the records proof of something they don’t say.”

Mio’s statement went into the file under a restricted number. The public copy used her chosen wording and included the fact that she had declined to identify the page. It did not include the question she had asked about the thirty-one names.

That evening, the archive received a request to inspect the original form. The requester was Dr. Kreutz. His note did not ask to see the cut name or the schedule. It asked for the blank reverse side.

# Chapter 115: Across the Table

---

Kreutz asked to see the reverse side because the front had already been described.

Ishikawa scheduled the review at the archive, with the conservator present and both parties’ counsel informed. The original would remain in its sleeve. Kreutz could view the scanned reverse; Ren could attend as a witness, but neither could handle the paper. Mio was not invited. Ishikawa sent her the scope anyway, so she could object if the meeting touched material she had asked to keep private.

She did not object. She did not attend.

Ren arrived with the headmistress. Kreutz came alone, carrying a flat envelope. Ishikawa asked whether it contained evidence.

“It contains a form from my office,” Kreutz said. “I don’t know if it came from Lantern House or from the clinic. I thought the reverse might tell me whether it belongs beside the recovered page.”

The conservator logged the envelope before opening it. Inside was a blank form with the same narrow margin and a box for a second count. It had no names, date, or signature. The paper stock was common. The bottom edge had been torn rather than cut.

Kreutz set it beside the screen, not the original. “That is why I asked to see the reverse. The front has the same kind of field. The back may show how the sheet was meant to be folded.”

“Or it may show how this one was folded,” Ishikawa said.

“Yes.”

The conservator displayed the reverse of the recovered page. A faint impression ran along one edge, but the marks were too weak to read. The blank form showed a crease down the center and a small box printed on the back for an office stamp. The recovered page had no visible stamp impression.

Kreutz leaned toward the screen. “The form from my envelope was used for evening rounds.”

“Do you know that from the paper?” Ren asked.

“From the way it was folded.”

“The crease is on the blank form,” Ren said. “The recovered page has a different crease.”

Kreutz looked again. “You’re right.”

The conservator enlarged the image. The recovered page had been folded into thirds. The blank form had been folded in half. The reverse did not establish whether the two pages came from the same office, much less the same year.

Kreutz put his hands on the table. “I remembered the forms in a stack. I wanted to know if the memory was accurate.”

“You could have said that before we opened the envelope,” Ishikawa replied.

“I could have.”

Ren remained still. “Would you like the record to say you recognized it?”

“No. I’d like it to say I thought I recognized it, then corrected myself.”

The conservator wrote the distinction in the handling note. The blank form was assigned a separate number and returned to Kreutz under a receipt. The recovered page stayed in the archive. The meeting had produced a comparison, not a match.

Kreutz looked at the blank form in its sleeve. “You were nine when the second count began.”

“I don’t remember when it began,” Ren said.

“You remember the phrase.”

“I remember adults using it.”

“Did it frighten you?”

Ishikawa interrupted. “That is outside today’s scope.”

“It is a question about his memory.”

“It is a question about a person who is not here as a witness to the document.”

Ren looked at the inspector. “I can answer if you want it on the record.”

“You don’t have to,” Ishikawa said.

Kreutz waited. The room held the silence long enough for the archive clerk to stop typing.

“I don’t know,” Ren said. “I remember the words. I don’t remember what I felt when I heard them.”

Kreutz lowered his eyes to the blank form. “That is an answer.”

“It is what I know.”

Ishikawa recorded the exchange under the meeting scope. He did not include the question in the document-comparison report.

Kreutz slid the envelope back toward himself. “I kept these forms because I thought they would help me remember what the routine was.”

“Did you keep a register of where they came from?” Ren asked.

“No.”

“Then the forms can’t tell you which building used them.”

“They might tell us what the routine looked like.”

“Only if they are from the same routine.”

Kreutz smiled without warmth. “You have learned to make the smallest claim possible.”

“I learned that larger ones are easier to repeat.”

Himeno, seated by the wall, turned a page in her notebook. “The form you brought has no date or provenance. The archive’s page has a partial name and a line about the second count. Neither explains the thirty-one transfers.”

“Correct,” Ishikawa said.

Kreutz did not look at Himeno. “You are building a file that can say nothing with perfect accuracy.”

“A file that says nothing may still preserve the next thing we learn,” she said. “A file that says too much can make it impossible to see.”

The meeting ended with three actions. Kreutz would search his remaining office records for a source inventory. The archive would retain the scan of the blank form but not claim it as a Lantern House document. Ishikawa would request the stored maintenance box for the two-letter gate entry. No one would interview Mio about the form again without her consent.

At the door, Kreutz asked Ren to stay a moment. Ishikawa said the conversation could continue only if it remained within the recorded scope. Kreutz agreed.

“The Matron kept the old count books,” he said. “I did not.”

Ren gave no answer.

“Would you ask her for them?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“She can decide whether to provide them.”

“And if she refuses?”

“Then the file records that she refused.”

Kreutz watched him. “You have learned to make other people’s choices look like evidence.”

“People’s choices are evidence of what they chose.”

“Not why.”

“No.”

Kreutz picked up his envelope. “Then perhaps you understand the problem.”

He left the room without asking to see Mio. Ren waited for the clerk to return the receipt for the comparison, checked that it described the blank form as “source unverified,” and handed it back.

The archive added a note to the inquiry log: Kreutz believed the Matron retained count books; no inventory or independent witness had confirmed their existence.

At 6:40 that evening, a new message arrived from the Matron’s solicitor. The Matron was willing to meet Ishikawa, but only if Mio was not present and Kreutz was not told what she said.

Ishikawa read the condition twice. The next record would begin with a person who had refused to be counted in the room.
