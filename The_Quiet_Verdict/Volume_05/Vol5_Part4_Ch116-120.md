# Chapter 116: The Condition

---

The Matron arrived at the station with a lawyer, a cloth case, and one condition she had not put in writing.

She would speak without Dr. Kreutz or Mio in the room. She would not identify a child from a record unless that person had already consented. The interview could be recorded, but the transcript would remain restricted until she had reviewed the parts attributed to her. Ishikawa agreed to the first two conditions. He could not promise the third: she could correct the transcript, but not veto an accurate answer because she disliked it.

Her lawyer considered the distinction, then nodded. The recorder was placed where she could see it. A second device logged the start time. The evidence clerk wrote down the room number, the people present, and the fact that the Matron had brought a cloth case herself.

It contained two slim account books and a packet of folded slips. The covers were gray, without labels. Ishikawa asked whether she was offering them as evidence.

She said she had brought the books because the inquiry had begun to use a number from a page she could not authenticate. She would rather let a conservator document what she had kept than let a newspaper reproduce a blurred photograph and call it a roster. She did not say whether the books supported the police or the school.

“I am offering them for examination,” she said. “The originals stay with my lawyer until we agree on a preservation plan.”

“That is not the same as surrendering them.”

“No. I will not surrender them today.”

Ishikawa asked whether she feared the books would be altered in her custody. The Matron said she feared the opposite: that a page removed from its binding would acquire a certainty it had never possessed. Her lawyer added that preservation did not require immediate transfer of ownership.

“If a court orders production?” Mori asked.

“I will comply with a lawful order,” the Matron said. “That is not the same as volunteering every page before anyone has recorded what it is.”

Ishikawa wrote both positions into the agreement. The archive specialist would photograph the spines, bindings, and page edges before reading the entries. The original sequence would be preserved as found; no missing page would be described as removed unless the binding showed it.

The lawyer slid a signed letter across the table. It authorized the archive specialist to photograph the pages in the room, under supervision, and to prepare a sealed restricted copy. No page could be removed. The Matron would retain custody of the originals, and the police could request a court order if the inquiry later required one.

Ishikawa read every line. “We can accept this as a voluntary review. We cannot call it a full search or a complete record.”

“Then don’t,” she said.

The first book covered four months of evening counts. Its pages were ruled in narrow columns: date, first total, second total, correction, initials. The entries were mostly numbers. Several were crossed through once and rewritten in the margin. A few ended with a short dash where the initials belonged.

“Who made these?” Ishikawa asked.

“I did some. The night staff did the others.”

“Can you identify which ones are yours?”

“Not all. We changed shifts. I was not the only person who counted.”

“Were these counts of residents?”

“Of people present at the rooms we were responsible for.”

“That is not necessarily the same thing.”

“No.”

The Matron did not lean toward the book. She watched the archive specialist photograph the first spread, then asked her to include the page edges and binding. The specialist explained that the images would record visible condition, not prove when an entry had been made.

The second book covered a different period. It had a column headed “before lock.” Some nights the second total was lower than the first. Beside one entry, a note read “infirmary.” Another said “staff on road.” The Matron identified those as ordinary explanations but could not remember the people involved.

“Why keep a count if people were elsewhere?” Mori asked.

“Because ‘elsewhere’ had to be checked. A number is not an explanation.”

“Did the count decide whether a child could leave?”

“No. It told us whether the room was empty.”

Mori asked whether a child could be sent to another building between counts. The Matron said that was possible, but the books did not record where a person went after leaving her room. A name might appear in another book, if one existed; she could not promise that the records had been kept together.

“Did you ever compare the totals across buildings?” Ishikawa asked.

“Not routinely. Each supervisor was responsible for a room. The director might ask for a combined number, but I don’t remember a combined book.”

The archive specialist marked the scope of the pages: room-level counts, not institution-wide totals. The distinction ruled out one tempting shortcut. No one could add the rows together and call the result a roster.

Ishikawa paused. “Which room?”

“The dormitory I was responsible for.”

“Not every Lantern House building?”

“No.”

“Not a transfer list?”

“No.”

The answer reduced the reach of the books. It also made them more exact. The columns recorded people present in particular rooms at two times. They did not record destination, authorization, or the reason anyone was absent.

The Matron said the books had been used to notice discrepancies, not to explain them. A supervisor was supposed to follow up in person or ask the next shift.

“Was that follow-up written anywhere?” Ishikawa asked.

“Sometimes in the correction column. Sometimes in a separate note. Sometimes not at all.”

“So a blank correction does not mean no one checked.”

“No. And a note does not mean the check was finished.”

The folded slips were separate. Each carried a date and a brief instruction: leave the corridor clear; keep the east door unlatched until the count; report a broken lamp. Some were signed. Others were not. The Matron said she had saved them because they had been passed between shifts and sometimes contradicted one another.

“Who wrote this one?” Ishikawa asked, pointing to the instruction about the east door.

“I don’t know.”

“You saved it.”

“I saved what was left in the desk. I did not write every note.”

“Was the door at Lantern House?”

“I believe so. I can’t prove that from this slip.”

Her lawyer asked for a break. The recorder was stopped at 10:42. The clerk marked the stop time, and no one discussed the contents while the devices were off. When the interview resumed, the Matron read back the last answer and changed “the door at Lantern House” in the draft transcript to “a door I believed belonged to Lantern House.”

Ishikawa accepted the correction. The original wording remained visible in the revision history.

Himeno had been waiting outside. She did not enter. She asked Ishikawa only whether the Matron had requested her presence. He said no.

“Does Mio know she came?”

“She knows the meeting was offered. We did not give her its contents.”

“Will she be told what is in the books?”

“Only if the review reaches information that concerns her, and then through the consent process.”

Himeno nodded. She did not ask to see the pages.

At the end of the session, the archive specialist sealed the image files and generated checksums in front of the Matron and her lawyer. The account books returned to the cloth case. The lawyer signed the custody line; the clerk signed the image log.

The Matron stood, then put one hand on the chair back. “There is another book.”

Ishikawa did not reach for the case. “Where?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you mean you lost it?”

“I mean I remember a third book. I cannot tell you whether it was a separate volume, a duplicate, or the loose pages I just showed you.”

“Why mention it now?”

“Because you asked whether these were all of them.”

The recorder was still on. The statement entered the transcript as a memory, not a verified inventory. The Matron’s lawyer closed the cloth case and carried it out.

At the door, Ishikawa asked whether she would return for a second session.

“If I decide the first transcript says what I said.”

The evidence clerk looked at the clock. The interview had lasted seventy-three minutes. The books had been photographed, but no child’s fate had been established.

The missing third book existed in the record only as the Matron’s uncertain memory.

# Chapter 117: The Margin

---

The first discrepancy appeared in the margin, not the totals.

The archive specialist compared the photographed pages from the two account books. She did not combine them. One covered the east dormitory; the other’s cover was missing, and its room could not be established. The first book showed a first count of thirty-two on a Tuesday and a second count of thirty-one. A note beside the lower number read “office.” The initials were not clear enough to attribute.

The relocation schedule also listed thirty-one residents. The two numbers sat on separate screens, separated by a privacy barrier and a warning not to compare protected identifiers. They were not the same kind of record. One was a total of people present in a room at a time. The other listed people recorded as transferred. The matching number proved neither a link nor a destination.

Nia was not permitted to view the account-book images. She had been asked to prepare a public-record chronology while the archivist handled the restricted pages. When she received the public chronology, she noticed that it described the relocation schedule as “dated three days before the fire.”

“Which date?” she asked.

“The date on the schedule.”

“Is the fire date established?”

“The city report gives the night of the fire.”

“Does the schedule say the transfer happened on its date?”

“The title says ‘residents moved before the fire.’”

“That isn’t a date of movement.”

Ishikawa asked the archivist to check the wording. The schedule carried a date, but the line did not state whether it was the date of writing, approval, or transfer. The public summary was corrected to say it was “dated three days before the fire,” not “recording transfers three days before the fire.”

Nia put the correction in her notes, then crossed out a line she had written earlier: “Thirty-one moved.” The page kept both versions visible.

When Ishikawa reviewed the corrected chronology, he asked whether the schedule’s stated date could still be used to order the other papers. The archive specialist said yes, as the date written on that page, provided the caption did not convert it into a date of transfer. The distinction was added to the public timeline and to the source note, so later summaries would not quietly restore the stronger claim.

The archive specialist asked whether Nia had another question about the chronology. She pointed to the visitor log’s two-letter mark. “Does the office preserve handwriting samples from the staff who wrote these logs?”

“Some,” Ishikawa said. “Not everyone and not for every year.”

“Could a sample identify the writer?”

“It could support a comparison. It could not identify a writer by itself.”

“Would I be allowed to see the comparison?”

“If it becomes public.”

Nia accepted the limit. She did not ask to be given the sample under another label.

Wataru had been sorting the unsealed maintenance index. He found the work-order number the team requested, but its transfer slip showed that the box had gone to storage six years earlier. The box was marked “camera service, west entrance.” The two-letter gate entry was dated to a morning when the east camera had been out of service, not the west one.

“That may be an index error,” Mori said.

“Or two separate maintenance issues,” Wataru said. “We need the work order before we decide.”

The storage office found the box in a climate-controlled aisle. The Foundation’s counsel approved supervised review, but the box would take two days to retrieve. Ishikawa scheduled the review and asked the clerk not to tell the school what it contained before the inventory was checked.

Hoshina called about her article. The online headline had been changed to “Matron’s notebooks reveal uneven counts.” The body said the books showed at least one person missing at a second count. Hoshina had not written that sentence. Her editor had added it from an early draft of the police summary.

“I can correct it,” she said. “But I need to know what the notebook actually records.”

“People present in one room at two times,” Ishikawa replied. “Not missing persons.”

“Can I quote you?”

“You can quote the public statement after it is issued.”

“Can you issue it today?”

“When the Matron has reviewed her transcript and the archive approves the description.”

Hoshina said she would ask her editor to hold the update. She did not promise the headline would change before the print deadline.

The Matron’s lawyer returned a marked transcript the next morning. The Matron had corrected two names of rooms and changed “I kept the books” to “I retained the books.” She had not deleted a sentence. The evidence clerk compared every correction with the recording and logged the revisions.

One of the corrections mattered. Ishikawa had called the count book “the east dormitory register.” The Matron’s recorded words were “the east dormitory count book.” The transcript was amended. A count book was not a resident register.

The public statement followed that afternoon. It described a small number of recorded counts and said the books did not establish transfers or outcomes. Hoshina’s editor updated the online story, though the print edition still carried the earlier headline. Hoshina put the correction at the top of the web article.

At Aster, Ren read the statement on the board site. He wrote a note to the headmistress: “The correction should remain attached to every archived copy.” The headmistress asked whether he wanted a copy sent to the trustees.

“Yes.”

“Anything else?”

“No.”

She sent the correction with the first version attached. Ren did not ask what the Matron had said in her interview.

At the station, Ishikawa asked the archive specialist whether the thirty-two-to-thirty-one discrepancy could be explained by the note “office.”

“It could,” she said. “It could also refer to an item, a room, or something we don’t understand.”

“Can the Matron identify the handwriting?”

“She declined to guess from the photograph. She said she might recognize the hand in person, but only if she chose to return.”

Ishikawa did not ask her to come back immediately. He asked the specialist to preserve the question for the next session.

The first missing person in the account book might have been in an office. The page did not say which one.

The archive specialist cautioned that even “missing person” was too strong for the page. The count was lower by one; the margin note suggested an office, but no surviving key explained whether it meant a person, a task, or a room. Ishikawa amended the internal label to “one-count discrepancy.” Wataru asked whether that sounded evasive. “It sounds temporary,” Ishikawa said. “We can use a stronger term when the record earns it.”

# Chapter 118: The Unsent Letter

---

Kreutz’s letter reached Ishikawa without a signature.

It was typed on plain paper and delivered by hand to the station reception desk. The clerk logged the arrival time and asked the messenger to identify the sender. The messenger said he was a courier and left a receipt with the name of Kreutz’s clinic. The letter itself contained no name, greeting, or address.

It asked for three things: the Matron’s interview transcript, the account-book photographs, and the identity of the person who had written the east-door slip. It argued that Kreutz had run Lantern House and therefore had a right to see records concerning its routine.

Ishikawa brought the letter to the inquiry room. “He is asking for restricted material through an unsigned request.”

“He may have sent it,” Mori said.

“May have.”

The letter contained no identifying detail not already known publicly, except the phrase “east-door slip.” That phrase had not appeared in the public summary. Ishikawa checked the list of people who had received the restricted packet. Kreutz was not on it. The phrase could have come from someone who had seen the archive’s inventory, or from a guess based on the public description of the door.

Himeno asked whether they should call him.

“We can ask him to authenticate the request,” Ishikawa said. “We cannot ask as if we already know he wrote it.”

The clinic receptionist confirmed that a courier had been sent to the station at Kreutz’s direction. She could not say whether Kreutz had prepared the letter. When Ishikawa called the doctor, Kreutz said he had authorized delivery but had not read the page.

“Do you want to withdraw it?”

“No. I want to know what it says.”

Ishikawa read the requests aloud.

“I did not ask for the transcript,” Kreutz said. “I asked whether one could be prepared for me.”

“The letter asks for the transcript.”

“Then someone wrote it poorly.”

“Did you type it?”

“No.”

“Did you dictate it?”

“No.”

“Who prepared it?”

“I don’t know. The clinic secretary may have arranged the courier. I will ask.”

Ishikawa recorded the call and told him the documents were restricted. The archive could consider a request after the Matron reviewed the transcript, but Kreutz had no automatic right to it because he had once run the institution.

“Then ask her,” Kreutz said.

“She has not agreed to let us ask her on your behalf.”

A pause. “You are making her decision into a wall.”

“She made the condition. We are respecting it.”

“I understand.”

He did not ask to speak to the Matron. The call ended without an argument.

The clinic secretary later confirmed she had used a standard request template. Kreutz had given her three topics, not exact wording. She had written “east-door slip” after reading the public summary and an internal calendar entry that mentioned the inspection. She had not seen the account book or the Matron’s interview. Her explanation fit the route of the letter but did not establish who had authored each sentence.

Ishikawa added the courier receipt and the secretary’s statement to the file. The anonymous letter became a request with a documented delivery chain and an uncertain writer, not a threat or a leak.

Across town, Kreutz met Ren at the clinic’s old records room. He had asked the headmistress to arrange the meeting as a document handoff. Ren came with the school secretary, who carried the receipt for the blank form Kreutz had shown in Chapter 115.

Kreutz placed a sealed folder on the table. “These are the clinic’s retention schedules from the year Lantern House closed.”

The secretary checked the seal number against the receipt. It matched. Ren did not touch the folder.

“Why give it to me?” Kreutz asked.

“I am not receiving it. The school is.”

“You could have brought it yourself.”

“The secretary keeps the transfer log.”

Kreutz’s gaze moved to her. “And if the contents are harmless?”

“They will be recorded as received.”

“Will you open them?”

“Not without the archive specialist.”

Kreutz smiled slightly. “You insist on witnesses.”

“The folder is sealed.”

“Seals can be broken.”

“Then the record should show who broke it.”

The secretary signed the receipt. Kreutz signed as depositor. Ren signed as the person who arranged the transfer, not as custodian. The distinctions took longer than the handoff itself.

When the secretary had left the room to file the receipt, Kreutz spoke quietly. “You could have asked the Matron what she remembers.”

“She has agreed to speak to Ishikawa.”

“Not to you.”

“No.”

“Does that bother you?”

“I did not ask.”

Kreutz’s expression did not change. “You have made refusal very easy for everyone around you.”

“Is that what you think?”

“It is what I observe.”

Ren looked at the sealed folder. “Then you should distinguish what you observe from what you infer.”

Kreutz’s hand rested on the edge of the table. “You sound like Ishikawa.”

“I read his reports.”

“Do you want to know what is in the folder?”

“If it is relevant, the archive will open it.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

The secretary returned. She collected the receipt and the folder. Kreutz did not try to stop her. Ren thanked him for the deposit and left without asking what the schedules contained.

That night, the archive specialist opened the folder under two-person supervision. The retention schedule listed categories, storage periods, and disposal dates. It did not list individual forms. One category, “night observation sheets,” had been marked for destruction six years earlier. The initials beside the mark were illegible in the scan.

The clinic’s records officer said the schedule authorized destruction but did not prove it had occurred. There was no disposal certificate in the folder.

The archive asked whether a witness had certified the disposal. The records officer checked the retention policy and said the form normally required a second signature, but the surviving copy had only one. He could not tell whether the missing signature had been on another page or never entered. Ishikawa requested any annual inventory that might show the box count before and after the scheduled date.

The Matron’s count books were still in her lawyer’s custody. The clinic’s schedule showed that similar records might once have existed in Kreutz’s office. It did not show that they were the same books.

The archive specialist placed the empty section of the folder under a separate tab. Its label read “destruction certificates.” No certificate had been filed.

# Chapter 119: The Recommendation

---

Nia’s placement file had a recommendation without a source line.

The fellowship office sent the archive a routine renewal form. It listed Nia’s name, age, guardian’s consent, travel allowance, and the mentor who had supervised her first month. The line marked “referring institution” was blank. The archive clerk asked whether Nia wanted the form corrected before she signed it.

“I want to know who referred me,” Nia said.

The clerk checked the electronic record. The original application had arrived through the regional institute. The institute had listed Dr. Sagara as her mentor but not as the person who nominated her. A second field showed that the fellowship office had approved the placement before receiving the guardian’s signature.

“Is that allowed?” Nia asked.

“The initial offer can be conditional,” the clerk said. “The placement does not begin until the guardian and student sign.”

“Who sent the conditional offer?”

“It came through the institute’s liaison account.”

“Who used that account?”

“The institute administrator, according to the log.”

“Can I see the log?”

“It contains other students’ information.”

“Then give me the line that concerns my application.”

The clerk said she would ask the privacy officer. Nia folded the renewal form but did not sign it.

Sagara arrived for their scheduled review and found her waiting with the unsigned page. She asked him whether he had recommended her to the fellowship.

“I recommended your work,” he said. “I did not nominate you for this placement.”

“Who did?”

“I don’t know.”

“You brought me the cold-case packet.”

“The institute gave it to me as a practice file. I thought you would like the problem.”

“Who wrote it?”

“It was an old public case.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Sagara examined the packet’s cover. The source line named a records course, but no instructor. The three clocks were listed in the appendix. He had assumed the case packet had been compiled by the course office.

“I can ask,” he said.

“Please do. Don’t ask them to make it sound official. Ask who sent it.”

He agreed. Nia put the packet back in its sleeve. It was not evidence in Ishikawa’s inquiry. It was the exercise that had taught her to distrust a timeline whose clocks had not been compared.

The privacy officer approved a redacted extract from the fellowship log. It showed that Nia’s application had been forwarded by the institute administrator and that the conditional offer had been generated from a regional education fund. The fund’s public charter supported research training for students. The donor list was not part of the archive’s file.

“Can you tell whether the fund selected me?” Nia asked.

“No,” the officer said. “The document shows approval, not who made the decision.”

“Then write that.”

The officer amended the note. Nia signed the renewal form only after the referral field was changed to “source not identified in records reviewed.” She kept a copy beside the original with its blank line.

Ishikawa watched her put them away. “Does it concern you that you don’t know who referred you?”

“It concerns me that the form was about to say the institute did.”

“Do you think someone chose you?”

“I think someone approved an offer. I don’t know why.”

“Would you stop the placement?”

Nia considered the question. “Not because of a blank field. I’ll stop if I find out the work has conditions I didn’t agree to.”

The answer was entered in the placement review. Ishikawa did not ask her to stay. The inquiry needed her timeline, but she was not obligated to provide it.

Sagara’s call came two days later. The institute administrator had confirmed sending Nia’s application, but said the fellowship office had invited her to apply. The invitation had been addressed to Nia directly, with the cold-case exercise attached. The administrator could not identify who had prepared the packet or selected her name from the institute’s student list.

“Did they keep the email?” Nia asked.

“The administrator says it was forwarded from a shared mailbox. The original sender is hidden by the forwarding system.”

“Can we request the server log?”

“The institute says it deletes routing data after a year.”

“How long ago?”

“Eleven months.”

Nia sat still. “Then ask for the deletion policy and the date it was applied.”

Sagara did. The institute provided the policy and a system report showing that the shared mailbox had been migrated before the ordinary purge date. The original routing header was not present in the migrated copy. It was impossible to tell whether it had been deleted, not retained, or lost in transfer.

Nia read the report at the archive, outside the inquiry room. She did not put it into Ishikawa’s case file. It concerned her own placement, not the Lantern House evidence.

“Do you want to keep working?” Sagara asked.

“Yes.”

“Even if the invitation came from someone you can’t identify?”

“I’m here because I chose to accept it. If the conditions change, I can choose again.”

He nodded. He did not tell her that the choice proved she was safe.

On the table, the cold-case packet lay beside the fellowship form. Its three clock times were printed in a font used by the regional institute. The archive clerk checked the course catalog. The course had not existed in the year printed on the packet.

“That could be a reprint,” Sagara said.

“It could,” Nia replied. “Do we know who reprinted it?”

“No.”

She wrote the question on a separate page, then left it out of the case chronology.

When Ishikawa asked for her updated timeline, Nia gave him the version that concerned the photographs. The placement packet and donor question remained in her own folder.

“You’re keeping two files,” he said.

“One is mine.”

“Do you want help with it?”

“Not yet.”

Ishikawa accepted that answer. Nia clipped the cold-case packet behind the fellowship papers, not the Lantern House chronology.

The cover page was marked “public source.” The envelope it had arrived in was not.

Nia laid the fellowship form beside the packet and drew no line between them. One document described how she had been admitted to the archive; the other was the exercise that had first drawn her to the case. She had chosen to continue the placement, but she had not chosen the source that put her name on the invitation list. Until the routing record was recovered, those remained separate facts.

# Chapter 120: The Third Column

---

The third book was not a book.

The Matron returned to the station with a transparent sleeve containing four loose pages. She had found them folded inside the cloth case’s lining after the first interview. The pages had no cover, no consecutive numbers, and no visible dates. She had not included them in the first review because she had forgotten they were there.

Her lawyer placed the sleeve on the table and stated that the pages were offered for supervised imaging only. Ishikawa repeated the limitation. The archive specialist photographed both sides without removing the sheets. The Matron watched each image appear on the monitor.

“Do you recognize the handwriting?” Ishikawa asked.

“Some of the initials are mine.”

“Which ones?”

She pointed to two marks on the lower edge of separate pages. “Those.”

“Can you identify the other hands?”

“No.”

The pages were arranged in columns. The first listed a time. The second contained a number. The third showed a box beside the words “door open.” A fourth column had been cut from the right edge. No heading survived.

The top page carried a time of 8:40 and a total of thirty-one. Beneath it, a later entry showed thirty, followed by a checkmark. The next page repeated the time but not the same date format. At the bottom, in different ink, someone had written: “second count signed.”

Wataru stood behind the archive specialist. He did not approach the screen. “Could the thirty-one be the schedule?”

“It could be a number,” the Matron said. “I don’t know what it counts.”

“Could the thirty be people in the room?”

“It could.”

“Do you remember a night when one person went to the office?”

“No.”

“Does the checkmark mean the count was corrected?”

“I don’t know.”

The Matron’s answers were neither denial nor confirmation. Ishikawa asked the archivist to compare the paper fibers with the account books. The specialist said the pages could be from the same paper stock, but the samples were too small and the stock too common for a match.

Nia was allowed to observe the public-facing image after the privacy officer masked the cut column and any initials. The Matron had agreed to the limited viewing. Nia did not ask to see the unredacted page.

“Is the time a clock time?” Nia asked.

“Likely,” the archivist said. “Not certain.”

“Is it the same time format on all four pages?”

“No. Two have a colon. Two do not.”

“Could that mean different writers?”

“It could mean different writers, different habits, or a later copy.”

Nia wrote those alternatives into her notes. She did not use the number thirty-one in the timeline because the document’s category was missing.

Ishikawa asked whether the Matron remembered the cut edge. She said she did not. She had not cut it herself, to her knowledge. The lawyer asked that the statement be phrased as “does not recall cutting the page,” not “did not cut the page.” The clerk corrected the transcript.

The pages went into a new sleeve. Their order was recorded as received, not as original order. The Matron signed the seal and retained custody. The archive kept the verified images under restricted access.

After the meeting, Ishikawa asked Nia for her observation. She handed him a short note: “The public image shows a three-column form with a missing fourth column. It does not establish that the number 31 refers to people, transfers, or a complete count.”

“You could have suggested we compare the dates,” Mori said.

“The dates aren’t consistent enough to align yet.”

“We can normalize them.”

“You can. But if you normalize before deciding which calendar or shift the entries use, you might manufacture a sequence.”

Mori looked at the archive specialist. She said Nia was right: the pages lacked a year, and one time could be a shift label rather than an hour. The team would preserve the originals and seek an independent key before calculating a date sequence.

The Matron’s solicitor requested a copy of the transcript. Ishikawa sent the restricted version through counsel. The Matron returned one correction: the phrase “second count signed” was not a phrase she remembered using. She did not say who had written it.

The correction did not close the inquiry. It left the account books useful for one narrow purpose: showing that a number recorded at one check could differ at another, and that staff sometimes wrote a reason in the margin. It could not establish who had left a room, whether the reason had been verified, or where anyone went afterward.

Kreutz received no copy. He did not ask for one.

At the school, Ren received a notice that the archive had added four pages to the restricted record. The notice did not disclose their contents. He read it and placed it in the office file.

The headmistress asked whether he wanted to know what the Matron had supplied.

“If she wanted the school to know, she would have sent it to the school.”

“Do you think she is hiding something?”

“I don’t know.”

The headmistress studied him. “You never volunteer a theory.”

“I am not the investigator.”

She accepted the answer, though it did not reassure her.

That evening, Kreutz sent Ishikawa a second note. It was signed this time. He asked whether the Matron had provided the old count books and requested that any school records be preserved before the end of the six-week inquiry.

Ishikawa replied that the Matron had voluntarily provided limited pages for imaging. He did not describe the columns. He reminded Kreutz that the inquiry’s scope did not guarantee access to restricted evidence.

Kreutz’s reply arrived minutes later: “Then preserve the page with the missing column.”

The phrase was not public. It had not appeared in the response.

Ishikawa called the clinic. Kreutz said he had not seen the pages. He had inferred that a limited submission might include a damaged form. He had guessed there was a missing column because the Matron’s old books used ruled tables.

The inference was possible. It was also specific.

The call log established that Ishikawa had received the note before contacting the clinic. It did not establish how Kreutz had chosen those words.

At the end of the day, the evidence clerk filed the four pages under “unclassified count fragments.” The source was recorded as the Matron; the dates, subject, authorship, and missing column’s contents remained unknown.

A blank column had become a question only after someone named it.
