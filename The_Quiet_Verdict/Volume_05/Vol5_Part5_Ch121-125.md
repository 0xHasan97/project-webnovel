# Chapter 121: The Count That Did Not Travel

---

The Matron returned after correcting the transcript herself.

She brought no books this time. Her lawyer carried a copy of the restricted interview, its revision history, and a list of questions the Matron was willing to answer. The first page said she would not discuss individual children. The second said she would not be asked to authenticate any handwriting beyond her own initials. Ishikawa read both aloud before the recorder started.

“You asked to meet without Mio or Dr. Kreutz,” he said. “Would you tell us why?”

The Matron placed her hands flat on the table. “Mio has been asked to describe what she remembers too many times. I don’t want her to hear my account and change hers to fit it.”

“Has she changed it before?”

“I don’t know. That is why I don’t want to be another person in the room when she decides what she remembers.”

“Why exclude Kreutz?”

“He was in charge. If I say I don’t remember something, he may remember it for me.”

“Has he done that?”

The Matron looked at the recorder. “I am not accusing him. I am telling you why I asked for this arrangement.”

Ishikawa repeated the answer without the question’s implication. The evidence clerk noted that the Matron’s condition was about the interview setting, not a claim that either person had influenced her.

The archive specialist displayed the masked image of the first count book. The Matron confirmed that she recognized the room notation on the cover as “east dormitory.” She could not say when it had been written. She pointed to a pair of initials beside several entries and said they might be hers. She did not claim the remaining marks.

Ishikawa turned to the thirty-two-to-thirty-one line. “The note beside the lower total reads ‘office.’ What did that mean in your practice?”

“It meant the correction was to be checked with the office.”

“Not that the person was in the office?”

“Not necessarily.”

“Could it mean one person had gone there?”

“It could. It could mean the number was disputed. It could mean the next shift was supposed to confirm it. I would need the heading and the date to tell you which.”

The archivist zoomed the image. No heading survived above the note. The page edge had been clipped before the Matron received it, or before it entered the archive; the current examination could not establish when. The Matron did not recognize the cut.

“Did you ever use ‘office’ as a substitute for a name?” Mori asked.

“No.”

“Could another staff member have?”

“Yes.”

“Would you expect that to be written in the correction column?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then why is there no correction?”

“Because the column is partly missing. Or because no one wrote one. I cannot tell.”

The answer did not solve the discrepancy. It changed the safest description from “one person went to the office” to “one-count discrepancy; marginal note reads ‘office.’” Ishikawa asked the clerk to record that the Matron had not identified the mark as a location.

They moved to the folded slip that said to keep the east door unlatched until the count. The Matron read the line on the screen twice.

“Do you recognize the wording?”

“I recognize the instruction. I don’t recognize the hand.”

“What was the purpose of leaving the door open?”

“For staff moving between the dormitory and the service corridor.”

“Could it have been left open for a child to leave?”

“It could have been used that way. The instruction does not say that.”

“Was the door always left open?”

“No. The note says until the count. It doesn’t say what happened after.”

“Who signed the second count?”

“I can’t tell from the slip.”

The Matron’s statement made the wording more ordinary, but not harmless. A door left open for staff could also be crossed by someone else. She had not seen who used it on the date of the slip. She could not connect the instruction to the later paper in the alcove.

Himeno attended by video from a separate room. She asked whether the Matron wanted Mio’s statement excluded from the review.

“No,” the Matron said. “Mio’s words are hers. I am asking you not to put them beside mine as if one verifies the other.”

Himeno thanked her. “That is fair.”

The archive specialist asked if the count books had been kept in the same place as the folded slips. The Matron said they had been stored in a desk drawer at Lantern House, but she could not remember whether all the slips were there before the fire. She had found the cloth case later in a box of office material. She did not know who had packed the box.

“Did you open it when you found it?” Ishikawa asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you make an inventory?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I thought they were routine papers. I did not think anyone would call them evidence.”

The clerk recorded this as the Matron’s account of custody, not a verified chain before the first interview. The lawyer asked that “found in a box” not be changed to “recovered from the fire site.” The original box had not been brought, and its origin was not established.

At 11:16, the interview stopped. The clerk marked the time. The Matron reviewed the typed transcript before leaving. She corrected “the east door was kept open” to “a note instructed staff to keep an east door unlatched until a count.” She said the first sentence sounded like a universal practice. The second described only the words on the slip.

Ishikawa accepted the correction. The earlier version remained in the revision history.

Outside, the Matron’s lawyer asked whether she would permit the archive to publish a cropped image of the instruction. She said no. The image contained partial initials near the edge, and she did not want the public to turn them into names. The archive could quote the words and describe the limits.

When she left, the evidence clerk placed two copies of the transcript in separate envelopes: one for the restricted file and one for the Matron. A third copy, with personal references removed, was prepared for the archive. The chain of versions was logged.

Ishikawa looked at the line about the door. “We have a plausible routine.”

“We have a witness’s memory of a routine,” Himeno said through the screen. “Keep those apart.”

He nodded. The report would say the instruction was consistent with staff movement, not that it had been used only for staff movement.

The Matron had offered a reason for excluding Mio and Kreutz. She had not named a child, identified a hand, or explained the thirty-one. Her choice preserved the boundary she had asked for and left the count where the page left it: unfinished.

# Chapter 122: The Wrong Box

---

The storage box arrived with a label that named the wrong entrance.

Two clerks brought it into the records room on a trolley. The cardboard was sealed with the storage company’s numbered tape. The box number matched the transfer inventory; the description read “West entrance—security and electrical maintenance.” Ishikawa checked the seal against the retrieval receipt. The archive specialist photographed the box before it was opened.

The first folder held work orders for a west-facing camera. The second contained a purchase request for replacement cable. The third had a route sheet with both entrances on it. A carbon copy at the bottom was stamped “East boundary camera—intermittent loss.”

Mori leaned over the table. “So the box was mislabeled.”

“Or the route sheet was filed here for another reason,” said the archive specialist. “We should not change the box description until the whole inventory is complete.”

The work order was dated two days before the visitor-log entry with the two-letter mark. It recorded a call from Aster’s facilities desk: the east boundary camera had lost its picture during rain. The technician inspected the unit, found water at the exterior junction, and disconnected it pending a sealed conduit. The repair field was blank. A note in the margin read, “parts requested.”

The technician had signed with a surname and a work number. The second signature, in the authorization field, was a short mark that might have been “G2” or two initials. It was too faint to read from the carbon copy.

The visitor log showed a camera technician signing into Aster on the morning of the mark. The log did not show which camera he had inspected. It did not list the work-order number. The file tied a known outage to the east camera, but did not prove that the visitor-log entry recorded that same work.

The archive specialist kept the three facts in separate columns: the call, the work order, and the visitor entry. She did not draw an arrow between them.

The district chief asked if the outage changed the investigation.

“It explains why footage is missing,” Ishikawa said. “It does not tell us who used the gate.”

“Was the camera repaired?”

“The file doesn’t say.”

“Can we ask the technician?”

The name was clear enough to read. The technician had retired, and the company had been acquired. The chief authorized a voluntary request through the successor company. No one would contact the former worker until the archive confirmed the correct identity and the inquiry’s scope.

Wataru reviewed the box inventory under his outside-adviser terms. He could see only the unredacted work records that had been authorized for the task. He found an index slip stapled to the route sheet. It listed the east camera ticket under the west entrance because the job number had been opened against the west-wing maintenance account.

“That may be why the box label says west,” he said.

The archive specialist checked the accounting code. The east gate and west entrance shared a cost center. The slip explained the filing path, not the repair location. The box was no longer simply “wrong”; its label described the account that stored the work, while the work order described the device.

Mori pointed at the mark. “Could that be G2?”

“Could be,” Wataru said. “But we don’t know what G2 means.”

The technician’s work number ended in G-2. The photocopied visitor entry looked similar, but it was not a handwriting comparison. The mark could be a code, an initial, a time notation, or something else. The archive specialist added the work number to the list of possible comparison material but did not identify the mark.

The successor company located the technician’s personnel file, but it could not release it without his consent. It agreed to forward a request. The technician’s work order could support a contact attempt; it could not authorize the police to compel an interview.

Himeno asked whether the school had a record of the new conduit. The facilities manager searched the current maintenance database. A purchase order for weatherproof cable appeared six weeks after the work order. The invoice named the west-wing account. The installation field had been signed, but the work description said only “camera line, east sector.” There was no site map attached.

“Does that mean the east camera came back?” Himeno asked.

“It means cable was purchased for a camera line in the east sector,” Ishikawa said. “We need the installation record before we say the camera was restored.”

The facilities manager found a contractor name on the invoice. That company had closed. The school had no completion certificate in its current files. The Foundation’s counsel said the old office might have retained one, but the boxes had not all been indexed.

The work-order box changed the public statement. The archive added a note that an east-boundary camera had been reported intermittently failing and disconnected pending parts. It did not state the period of outage beyond the work order’s date or whether the camera later returned to service.

Hoshina asked if the two-letter mark might be the technician’s code. Ishikawa said it was one possible reading, not a finding. She asked whether she could report that the camera failed during rain.

“You can report the work order’s wording,” he said. “The record says intermittent loss during rain. It doesn’t say the storm caused the fault.”

Hoshina amended her draft. The headline became less dramatic and more accurate. Her editor moved the story down one column.

That afternoon, Ren received a request from the headmistress to identify any camera records he had seen in the incident logs. He returned a written statement: he had selected pages mentioning the boundary, gate, or service route; he had not examined the complete maintenance box; he did not recognize the mark from the copy he had delivered.

Ishikawa read it and attached it to the file. Ren had not supplied the work order, but he had limited his statement to what he had personally reviewed. That made it more useful than a guess.

The technician had signed a repair request. No surviving page proved the repair was completed. No page proved the gate had been used during the outage.

At the time of the work order, the camera had no picture. The gate remained unknown.

# Chapter 123: Across the Table

---

The table was too small for both folders, so the archive officer kept one closed.

Kreutz had requested a recorded meeting with Ren after the school published the corrected note about the count books. The trust’s lawyer approved an hour in the archive conference room, limited to documents already public or supplied by Kreutz. The Matron’s pages would not be brought in. Mio would not attend. Ishikawa sat at one end; Himeno and the headmistress sat along the wall. Wataru was allowed to observe, but not to ask questions on behalf of the inquiry.

Kreutz placed a copy of the clinic retention schedule on the table. Ren placed the school’s public correction beside it. Neither opened the other’s folder.

“I asked for this meeting because people have begun describing an ordinary count as a transfer record,” Kreutz said. “That is wrong.”

“Then why did you ask for the account-book photographs?” Himeno asked.

“To see whether I recognized the form.”

“You have not seen them.”

“No. The Matron refused access to me.”

“Did you expect her to agree?”

“I hoped she would.”

Ishikawa asked that they remain with the stated scope. Kreutz nodded. “I am not asking you to give me her pages. I am asking to put my own account on record.”

The archive officer turned on the recorder. Kreutz said he had overseen Lantern House but had not supervised every night count. The Matron managed the dormitory routine. He recalled instructions to keep a service door unlatched until a second count was complete, but he could not say whether the slip in the archive was one he had written.

Ren looked at the schedule copy. “You remember the instruction. Do you remember who signed the count?”

“No.”

“Do you remember whether the door stayed open after it was signed?”

“No.”

“Then your memory ends before the door closes.”

Kreutz turned toward him. “My memory ends where it ends.”

The recorder captured the pause. Ishikawa did not interrupt.

Kreutz said the east door had been used by staff moving between the dormitory and the service corridor. He could not rule out that a resident used it. He had not kept a complete log of who passed through. The retention schedule showed that a category of night observation sheets had been marked for destruction, but he did not know whether the sheets had been destroyed.

“Why not?” Ren asked.

“Because I did not sign the surviving form.”

“You ran the institution.”

“I did not run the records room alone.”

“Who did?”

Kreutz’s mouth tightened. “That is not on the page.”

“Do you remember?”

“I remember a records clerk. I do not remember the name.”

Wataru shifted in his chair but did not speak. The table gave him no role in the exchange except as witness.

Ishikawa asked Kreutz to distinguish what he remembered from what he had concluded. Kreutz agreed to dictate two separate paragraphs. In the first, he described the door instruction and the staff route. In the second, he stated that the account books might have been used to confirm that the dormitory was empty. He marked the latter as inference, not recollection.

Ren read the two paragraphs. “The instruction says ‘until the count,’ not ‘until the count was signed.’”

Kreutz looked at the public copy. “The other page says ‘second count signed.’”

Ren turned the public correction faceup. “That wording is from a restricted fragment. You said you had not seen it.”

“I said I had not seen the photographs.”

Ishikawa leaned forward. “Have you seen the fragment itself?”

“No.”

“Then where did you get that phrase?”

Kreutz looked at the recorder. “I remember a count being signed. I cannot tell you whether that is the same wording.”

The archive officer marked the phrase as Kreutz’s recollection, not a quotation from a document he had examined. Ishikawa asked how the wording had entered his memory. Kreutz said he could not separate it from the many instructions he had heard over the years. The answer was entered without endorsement.

“That page has not been matched to the door slip,” Ren said.

“Correct.”

“Then you are joining two instructions.”

Kreutz glanced at Ishikawa. “I am describing what I remember. I did not say the sheets were the same.”

The archive officer added the distinction. One slip contained an east-door instruction. Another fragment contained the phrase “second count signed.” Their physical relationship had not been established. The meeting had nearly merged them through a shared recollection, and the transcript now preserved the separation. Kreutz’s familiarity with the phrase was logged as a question about his source, not proof he had seen the restricted page.

Kreutz closed his schedule copy. “You are very careful about what the paper cannot say.”

Ren’s expression did not change. “The paper will be quoted after we leave.”

“And what will they quote?”

“Whatever fits the headline.”

“Then perhaps we should give them something that does not fit.”

Ren did not answer. Kreutz asked whether Ren remembered the night counts from Lantern House. Ren said he remembered adults using the phrase “second count,” but not the records or the reason for it. Ishikawa asked whether he wished that answer included in the transcript. Ren said yes, with the date of the meeting and the fact that he was answering from memory.

Kreutz watched him sign the page. “You can make uncertainty look like a choice.”

“It is a choice to mark it.”

“Not to be uncertain.”

“No.”

The recorder continued. Kreutz asked whether Ren wanted the old records found. Ren answered that the inquiry had requested them. Kreutz asked if that was the same as wanting them. Ren said no.

Himeno looked up from her notes. “Why did you ask him that?”

“Because the distinction matters,” Kreutz said.

“Then ask the question you mean.”

Kreutz turned back to Ren. “Do you want the records found?”

“I want them preserved.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“It is the answer I can give.”

The hour ended with two signed statements and no shared conclusion. Kreutz’s memory of the door routine was logged separately from his inference about the count books. Ren’s memory was limited to hearing the phrase. Neither authenticated the recovered pages.

Before leaving, Kreutz asked to keep the public correction. The archive officer made a copy and logged the transfer. Ren kept his own copy. The two men left by different doors.

On the table, the closed folder remained unopened. It contained no secret document. It was the Matron’s restricted file, which the meeting had not been allowed to touch.

The recorded conversation had put a boundary between what Kreutz remembered and what he wanted Ren to remember.

# Chapter 124: The Exercise File

---

The case packet had a public-source stamp and no public source.

Nia brought it to Dr. Sagara’s office, not the archive. The inquiry had no jurisdiction over her fellowship application or the exercise file, and she had asked Ishikawa not to place it in the Lantern House record. Sagara had requested the institute’s catalog, the course schedule, and any version history associated with the packet.

The course listed on the cover had not existed in the year printed there. The institute had later created a seminar with the same title, but its first syllabus was dated nine months after Nia received the packet. The stamp could have been applied during a later workshop. It did not establish who wrote the pages.

Sagara placed the documents side by side. “I thought the course office prepared this.”

“Did you ask them?”

“I did today.”

“What did they say?”

“They don’t recognize the cover. The packet was not in their archive.”

“Who gave it to you?”

“A colleague forwarded it. He said it was a useful exercise.”

“Did he name the sender?”

“No.”

Nia wrote down the chain: unknown sender to colleague; colleague to mentor; mentor to her. The packet itself contained a case number and a list of clock times. No jurisdiction, docket stamp, court name, or release date appeared. The phrase “public source” was a printed label, not evidence of publication.

Sagara asked if she wanted to stop using it.

“I already stopped using it as a real case.”

“What is it now?”

“An unattributed exercise with a plausible case number.”

He accepted the correction. She had found the clock reset in its timeline, but that conclusion applied only to the packet’s internal sequence. It did not solve a real disappearance unless the source could be established.

The institute administrator sent a scan of the shared mailbox’s remaining metadata. The original message was unavailable, but the forwarding record included an attachment name: `Station_Clock_Exercise_FINAL2.pdf`. The file’s creation date was two years after the case number on its cover. The author field was blank. The PDF had been exported by a software version released in the same year as the fellowship’s conditional offer.

“Could the file have been rescanned?” Sagara asked.

“Yes.”

“Could the dates have changed in conversion?”

“Yes.”

“Does the metadata prove it was created then?”

“No. It proves this copy carries that metadata.”

Nia recorded the limitation. It was still a fact worth keeping: the file she received was not a verified contemporary copy of the case record.

The administrator could not recover the original routing header. The shared mailbox had migrated, and the system’s old audit logs had expired under the retention policy. No one in the institute remembered selecting Nia specifically. She had been one of twelve students who received the packet.

“Was I the only student offered the fellowship?” she asked.

“No. Three other students received conditional offers,” Sagara said. “Their applications were not accepted.”

“Do we know who chose them?”

“No.”

Nia folded the fellowship form and put it away. The unknown referral was no longer unique to her, but it remained unaccounted for. She had been selected from a group, not singled out in a document anyone could produce.

At the archive, she told Ishikawa she would pause her personal inquiry while she completed the placement’s public timeline. She asked that the fellowship paperwork remain outside the police file.

“Are you concerned the referral is connected to this case?” he asked.

“I don’t know. I don’t have a reason to connect it.”

“Then why keep investigating?”

“Because I want to know who sent the packet. Not because I think it was about Lantern House.”

Ishikawa wrote down the distinction and asked if she wanted a copy. She said yes. The statement recorded her purpose, not a theory.

The institute offered to contact the colleague who forwarded the file. Sagara agreed to make the request, but Nia asked that he not tell the colleague she suspected anyone. “Ask what he received and when,” she said. “If he remembers, let him say it first.”

That afternoon, the colleague replied that he had received the packet in a paper envelope after a regional workshop. He had not kept the envelope. He thought the sender had been a retired investigator, but could not remember the name. He forwarded the PDF to Sagara because the clock issue resembled a training exercise he had once used.

The workshop program listed five retired investigators. None had written a paper under the packet’s title. One had been Dr. Sagara’s mentor years earlier; he had died before the fellowship began. The connection was biographical, not proof of authorship.

Nia read the program and closed the file. She did not ask the archive to look up the deceased mentor’s records. She had no authorization and no evidence that the packet came from him.

Sagara asked if she was disappointed.

“I’m less certain than I was.”

“Is that disappointing?”

“It is a result.”

She kept the packet. The cover still said “public source,” though no public source had been located. She did not cross it out. She added a sticky note: “Provenance unverified; do not cite as a real case.”

On her way out, she passed the inquiry room. Through the glass, she saw the public timeline for the east camera displayed beside the visitor log. She did not stop to read it. The work was outside her scope, and she had told Ishikawa she would not ask for a broader one.

The packet’s unknown sender had not been found. The part Nia could control was what she would claim about it.

# Chapter 125: The Gate Code

---

The mark in the visitor log was not a name, but no one could prove what it was instead.

The archive specialist began with the maintenance records. The work order’s author used “G-2” to identify the second east-sector camera. A separate inventory listed the gate relay as “G2.” On the visitor-log copy, the mark appeared to begin with a curve and end in a short stroke. It could be read as “G2,” “G-2,” or two joined initials.

Wataru asked whether the shared code was enough to identify it.

“No,” the specialist said. “The same characters appear in several places. They are not a signature.”

The maintenance box contained a key-issue slip dated the same morning as the visitor entry. It logged a service key as “G2—temporary.” The receiving field was blank. The return field had a tick but no time. The slip had been filed with camera documents because the same account paid for both the lock and the camera cable.

The visitor log’s mark might be a key code. It might be an employee’s initials. The available copy did not show whether the dash had been lost in photocopying. No original key ledger survived in the box.

Ishikawa asked the school to search its current key inventory. The facilities manager found a cabinet list from the year after the incident. It included “G2—east gate service,” marked retired. There was no issue history. The list confirmed that G2 had been a key label; it did not show who held it on the morning in question.

The first lead had changed shape. The team had once treated the mark as a possible writer’s initials. It was now equally plausible that it was a key code copied into the wrong column. Neither reading could be selected from the scan alone.

Nia was invited to review only the public version of the updated chronology. The archive specialist had removed the key-slip image and personal information. Nia read the summary and pointed to a phrase: “The visitor-log mark corresponds to the G2 key.”

“It doesn’t correspond,” she said. “It resembles the same label.”

The specialist checked the draft. Nia was right. The word “corresponds” implied a verified match. It was changed to “may use the same code.”

“Would you put that in the public summary?” Ishikawa asked.

“Yes, if you include that the original key issue record is missing.”

He did. The correction went out with the date and version number. The earlier summary remained available beside it.

The district chief reviewed the six-week inquiry’s remaining time. The archive had requested the contractor’s completion certificate, the original visitor log, and the missing key register. The Foundation had not located them. The successor camera company had not received consent from the retired technician. The chief asked whether the inquiry had enough to request a broader search.

“Enough to request, not enough to compel,” Ishikawa said.

The trust’s counsel offered a second voluntary inspection of the lower corridor if the team gave a fixed list of questions. The offer excluded the chained door and any surface beyond it. The inspection would not become a search. Ishikawa accepted the offer and submitted three questions: whether the door’s hardware matched the stored maintenance inventory, whether the threshold carried a surviving key-code plate, and whether the corridor’s camera coverage could be mapped from the existing conduits.

The trust approved the scope. Nia’s name was not included on the entry list. She did not ask to be added. Wataru would attend as an observer to the record process, not as an investigator. Hoshina could wait outside the boundary and report the public findings.

Kreutz asked to attend. The trust denied the request because the inspection was limited to the police and property representatives. He did not challenge the decision. Instead, he sent a signed statement confirming that G2 had been used for a service key at Lantern House during his tenure. He could not identify a key-holder or the date it was used.

The statement was placed beside the inventory, not inside it. It supported the existence of a code. It did not establish that the visitor-log mark referred to that code.

Before the inspection, Ren met Kreutz once more in the archive conference room. The headmistress and Ishikawa were present. A copy of the G2 entry sat between them.

“You have said it was a service key,” Ren said.

“I remember the label.”

“Do you remember who held it?”

“No.”

“Do you remember if it opened the east gate?”

“I remember it was assigned to that gate.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No.”

Kreutz rested a finger near the code, without touching the page. “You want the person who used it.”

“I want the record to say whether it knows.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then it doesn’t.”

Kreutz looked at Ishikawa. “He has learned to make a question sound like an answer.”

“Noted,” Ishikawa said.

The meeting ended before either man moved the paper. The archive officer logged the page’s position and returned it to the file.

At the gate, the inspection team waited for the trust’s representative to unlock the service entrance. The key was new and carried a printed label: EAST ACCESS—CURRENT. The old G2 inventory remained in a sealed folder inside Ishikawa’s case bag.

The second survey had not begun. The property representative checked the authorization, then asked who would record each person crossing the boundary. The team assigned one clerk to the roster and another to the camera. Every movement would be logged, but no one called the procedure a guarantee.

The old code could have opened the gate. The new key could show them where the gate led.

Neither could tell them who had carried the first one.
