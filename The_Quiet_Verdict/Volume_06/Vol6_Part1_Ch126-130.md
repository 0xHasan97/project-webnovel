# Chapter 126: The Narrow Inspection

---

The second inspection began with a boundary line and ended at a sealed junction.

Ishikawa read the authorization aloud at the east gate. The team could compare visible hardware with the stored maintenance inventory, look for a surviving key-code plate at the threshold, and map the visible camera conduits. The chained door would remain closed. No one could cross it, remove a fitting, or open a wall. The trust’s representative confirmed each limit before the new key turned.

Ren was not on the entry list. Nia was not on it either. Wataru attended under the same outside-adviser restrictions as before, carrying a notebook that belonged to him and a copy of the permission. Hoshina waited on the public side of the marked boundary. She could report what the team released, not what she guessed they had seen.

The engineer photographed the hinge and latch from both sides. The model number matched a common commercial lock used in municipal buildings. It did not match a unique purchase record. The trust’s representative said the lock might have been replaced during a later repair; the records did not establish when. Ishikawa asked that the statement be attributed to the representative, not added as a finding.

At the threshold, the conservator found two empty screw holes beneath a rectangle of newer paint. No plaque remained. The holes were too shallow to show the size of the plate, and the paint could have covered an older sign or a routine repair. There was no surviving G2 mark.

The team photographed the area with a scale and marked the image number in the inspection log. Nobody scraped the paint or tried to fit a key. The absence of a plate could not establish that one had ever carried the G2 code.

The trust’s representative asked for a chance to inspect the photographs before they were released, citing the surviving privacy terms. Ishikawa agreed to let counsel review them for personal information, not to alter the record or suppress an unwelcome result. The clerk made a checksum copy before sending the preview. The trust returned it with one request: crop a modern alarm label visible at the edge of a frame. The original image remained untouched; a separately labeled public crop was made, and the request was logged.

The surveyor traced the exposed conduit along the ceiling. A newer cable ran toward the outer stair, then disappeared above a plastered section. An older metal conduit ended at a capped junction near the lower landing. The cap was intact. The cable could not be followed behind the wall without opening it, which the order did not allow.

“Can we say there was no camera in the corridor?” Mori asked.

“No,” the surveyor replied. “We can say this visible conduit does not establish camera coverage. It could have served a light, an alarm, or another line.”

The archivist added that no camera lens or labeled cable was visible in the permitted area. That was the full observation. No one could infer that the corridor had never been recorded, or that a camera had not been installed elsewhere.

Wataru asked the engineer whether the new cable could have been part of the east-boundary camera repair. The engineer compared it to the work order’s brief description. The invoice did not include a route plan, and the cable could not be traced to the camera from this point. “Possible,” he said. “Not demonstrated.”

Ishikawa asked whether the team had answered any of the three questions. The lock was common, the code plate was absent, and the visible conduit did not establish a camera path. Each answer was recorded as limited by the authorization and the surviving surface.

Hoshina asked for the exact language before publishing. Ishikawa dictated a sentence: “A visual inspection found no surviving key-code plate and no identifiable camera connection in the authorized corridor; the inspection did not open the wall or chained door.” She read it back and kept the limits intact.

The trust’s representative signed the inspection log. The evidence clerk sealed the images and recorded the time, file hashes, and each person’s departure. The door remained closed. The new key went back into its envelope, which the representative signed across the seal.

At the station, the district chief asked whether the visit had advanced the case.

“It removed two easy claims,” Ishikawa said. “We can’t say the lock proves G2. We can’t say the conduit proves a camera.”

“That is not much.”

The archive specialist added that the capped junction’s condition could establish only that it had not been opened during this inspection. The cap’s age and purpose were not established. The team agreed not to describe it as a disconnected camera line in the public release. The release would include the conduit map with the uncertain segment marked in gray.

“It is more than a wrong answer.”

The records office sent a message while they were closing the inspection file. A retired facilities clerk had responded to the voluntary interview request. His name was Hara Tadao. His personnel card placed him at Aster during the period when the east gate was in use, though his job title had changed twice. He would meet the team in the public archive conference room the following morning.

The district chief asked whether Hara had been a key-holder.

“The card says facilities clerk,” Ishikawa replied. “The rest is what we need to ask.”

The invitation contained no promise that Hara would remember the gate. It did contain a time, a location, and a request that he bring any records he had kept.

The corridor had yielded no code. The man who might explain the code had agreed to speak.

# Chapter 127: The Key That Had No Name

---

Hara Tadao placed his hands on the table before anyone asked him to.

The archive conference room had a fixed recorder, a wall clock, and a second clock on the laptop used to log the call. The clerk wrote both times in the session record. Hara signed the visitor register, checked the spelling of his name, and accepted a glass of water. Ren attended as Aster’s student records witness because he had previously selected the incident-log pages that led to the maintenance search. The headmistress sat beside him. Ishikawa and the archive specialist sat opposite Hara.

The interview began with scope. Hara could leave at any time. The team wanted to ask about the facilities records and the G2 notation, not his private life or the school’s former residents. He agreed and asked that the recorder remain on during breaks if no one was questioning him. Ishikawa said the room’s fixed security camera ran continuously, but the image would be preserved only as part of the access record.

Hara had worked in facilities for eleven years, first as an assistant and later as a clerk. He said his job was to receive work orders, record key movements when someone reported them, and send repair requests to the contractor. He was not a guard and had not been assigned to the east gate full-time.

“Did you issue a G2 key?” Ishikawa asked.

Hara considered the wording. “I remember a G2 mark. I’m not sure it was on the key.”

“Where might it have been?”

“On the cabinet slot. Or on the tag that went on the key ring. We used both kinds of label.”

The archivist asked him not to choose between the possibilities until he had seen the document. The visitor-log copy was displayed, with unrelated names masked. Hara leaned toward it but did not touch the screen.

“That looks like the way I wrote a two,” he said. “It could be G2. It could be two initials. I can’t authenticate it from this copy.”

“Was G2 the east gate?” Mori asked.

“Maybe. I remember an east key in the cabinet. I don’t remember its code. If the inventory says G2 was the east gate, that tells you what the later inventory called it. It doesn’t tell you what the mark meant on the day in that log.”

Ishikawa asked whether Hara had kept copies of the issue slips. He said he had sometimes taken carbon copies home when a repair request remained open, but he had not checked the boxes since retirement. They might be in a desk drawer at his daughter’s house, or might have been discarded when she moved him. He did not know.

“Would you be willing to ask her?”

“Yes. I’ll call after this. If the papers are there, I can bring them in.”

The answer was entered as an offer to search, not confirmation that any record survived.

Ren had not spoken. When Ishikawa asked if he had a question about the selected log pages, he looked at the public copy and asked whether the date was printed on the original or added to the transcript. The clerk checked the copy’s image number. The date appeared in the log’s own column; the scan did not show whether the ink was contemporaneous.

Hara said the forms were filled in at the time, but he could not identify the handwriting on this entry. He also could not verify the date from memory. His general description did not authenticate the specific log.

The archivist asked whether the east gate’s key had a duplicate. Hara said facilities kept a spare in a locked cabinet, but he could not remember which supervisor had the cabinet key. A sign-out might have been recorded on a separate sheet. The surviving inventory did not include one.

“Could you identify the supervisor?” Ishikawa asked.

“Not without the roster. There were changes.”

The clerk read back the statement. Hara corrected “I issued G2” to “I remember a G2 mark but cannot say whether it identified the key or its cabinet slot.” He initialed the change.

At the end of the interview, he agreed to contact his daughter and report whether the old papers existed. He did not promise a date. The archive staff gave him a copy of his statement and a contact number for the records office.

Hara stood slowly, folded the copy once, and put it into his coat pocket. Ren held the conference-room door while he passed. Hara thanked him. Neither man spoke about the gate.

In the corridor, a staff member asked Hara if he needed help arranging a taxi. He said he would take the bus. The lobby camera showed him leaving the building at 10:39. The visitor register contained no destination, only his signature and exit time.

The team’s minutes recorded three limits: Hara could not identify the mark, could not name the person who held the spare, and had not yet searched for his old papers. His offer was useful because it gave the archive permission to ask again.

Before Ishikawa left, he checked that the interview file included both clock sources and the corrected sentence. The clerk sealed the copy. Ren signed the witness register and returned to school with the headmistress.

The facilities manager agreed to check the retired staff directory for supervisors who had held the cabinet key. The archive would not treat a name in that directory as proof of key custody; it would only give Hara a list to test against his memory. The records office sent him a request for consent to compare the names, not a demand for an identification.

Hara called the next afternoon. He had not found the carbon slips yet, but he had remembered that the spare cabinet was moved during the west-wing renovation. The relocation sheet might identify who signed for the cabinet, though he could not recall its label. He agreed to review the sheet if the school found it and to correct any transcript line that overstated his memory.

Ishikawa asked whether Hara’s call changed the interview record.

“No,” the clerk said. “It becomes a separate contact note.”

They logged the date, the call duration, and the exact scope of what Hara had said. The cabinet’s move was not added to the public chronology until the relocation sheet could be located. Hara’s final answer had not been a name. It had been a promise to look.

# Chapter 128: The Interval

---

The recorder had not been stopped when Hara fell.

The archive’s fixed camera faced the conference-room entrance and the table’s near side. It did not record sound. The meeting recorder captured speech but not the lobby. A third camera covered the corridor outside the reading room. When the staff later assembled the sequence, they used the time stamps from all three systems and noted that their clocks were not synchronized to the second.

The interview ended at 10:34 on the conference-room clock. The clerk stopped the question recorder but left the room recorder running under the consent Hara had given. During the break, the headmistress took a phone call in the lobby. Ishikawa and Mori went to the records desk. Ren sat alone in the reading room with the public copy of the visitor log.

The corridor camera showed him take a small black notebook from his bag. He opened it near the back, wrote for several seconds, and closed it. The camera did not show the page. He put the notebook away and returned to the conference room.

The archive later compared the system clocks against the building’s daily time check. They could establish an interval, not a perfect second-by-second sequence. Ren’s writing ended between 10:40:12 and 10:40:19 by the calibrated range. The room camera and recorder placed Hara’s sudden cardiac arrest between 10:40:43 and 10:40:51. The two ranges leave between twenty-four and thirty-nine seconds between the end of Ren’s writing and the medical event; neither system supports a more exact interval. Hara died during the responders’ efforts, at a time the records place no later than 10:46:39. The cause remained subject to the medical examiner’s review.

At the table, Hara had been discussing whether he would call his daughter. The audio caught him saying, “I’ll ask her tonight.” A chair moved. The recording then captured the headmistress calling his name and Ishikawa asking someone to call emergency services.

The camera showed Hara’s hands leaving the table edge. He fell out of frame. The people in the room moved at once. Ren stood, then stepped back when Ishikawa called for space. The headmistress knelt beside Hara. The archive staff unlocked the outer door for the responders.

Hara had no visible injury. The first responder’s report recorded a sudden cardiac arrest and immediate treatment. Responders attempted resuscitation, but did not restore a pulse. A physician confirmed death after the effort ended; the written record rounded the time to the nearest minute. The initial report did not establish an underlying cause. The coroner’s examination would take longer.

Ishikawa asked the archive to preserve the original recordings, not just the clipped sequence. The clerk generated hashes and stored the files read-only. Each clock offset was documented. No one rounded the interval into a precise number.

Hoshina heard that Hara had died from a source at the archive. She called Ishikawa before publishing. He confirmed only that an interview witness had suffered a medical emergency and died at hospital. He would not confirm the preliminary cardiac description before family notification. Hoshina’s editor wanted the G2 connection in the headline. She refused to call it a key-holder’s death; Hara had not been one.

The headmistress gave a written account of the interview and the emergency. She noted that Ren had been in the reading room during the break. Ren provided a short statement: he had reviewed the public log copy and made notes. He did not claim to know Hara’s medical history or the cause of the collapse.

The archive specialist checked the footage. It could show a notebook, a page turn, and a person writing. It could not show what the page said. The interview’s name placard established that Hara Tadao had been present before Ren entered the reading room, but the camera did not capture the notebook’s contents or any connection between the two events.

When Ishikawa asked Ren whether he would provide his notes, Ren said the notebook contained other school records and he would not hand over unrelated private material without a proper request. He offered to provide the page concerning the interview through the headmistress. The distinction was entered in the record. The police did not seize the notebook.

The archive staff notified Hara’s daughter. She asked for the original interview recording and a copy of her father’s signed statement. Both were provided through the records office. She did not yet consent to a search of his home papers.

The district chief reviewed the sequence without the sound muted. “A man makes notes; a man collapses. That is the record.”

“And the timing?” Mori asked.

“An interval reconstructed from clocks with known offsets. It may matter. It does not explain itself.”

The inquiry added a medical-event file, separate from the gate records. Hara’s testimony remained valid as testimony about what he remembered, not as an authenticated key register. The offer to search his daughter’s boxes remained unresolved.

The black notebook had appeared on the corridor camera. Its page had not. A preservation copy of the relevant corridor sequence was sealed separately from the interview audio, so later review could not replace the original with a convenient clip. The clerk recorded the camera’s retention window and requested the preceding hour as well as the interval around Hara’s collapse. The request did not imply a suspicion; it protected context before the system overwrote it.

When the full hour arrived, the corridor showed staff entering and leaving the records desk, Ren’s brief note-taking interval, and the team returning to the conference room. No one approached him while he wrote. The image did not show the notebook’s page or the writing instrument clearly enough to identify it. The cameras established opportunity for ordinary note-taking just as firmly as they established the timing that made the notes worth preserving.

The chief directed Ishikawa to keep the event file independent from the G2 search unless a record connected them. The medical examiner had not made that connection. The camera had not made it. The chronology could retain both facts without joining them.

# Chapter 129: The Papers at Home

---

Hara’s daughter came to the archive with one document and no box.

She had received her father’s statement and listened to the recording with the records officer present. She did not want the meeting copied for the press. She agreed that the inquiry could use the signed transcript, with personal details masked, and asked the archive not to publish the emergency footage. The police accepted both conditions.

“I’m not here to give you his things,” she said. “I’m here to tell you what he told me.”

Ishikawa asked whether Hara had mentioned the old key slips before the interview. She said he had called the previous evening to ask if she still had a small metal filing box from his last move. He described it as containing “school maintenance papers,” but he had not asked her to bring it. She had not known it might relate to the east gate.

“Do you know where it is now?”

“In storage. I can look, but I need to decide what I’m willing to open.”

The records officer explained that no one could compel her to produce the box under the current order. If she chose to search, the archive could receive copies or arrange supervised review. She asked for a written list of the specific terms the team was seeking. Ishikawa agreed to send one.

The one document she brought was a page from Hara’s personal calendar, written in his hand. The entry for the previous night read: “Ask M. about old Aster papers. G2—slot or tag?” The date was printed by the calendar. The handwriting was not independently authenticated, though his daughter recognized it as his.

Hara had been uncertain before the interview, not after it. The note did not tell them which interpretation was correct. It did establish that he had intended to check. The team also documented a possible alternative: a familiar code might have triggered his memory without identifying the mark in the visitor log. His daughter said he had worked with dozens of cabinet labels and tags. The archive therefore treated the calendar entry as evidence of his unresolved question, not evidence that he had found the answer.

Mori asked if the inquiry could use it. Hara’s daughter said yes, with the source and her identification of the handwriting clearly stated. The clerk scanned the page, returned the original, and logged the permission.

The district chief asked Ishikawa if this changed his view of the collapse.

“It changes nothing about the cause,” Ishikawa said. “It changes what we know Hara was trying to verify.”

The coroner’s office sent a preliminary notice that afternoon. No external injury had been found. The cause of death remained undetermined pending examination of tissue and medical history. The notice did not say that the event was natural, and it did not suggest foul play. The inquiry filed it outside the maintenance records. Because the time came from a rounded field and the camera clocks had documented offsets, the clerk refused to describe Hara’s death as occurring at a precise number of seconds after Ren’s note-taking.

Hara’s daughter called again after receiving the search list. She had located the metal box but had not opened it. She wanted a family member present and an inventory made before anyone copied anything. The archive specialist offered a supervised appointment. It was scheduled for the following week, subject to her confirmation.

At the school, Ren submitted a single page of notes through the headmistress. It contained the words “G2—meaning not confirmed,” the date, and the time of the interview. The page was signed and sealed. The police compared it to the public transcript and found no material difference. No one asked for the rest of the notebook.

The headmistress said Ren had followed the procedure she gave him. Ishikawa agreed. “We have his notes from the interview, not a complete account of what he wrote during the break.” The distinction went into the case chronology.

Nia asked whether she could update the public-facing timeline. Ishikawa said she could add the existence of Hara’s statement and the fact that his recollection did not identify the code. She could not include the personal calendar page until Hara’s daughter approved a public summary. Nia accepted the boundary and kept her draft in the supervised folder.

In her correction, she changed “former key custodian” to “former facilities clerk.” The earlier phrase had appeared in a working draft, based on assumption. She saved the old version beside the revised one, with the change logged.

Hoshina published a brief notice that an interview witness had died after a medical emergency. She did not state a cause. She quoted the records office as saying his testimony did not identify a key-holder or authenticate the visitor-log mark. The article drew fewer readers than the first headline her editor had proposed.

Hara’s daughter left with the calendar page. The metal box remained unopened in storage. The inquiry had a possible record, a scheduled review, and no key number it could confidently assign to the visitor-log mark.

Ishikawa’s office sent Hara’s daughter a written list of the categories sought: old key issue slips, cabinet relocation papers, and any maintenance index bearing the east-gate or G2 label. It explicitly excluded unrelated family papers. She replied that she would consider supervised review but wanted the box inventoried by a family member first. The archive accepted the condition and moved the appointment without making a claim about what the box contained.

At the end of the day, the archive received a second request: the catalog entry for Lantern House’s resident intake register had been flagged for access review. Nia’s initials appeared in the request field. The page count on the index did not match the count on its old microfilm reference.

# Chapter 130: The Index That Counted Twice

---

The catalog’s two numbers referred to two different things.

Nia had not opened a restricted file. She had asked the archive’s public catalog to identify records that might explain the route by which names were entered into Lantern House’s registers. The system returned a summary: “Resident intake register, one volume; access restricted pending privacy review.” A linked microfilm reference listed sixty-four frames. A later transfer index listed sixty-three images and one item marked “withheld at source.”

She flagged the mismatch for the archivist, then stopped. Her placement rules did not permit her to search the restricted volume or infer what had been withheld. She sent the question through Sagara, who submitted it to the archive’s access officer.

The response arrived the next morning. The two counts were not necessarily inconsistent. One could refer to the original frames and one to images supplied for review; the withheld item might be a cover, blank leaf, or protected page. The catalog did not say. The access officer would compare the transfer receipt and the current inventory before deciding whether any supervised review could be authorized.

Nia added the discrepancy to her timeline as “catalog counts differ; reason unknown.” She did not write “missing page.”

Ishikawa reviewed the line and approved it. “What made you ask for this register?” he said.

“The route between a name being written and a placement entry being made. If the records are separate, we should know that before comparing totals.”

“Does the catalog connect the register to the seventy-one entries?”

“No. It only says resident intake.”

“Then keep it separate until we know.”

She did. The seventy-one placement entries, the thirty-one-name relocation schedule, and the room-level counts remained in separate sections of the working chronology. The register’s existence did not prove it was the source of any of them.

The access officer found a transfer receipt in the county archive. It described one bound volume and a sealed envelope of “loose indexes.” The receipt did not list individual pages. A microfilm technician’s note from a later year said one frame was unreadable and one frame was withheld for privacy. The sixty-four and sixty-three totals could be reconciled if the unreadable frame had been counted differently, but the withheld item remained unexplained.

The archive offered Ishikawa a supervised inspection of the volume’s binding and the envelope’s exterior. No names could be viewed without a separate privacy determination. Nia could observe the public handling procedure if her guardian and supervisor approved. Sagara checked the authorization and agreed, on the condition that she not handle the material or photograph the room.

Hara’s daughter confirmed the appointment to inventory the metal box, but asked to move it by a week. The archive accepted. The delay did not affect the register review. The two records had different custodians and different access rules.

Before the inspection, Nia prepared a list of questions. Was the bound volume intact? Did the envelope contain an index, a cover, or a page? Could the old microfilm note be matched to a physical item? Which parts of the register, if any, were created after the institution closed? She gave the list to the access officer. She did not include a question about Ren.

The volume arrived in a gray conservation tray. The seal number matched the transfer receipt. The access officer photographed it closed, recorded the binding and the spine, and compared the envelope’s exterior against the old inventory. The envelope had a paper seal, but the seal’s date was missing from the surviving copy.

The conservator found no obvious gap in the binding. Several leaves were brittle, and one section had been reinforced with a later strip of paper. The reinforcement concealed part of the inner margin. The conservator did not remove it. Without a conservation treatment, no one could determine whether a line continued beneath it.

The sealed envelope was not opened. Its exterior label read “index—restricted,” with a number that appeared on neither catalog entry. The access officer photographed the label and entered the discrepancy as a new question. The envelope’s contents were not assumed to be the withheld image.

The session ended with the volume returned to its tray, the seal intact, and the access decision still pending. Nia watched the clerk sign the handling sheet. Every person who had touched the outer packaging was listed. The log showed no unexplained gap.

In the corridor, she asked Sagara whether the mismatch meant the archive had lost something.

“It means the old and new counts don’t line up,” he said. “The reason is still open.”

“Could the reason be ordinary?”

“Yes.”

“Could it be deliberate?”

“Yes.”

She wrote both possibilities in her notes, then circled neither.

The access officer sent the team a provisional statement: one volume and one sealed index envelope were present; the catalog’s image counts could not yet be reconciled; no personal names had been reviewed. Nia read it twice and asked the clerk to add that the envelope had not been opened. The clerk did.

The case file received a new item number. The old microfilm number remained beside it, not beneath it.

When the corrected inventory arrived, a final line had been added in faded type: “Leaf count to be verified against original intake sequence.” It did not say a leaf was missing. It did not say what the sequence contained.

Nia placed the line in her chronology and stopped there. The next step belonged to the archive’s privacy officer, not to her curiosity.

After the session, the access officer checked the old reference numbers against the receiving ledger. The number on the sealed envelope appeared once in a transfer manifest, but the manifest described it only as “supplementary index material.” A clerk’s initials were legible; the clerk had retired and could not be reached until the following week. No date connected the envelope to the transfer of the bound register.

The access officer opened a preservation request for the manifest and the microfilm technician’s notes. It was not a request to unseal the envelope. Nia’s observer notes were reviewed by Sagara, who removed a personal name she had copied from a public index unrelated to the restricted volume. The corrected notes were countersigned and returned to her.

The archive kept the volume sealed, the envelope sealed, and the two catalog counts side by side. The question had become narrower: who assigned the envelope number, and from which source list? It still did not say whether any page was missing.
