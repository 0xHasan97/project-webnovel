**Current-State Recap: The Monster**

Lantern House and the inquiry. The 88-step route crosses the parcel boundary to a lower corridor, not beneath Aster’s classrooms. The alcove, capped drain, east-door instruction, cut name, and photograph gap remain unexplained. The Matron’s books and loose fragments record limited room-level counts, not transfers or outcomes. The east camera’s repair completion and the G2 visitor-log mark remain unverified. Former facilities clerk Hara Tadao could not identify the mark and died after his recorded interview; the camera/recorder clock ranges place his collapse 24–39 seconds after Ren was seen writing in his black notebook, but the page is not visible and the medical record has not established a cause. A narrow corridor inspection found no identifiable code plate or camera connection. Nia remains a supervised archive observer with her placement file kept separate from the police case. A restricted R-12 index and sealed envelope remain under privacy review.

The Mechanism (as stated in the existing case file): A clear name and face trigger a death within forty seconds of a heart event, or in the written manner within six minutes forty seconds. The writer must see the face; a name alone fails. Each use is recorded in the writer’s hand and cannot be erased. The ledger cannot be copied or photographed and exists in one place. It triggers a death but is not required for a death to appear natural. Anyone holding it may read it. The ledger does not state its number of uses; only the writer knows.

# Chapter 131: The Copy in the Tin Box

---

Hara’s daughter brought the box to the archive herself.

She had asked for a family member to be present, and for the first inventory to be made before anyone copied a page. The archive set up a clean table in the public records room, not the police conference room. Ishikawa and the records officer waited outside until she had checked the seal and said she was ready.

The metal box was small enough to carry in one hand. Its latch had a thin rust line but still worked. The daughter opened it, removed a folded towel, and showed the archive officer the contents without turning over any page: old carbon slips, two cabinet diagrams, a packet of blank tags, and a notebook with an address written inside the cover. She asked that the address remain private. The clerk recorded its presence without copying it.

“I don’t want all of this taken,” she said. “I want the few things my father mentioned checked against the inventory. He is not here to explain the rest.”

The records officer agreed. The team would not remove anything. The daughter would place candidate documents in a numbered sleeve, and she could stop at any point.

The cabinet diagram had a legend. G2 appeared beside a rectangular slot in the key cabinet, while the key tags were labeled with letters and numbers in a different format. The drawing was dated six years after the visitor-log entry. Its stamp identified it as a later revision. The old visitor mark could still have been initials, a cabinet code, or a different notation altogether.

Hara’s calendar note—“G2—slot or tag?”—was placed next to the diagram. The note showed that he had remembered the same ambiguity his daughter now saw. It did not make the later diagram a contemporaneous key record.

One carbon slip listed a temporary key issue. The cabinet field read G2, but the key number was blank. The recipient’s signature was on the copy, though a carbon smear crossed the first name. A date had been entered in a separate hand; the year was not legible. The slip might relate to the east gate because it came from the facilities box, but no location was written on the face.

“Can the handwriting identify the person?” Ishikawa asked from the doorway.

“Not from this copy,” said the archive specialist. “We can preserve it and seek the original if the family agrees.”

The daughter said she would consider it. She did not consent to a forensic comparison today.

The other slips were routine: a boiler-room cabinet, a roof access tag, two requests for replacement labels. One had a key returned without a time. None named a person who had used the east gate on the morning of the visitor-log entry.

The inventory took an hour. The daughter asked the clerk to read the list back before she signed. She corrected “key register” to “assorted key slips” and “Aster archive” to “father’s private papers.” Those changes were entered in the permanent record.

Ishikawa came in only after she had signed. He thanked her for allowing the review and said he would not ask her to decide what her father had meant. She looked at the cabinet diagram again.

“He told me he’d check,” she said. “I wish I had opened this box for him before.”

“No one could have known what was in it,” Ishikawa replied.

“That is true. It doesn’t make me wish less.”

The archive officer sealed the selected slips in a sleeve that remained in the daughter’s custody. She received the inventory and a copy of her father’s interview statement. No document was removed from the room.

After she left, the team compared the carbon slip to the visitor-log scan. The writing style was not a reliable match. The marks were made in different columns on different forms, and neither original was available for a proper comparison. The shared string “G2” could describe a cabinet location in one record and a person’s initials in another.

Mori asked whether the search had closed the key question.

“It narrowed one possible meaning,” Ishikawa said. “It didn’t identify the mark.”

The district chief asked if Hara’s death had affected the daughter’s decision to share the papers.

“She chose the scope,” Ishikawa said. “We record that. We don’t turn her cooperation into a theory.”

A separate request was sent for the original cabinet relocation sheet. The school’s current facilities office located a scan in an old renovation folder. It named two custodians but did not show who held the east key on the day of the visitor entry. The scan’s file date was newer than the paper date, and its source folder had no transfer history.

The archive logged the scan as an unverified copy. No one added the two names to the suspect list.

Hara’s daughter called the next morning to say she would keep the carbon slip at home for now. She would send a high-resolution scan after her brother had seen the inventory. The records officer thanked her and recorded the call. She had not refused access; she had not granted it either.

The old box had answered one question only in part. G2 could be a cabinet slot. The visitor-log mark remained a mark.

# Chapter 132: The Condition of the Grant

---

Nia asked the institute for the version of the fellowship agreement that existed before she applied.

She made the request through the privacy officer, with Dr. Sagara present. She specified that she wanted records about the funding conditions and the routing of her own application, not other students’ files. The institute agreed to search the migrated mailbox, the grant ledger, and the regional workshop archive. Sagara signed the supervision form but did not ask to see the response before Nia.

The first document arrived as a scanned addendum to the archive fellowship. The donor field remained masked. The amount matched the unrestricted grant listed on Nia’s copy, but the addendum placed conditions on how one youth placement would be offered.

The placement was to go to a student who independently identified a reset in a three-clock chronology. The exercise was to be circulated through a regional workshop as a “public-source case study,” though its underlying event could be hypothetical. The mentor named for the placement was Dr. Sagara.

Nia read the page twice. “This was written before I did the exercise?”

The institute’s officer checked the signature block. The addendum had been approved three years earlier. The packet had arrived at the workshop later. The exact date it reached Sagara was still unknown.

“Did you choose me?” Nia asked him.

“I recommended you after you corrected the chronology,” Sagara said. “I didn’t know the fellowship conditions were written that way.”

“Did you choose the packet?”

“No. A colleague sent it to me. I thought it was a useful exercise.”

“Did you know the case might be fictional?”

“No. The cover said public source.”

He did not look away from her, but neither did he reach for the document. The institute officer placed the addendum in a clear sleeve and noted that Sagara had not handled it.

The language did not name Nia. It did describe a test designed to select someone with the skill she had demonstrated. It did not say who wrote the exercise, who chose Sagara, or whether a real case had been adapted. The fund could have been an educational project with a theatrical cover. It could also have been built to find one kind of student.

The donor’s anonymity had not been required by law. It had been a condition of the grant. The institute had accepted that condition and separated the donor’s identity from the recipient file. The underlying banking details were held by an outside administrator and could not be produced without a separate request.

Sagara asked the officer why his name had been specified. She said the institute had no record explaining the choice. His mentor had been listed as a “qualified intermediary,” but no selection notes survived in the migrated folder.

“That phrase is on the form,” Nia said. “It makes it sound as if I was already expected.”

“It says the role was expected,” the officer replied. “It does not say you were.”

Nia nodded. She asked for the envelope tracking record and the workshop attendance list, not the donor’s personal bank data. The officer agreed to search for both. She also asked that her earlier application remain sealed from the police inquiry unless she gave permission. Ishikawa’s case was about Lantern House; this was her placement.

Sagara offered to withdraw as her supervisor if she preferred. Nia said she would decide after the archive search. She would not make that choice simply because the records made him look selected too.

At the public archive, she checked the catalog discrepancy she had logged. The sealed index envelope from the resident-intake register carried a reference that matched the addendum’s project code: R-12. The access officer confirmed that the code was present on the outside label in both records. Neither document explained what the code meant.

Ishikawa asked whether the matching code was enough to link the grant to the intake register.

“No,” said Nia. “It’s a lead. We need the ledger that assigns the code.”

The access officer agreed. The envelope would stay sealed until the privacy review was complete. Nia was not authorized to open it, and she did not ask for an exception.

The institute sent the workshop roster that afternoon. Sagara’s name appeared as a participant, not as a presenter. The packet had been distributed at his table, but the mailing list did not identify who delivered it. A box marked “sponsor copy” had been checked, though no sponsor was named.

The papers established a planned exercise and a preselected mentor. They did not establish that the case was staged, that Nia had been selected in advance, or that the donor had any connection to Lantern House.

Nia wrote those three limits on the top sheet of her notes. Under them, she added the single matching code, R-12, then circled it once.

The code had appeared before she had ever opened the packet. Its meaning remained under seal.

# Chapter 133: In the Margin

---

The archive did not open the envelope for Nia. It opened it for the privacy review, and let her observe the page that could lawfully be shown to her.

Her guardian had signed a new consent form. The institute’s privacy officer had agreed that the grant addendum concerned Nia’s own placement. The county archive’s counsel allowed a redacted image of one index card because the request code on it matched the project code in her fellowship file. Nia could see the card’s routing fields and the condition of the name line. She could not see the other residents’ names or inspect the bound register.

The archivist photographed the unopened envelope, checked the seal number, and cut it along the seam in the presence of two witnesses. Inside were three folded leaves, a typed index, and a card protected by a separate paper sleeve. Nothing was removed from the reading room.

The card bore the number R-12. Its heading had been partly torn away. The visible fields listed an intake date, an age band, and a transfer to Aster Institute in April. The name field was empty. Along the line, the paper fibers were roughened by repeated strokes. The card’s accession stamp sat over the worn fibers, placing the erasure before the archive received it.

A microfilm image made years earlier showed the same blank line. The archive technician could not recover the name from either image. The card had been altered before the microfilm copy, long before the present inquiry. No document identified who had removed it.

Nia did not ask the archivist to read anything hidden by the redaction. She asked whether the date and destination matched the timing on the publicly documented Quiet Ones list. The archivist said the month and destination were consistent with the entry marked “R,” but the card alone did not establish the person’s identity. Nia set the list beside the public copy and did not write a name in the blank.

The typed index included a cross-reference: “R-12 / Aster / observer route.” The same code appeared in the fellowship addendum. The third leaf was a routing memo from the grant administrator. It named Dr. Sagara as the required mentor, specified the three-clock exercise, and directed that the packet be sent through a regional workshop without its sponsor’s identity.

A second note was clipped to the memo. It was dated after Nia’s packet had reached Sagara but before Nia had applied for the fellowship. Its instruction was brief: “If the student identifies the reset without prompting, extend the observer placement. Keep the source undisclosed. Continue under R-12.”

The memo did not name Nia. The application record did. It showed that Sagara recommended her after she corrected the clock sequence, and that the fellowship office approved the offer using the R-12 fund. The route had been designed to select a student like her; once she appeared, the separate records showed that her name was used to complete the route.

Nia looked at the archive officer. “Who added this note?”

“The file doesn’t say.”

“Can the handwriting be compared?”

“The original is typed. The note has no signature. We can examine the paper and its transfer history, but we may not be able to attribute it.”

Nia asked the officer to show her the remaining exposed fields. One line contained an internal authorization: “Aster student contact—R. Aizawa.” It was not a birth name. It was not a signature. The archive did not treat it as proof of authorship. The name matched Ren’s school identity, and the route code matched his sealed intake file.

Sagara sat very still. He read the line once and returned his eyes to the table. “I was told I had found her,” he said. “I thought that was true.”

The records did not prove that Ren personally created every part of the exercise. They showed that someone had prepared a fictional-looking clock test, chosen Sagara in advance, arranged an anonymous grant, and kept the source hidden. A later authorization linked the route to Ren Aizawa and the R-12 file. The student who had believed she found the trail had been placed on it.

Nia asked for a copy of the redacted card and the routing memo. The archivist agreed, with the restrictions printed on each page. She asked for the earlier microfilm image too, so the record would preserve the erasure’s history. That request was granted.

The procedure officer explained that Nia could withdraw from the placement or continue under new supervision. Her family would receive the same choice in writing. The placement could not require her to investigate Ren or to use the information publicly.

Nia took the redacted copy. She aligned its edge with the old microfilm image. The name line remained blank in both.

“Is this enough to say I was selected?” she asked.

“It’s enough to say the path was prepared for someone with your skill,” Ishikawa said. “The later record puts your name on that path. It does not tell us who made every decision.”

“And Ren’s account?”

“He has not been asked about this file.”

“Then don’t call it an admission.”

“I won’t.”

The archivist sealed the originals in a new sleeve. The new seal number was entered beside the old one. Nia’s observer notes recorded the process, the redactions, and the precise words on the memo. She did not copy the name line as a puzzle or claim to know what lay beneath it.

Outside the reading room, the fellowship officer waited with the continuation form. Nia read the two choices: withdraw, or continue with a different supervisor and no requirement to participate in the case inquiry.

She asked for a third line to be added: continue the placement, but retain the right to refuse any task and keep her personal file outside Ishikawa’s case record.

The officer amended the form.

Nia signed only after the change was countersigned.

# Chapter 134: The Condition of Her Consent

---

Nia returned to the archive the next morning with a signed boundary, not a theory.

Her guardian had approved continued participation under the amended terms. Dr. Sagara would remain available as a teacher but would no longer supervise her access to the Lantern House file. A different archivist would review her notes. The institute’s privacy officer would retain the fellowship documents. The police inquiry would receive only a copy of the redacted routing memo, with Nia’s permission.

Ishikawa read the conditions before accepting the copy. One clause said Nia could withdraw at any time. Another barred the team from using her personal history as evidence against Ren. A third allowed her to question the records without being treated as a witness to events she had never observed.

“Why continue?” Ishikawa asked.

“Because the record says someone built the route. If I leave, the question doesn’t disappear. Someone else may answer it badly.”

“You don’t owe us the answer.”

“I know. I want the answer for myself.”

The statement was entered as her choice, not as a claim about Ren’s guilt. She had not seen the person who drafted the exercise or watched the grant being arranged. She had seen documents that linked her placement to the R-12 file and to Ren’s school identity. The source of the first idea remained open.

Sagara met her in the public atrium, away from the police rooms. He apologized for not asking more questions about the packet’s provenance. Nia told him she did not know whether another question would have changed anything.

“I thought I had discovered you,” he said. “You corrected my timeline. I liked that you had done it without being told what to look for.”

“That part was mine.”

“Yes.”

“You did recommend me after I found the reset. That was your choice.”

He looked down at the copy of the addendum. “Was it?”

“It was still a choice. They may have arranged the conditions. They didn’t move your hand.”

Sagara accepted the distinction. He asked whether she wanted him to contact the colleague who had forwarded the packet. Nia said yes, but only to request the envelope’s route and workshop records. No accusations, no mention of Ren, no suggestion that he had done something wrong.

The colleague replied that the packet had been left at Sagara’s table in a sealed workshop envelope. He had signed for a sponsor copy, but the envelope was already addressed to Sagara. He had assumed the organizers sent it. The workshop’s current office could not identify the person who delivered it; the volunteer roster from that year had been destroyed under the ordinary retention schedule.

The route explained how the packet reached Sagara, not who prepared it. Nia added that limitation to the provenance table.

Ishikawa convened the records team to decide how the new document should enter the case file. The chief wanted a clear statement about whether Ren had arranged the placement. Ishikawa refused to phrase the conclusion more strongly than the source supported. The memo tied the fund and candidate route to an Aster student contact called R. Aizawa. The intake card tied the project code to a blank-name record placed at Aster. Together, they strongly suggested Ren’s involvement. They did not establish his authorship of every document.

“Could he have been listed because someone used his name?” Mori asked.

“Yes,” Nia said. “That possibility needs to stay in the source note.”

“Do you believe it?”

“I believe it’s possible. I also believe the file was designed so I would find him.”

The room went quiet. Ishikawa asked whether she wanted that sentence in the official memo. Nia said no. It was her interpretation, not a fact she could prove from the records. The memo would state the documented chain, then separately identify her inference and its limits.

The team’s formal note said the R-12 file connected the prepared exercise, the Sagara mentorship condition, and the Aster student contact “R. Aizawa.” It stated that the identity behind the blank registry name field was not recovered. It did not say that Nia’s personal theory had been confirmed.

The headmistress received notice that Aster records might be relevant. She asked for a copy of the source list before agreeing to any search. Ishikawa sent it through counsel and requested preservation of student-side correspondence from the dates shown in the memo. The request did not authorize the school to search personal devices or seize Ren’s notebook.

The headmistress replied that the school would preserve the institutional mail logs and paper routing records. She would not disclose student records without proper authority. Ishikawa accepted the boundary and applied for a limited preservation order.

Nia watched her copy of the blank registry field go into the restricted sleeve. She signed the receipt and kept a duplicate of the redacted page. The name was still absent. The route around it was no longer empty.

At the end of the day, she submitted a new research question: who had designed the exercise, who had chosen Sagara, and when the Aster contact entered the chain? She did not write “why Ren chose me.” That was a question for a later record, if one existed.

Her consent had a condition: she would continue, but the inquiry would not own her story.

# Chapter 135: The First Question

---

The preservation order arrived without a search warrant.

It required Aster Institute to preserve institutional routing slips, grant correspondence, and archived mail logs connected to R-12. It did not authorize access to student devices or private notebooks. The headmistress accepted the order in writing and named a records custodian who would supervise the collection. Ren’s ordinary school file would remain sealed unless a separate authorization was obtained.

Ishikawa read the scope aloud to the team. Nia was not in the room. She had asked to receive only the public-facing explanation of what records were being preserved, not the private school files. The team complied.

The first package contained a paper transfer slip from Aster’s records office. It was dated three years earlier and routed to the regional institute’s workshop. The donor field was blank. The line for the initiating department read “special projects.” The contact field contained the typed name “R. Aizawa.” No signature was present.

The slip did not prove who had typed the name. It did establish that the contact was listed in Aster’s own routing record before Nia applied for the fellowship. The archive compared the paper’s date stamp against the office’s preserved stamp log. The stamp was in use that month. The slip’s transfer number matched the workshop envelope code.

A second page was a requisition for an educational grant, submitted through the Foundation’s administrator. Its stated purpose was to fund “one supervised youth placement in pattern analysis.” The donor remained anonymous to the institute, but the administrator’s payment reference linked the grant to R-12. The requisition named no recipient. It specified that the placement should be offered after a student identified a deliberate clock reset in the exercise.

The team placed the pages in sequence but did not join them by assumption. Aster had routed a packet to Sagara’s workshop. The anonymous grant created a conditional placement. The intake file carried the same code and an Aster destination. A later note directed the office to extend an offer to Nia after her correction. The contact field named R. Aizawa.

The series was specific enough to support a conclusion: Nia’s route had been prepared before she discovered the clock discrepancy, and Ren’s school identity was written into the chain. The documents did not establish whether he acted alone, who supplied the money, or what he expected Nia to do after joining the archive.

Ishikawa asked the headmistress to confirm whether Ren had held an official role in special projects. She said he had not. The department title referred to a restricted administrative code, not a student office. She could not explain why his name appeared there.

The records team requested the department index, but the Foundation’s counsel said the index contained protected third-party information. A privacy review would be required. No one asked the headmistress to identify the anonymous donor from memory.

Nia read the authorized public summary at the archive. It contained the exact lines from the transfer slip and the grant condition. She asked whether “deliberate clock reset” came from the exercise designer or from the donor. The papers did not say. The archivist added that unresolved question to the versioned timeline.

“You can see why I think it was built for me,” Nia said.

“I can,” the archivist replied. “But we’ll record what the pages show, and what you infer, in separate fields.”

“Good.”

The public summary did not name the anonymous donor or reproduce the registry card. The personal fields remained sealed. Nia requested that her own name be removed from any media statement until she decided whether to speak. Ishikawa agreed.

At school, the headmistress asked Ren to acknowledge the preservation order. She read it to him in the presence of the records custodian. The order did not accuse him; it required the school to preserve records. Ren signed the receipt, asked for a copy, and returned it without comment.

The custodian recorded that Ren had been calm and cooperative. The note did not describe his feelings. He did not ask which documents had been preserved or whether the inquiry had identified the R-12 code.

The next morning, Nia submitted a formal request to lead the public-source chronology under the archive’s supervision. She did not ask for police authority, sealed files, or access to Ren’s private records. She proposed a scope: document the source and limits of each timeline claim, separate the placement ledger from the count fragments, and publish corrections when a phrase exceeded its evidence.

Ishikawa reviewed the request. The chief asked if it was wise to make Nia more visible after the documents named her.

“She is already named in the placement file,” Ishikawa said. “The decision is whether she gets to set the terms of her own work.”

The chief approved the limited role with a separate supervisor, no witness contact, and a right to withdraw. Nia signed the amended appointment. The title on the form was “public chronology coordinator,” not investigator.

She returned to the workroom and placed the redacted R-12 card beside the grant timeline. The card’s name line was empty. The route to it was not.

On the whiteboard she wrote three questions: Who funded the route? Who wrote the exercise? What did the contact expect the student to do?

She did not write Ren’s name beneath them.

Then she uncapped a marker and drew a line from “Aster contact” to “R. Aizawa,” leaving the next box blank.

The first question had finally been asked in the right direction.
