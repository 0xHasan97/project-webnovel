The school’s preservation order produced six boxes and one empty field.

A records custodian delivered the boxes to the archive under seal. The list included institutional mail, grant routing slips, workshop correspondence, and paper transfer sheets. Student files and personal devices were excluded. Ishikawa checked the seals against the order; the headmistress checked the inventory against the school’s copy. Nia sat behind the marked line with her supervisor and watched the process. She had permission to observe the public handling, not to open a box herself.

The first box contained ordinary correspondence about the regional workshop. Aster’s office had sent a packet and a one-page instruction sheet. The instruction sheet described the three-clock exercise as “an assessment of source criticism.” It did not identify a real case. A second copy included the phrase “public-source case study,” printed in the footer. No source citation accompanied it.

The archivist checked the printouts against the school’s retention schedule. A blank page between the instruction sheet and the grant email had been counted as a leaf in the box inventory but not as a correspondence item. The counting rule explained a small discrepancy in the box totals; it did not explain why the original index had a separate field for “student contact.” The custodian added both counts to the log rather than making one replace the other.

Nia asked whether she could cite the blank leaf. Endo said she could cite the inventory discrepancy, but not assign meaning to an empty page. Nia entered the page number, condition, and source in her notes, then marked interpretation “none.”

The office routing form listed a delivery contact: R. Aizawa. The field marked “initiated by” was blank. The form carried a date stamp and an internal transfer number. The records custodian confirmed that the stamp was used by Aster’s central office at the time, but could not identify who completed the form.

Nia copied the field exactly, including the initial and the lack of a signature. She did not write “Ren ordered the packet.” The distinction made the sentence less forceful and more accurate.

The second box contained a short email printout between the school’s grant liaison and the outside administrator. The liaison asked whether the student’s identity was known before the workshop. The administrator replied: “No candidate has been named. The criteria are fixed; the recipient is not.” The message was dated before the exercise had been circulated.

Mori asked if this contradicted the note that said to extend the placement if a student found the reset.

“No,” the archivist said. “The condition could have been written before the student was known. The later note shows that Nia met it. It does not show that the grant’s author knew her name in advance.”

Nia read the exchange twice. Her route had been designed before she arrived, but the surviving email allowed that the person who designed it had not yet selected her. The later instruction linking her to the placement might have been a response to her result—or one step in a plan. The record did not resolve which.

The school’s custodian identified a redaction on the transferred copy. The original printout had been folded, and a line at the bottom was hidden in the crease. No one flattened it until the conservator photographed the fold. The line contained a second routing number, not a name. It matched the number on a sealed administrative index already under privacy review.

Ishikawa asked whether the line could be read aloud. The privacy officer said it could be logged in the restricted inventory but not circulated to the student observer. The request was outside the current authorization. Nia nodded and asked only that the index number be preserved for the next review.

The boxes were resealed. The archive assigned a new evidence number to the workshop correspondence and left the private index in its separate envelope. Nothing was removed from the school’s custody until counsel completed the transfer form.

The records custodian retained the seal receipt and sent the school a copy. Ishikawa’s office received only the authorized scans. The arrangement left an intact original at Aster, an archive handling log, and a police copy with its own hash. If a later version surfaced, the three could be compared without pretending they were the same object.

Hoshina asked for a statement. Ishikawa said the records showed a prepared exercise, a conditional grant, and a contact field using the cover identity “R. Aizawa.” They did not establish who typed the field or who designed the exercise. Hoshina wrote the wording down and read it back before she left.

The online article appeared that evening: “Aster Contact Appears in Nia Vale Fellowship Trail.” The body said the school record named “R. Aizawa” but did not prove who had entered the contact. The headline was accurate, though its comment section was not. Some readers called Nia a plant; others called Ren a sponsor. Neither claim came from the documents.

# Chapter 137: The Line Between Files

---

Nia’s first public chronology had two sections titled “Established” and “Not established.”

She had argued against a third section called “Likely.” Endo suggested that readers needed a place for hypotheses. Nia answered that hypotheses could be listed in a note only if each was attributed to the person making it. They settled on a separate “Questions raised” panel, with no unlabeled theories and no ranking of likelihood. The panel would link back to the source table so a reader could distinguish a document from a deduction.

She displayed the draft in the archive classroom. The first section named the existing records: the R-12 grant condition, the workshop packet, the Aster routing slip, the blank registry field, and her later placement. The second listed what those records could not show: who wrote the exercise, who typed the contact name, whether the donor knew Nia in advance, or what happened to any Lantern House resident.

The new supervisor, Ms. Endo, asked whether the timeline should mention the date Nia found the reset.

“It’s my own work,” Nia said. “But I don’t want it to sound like the clock result proves who designed the packet.”

“Then include the date and source, but keep the interpretation separate.”

Nia added a note that her correction established an error in the packet’s internal sequence, not the authenticity of the underlying case. She added a second note explaining that the fellowship condition predated her application, while the record naming her as recipient came later. Both statements had source citations.

The institute’s privacy officer delivered a response to Nia’s request for her own application log. The system had retained the date the offer was created and the date it was sent. A staff account had opened the file between those dates, but the migrated system did not retain the account’s display name. The access was routine for the fellowship office. The log could not show whether the user was a donor, a school employee, or an administrator.

Nia asked whether an access request could identify the account. The officer said the old system’s user table had been lost during migration. There was no preserved key that mapped the account code to a person. The absence was documented, not filled with a guess.

At the police station, Ishikawa declined an invitation to call the exercise a staged case. “We can say the packet’s source is unverified and the fellowship used it as a selection exercise,” he told the district chief. “We cannot say there was no real disappearance. We cannot say there was one.”

The chief asked whether the exact clock reset could be reproduced from the packet. Nia had prepared a demonstration using the three printed times. She showed the team that one clock had been reset after the witness statement was made. The sequence was internally inconsistent as presented. It remained possible that the packet was fictional, composite, or based on a real event with omissions.

Wataru listened from the end of the table. “The exercise was designed to reward the question she asked,” he said. “That tells us something about the person who wrote the condition.”

“It tells us the condition valued source criticism,” Endo replied. “It doesn’t tell us why.”

Wataru nodded. He did not add a theory to the record.

Nia asked that her personal fellowship documents not be attached to Hara’s medical file or the Lantern House count record. Ishikawa agreed. A separate cross-reference would note that the R-12 inquiry had procedural relevance to the school’s preservation request, but the source files would remain in the institute’s custody.

The move mattered. Before the correction, every unexplained document had been sliding toward one folder. Now the archive marked three separate inquiries: the gate and maintenance records; the count fragments and resident registers; and the provenance of Nia’s placement. Cross-references pointed to shared dates and codes without merging their conclusions.

The team’s weekly briefing drew a question about whether Hara’s death was connected to Nia’s file. Ishikawa said no such link had been established. Hara had been interviewed about the visitor-log mark. Nia’s documents concerned a fellowship route. Their appearance in the same archive did not connect them.

Ishikawa’s formal note separately listed what the R-12 file did not establish: the donor, the exercise author, the identity of the staff account that approved the offer, and whether the person entering the contact field knew Nia would satisfy the condition. The chief asked for that paragraph to be shortened. Ishikawa kept it intact, arguing that the limits were not a footnote to the finding; they defined it.

Hoshina quoted that answer. Her editor cut it from the headline but kept it in the body. Nia noticed the omission and requested a correction. The editor declined, saying the body remained accurate. Nia accepted the decision and updated the chronology to link to the full statement.

A school counselor asked Nia whether the coverage had made her feel unsafe. Nia said she wanted her visitor times kept private and requested that the public archive list her role without a daily schedule. The archive complied. Her safety request was not treated as evidence of a threat.

At the end of the day, she saved the chronology as version 1.0. Every claim carried a source and a confidence label. A note at the top stated that the document was a public records aid, not a police finding.

The project had started as a route somebody else built for her. The chronology was the first part she had designed herself.

The archive’s review software preserved the redline, the comments, and the final text. Nia’s account could edit the draft but could not publish it without Endo’s approval. A second reviewer checked every link and confirmed that the public copy omitted the registry’s personal fields. The release note stated that version 1.0 corrected earlier wording but did not supersede the source documents.

# Chapter 138: The Witness Column

---

Ishikawa added a fourth column to the case chronology: “Who can verify this?”

The change came after the district chief asked which statements depended on one witness alone. The original file had tracked date, claim, and document. The new column listed the person or record that could independently confirm each item. If no independent source existed, the cell said so.

The east-door routine depended on the Matron’s recollection and an unauthenticated slip. The camera outage depended on a work order and a later facilities note. The visitor-log mark remained an ambiguous image. Hara’s statement depended on his recorded interview; his death had its own medical file. Nia’s placement trail depended on institutional correspondence and a privacy-limited index. None of the columns silently borrowed certainty from another.

Mori asked if the matrix made their case look weaker.

“It makes it look like the case we have,” Ishikawa said.

The chief approved the revised chronology for internal use. He also asked Ishikawa to prepare a public version that omitted names protected by the privacy review. Nia worked on the public version under Endo’s supervision. She could propose wording; Ishikawa’s office approved release.

One line read: “The grant record names an Aster contact, R. Aizawa.” Nia changed it to: “A school routing slip contains the typed contact ‘R. Aizawa’; the person who entered the field has not been identified.” Endo accepted the edit.

Wataru brought a small notebook to the records room and asked the clerk to log it as his own working copy. It contained no copied protected names, only a list of questions and dates. The clerk marked it as an adviser’s personal note, not an official record. He left it closed during the team meeting.

Mori tested the verifier column against the maintenance packet. The interview with Kreutz was supposed to concern the contact field, not the child behind it.

Ishikawa scheduled it after Kreutz’s written response. The scope allowed questions about the grant and the typed “R. Aizawa” entry. The interview took place at the station with Kreutz’s counsel present. Nia attended only for the public portion, then waited outside while the protected questions were asked.

Kreutz said he recognized the R-12 code as one used by the Lantern House program office. He could not remember who created it. The grant administrator had handled several funds, and he had not kept the banking records. He did not know whether the same code on the later archive envelope referred to the same account.

“Was the contact field your instruction?” Ishikawa asked.

“I may have approved it. I cannot identify the form from this copy.”

“Did you ask the school to find a student who could detect the clock reset?”

“I asked for an exercise that would test how a student handled conflicting records. I don’t remember specifying the clocks.”

“Who specified them?”

“I don’t know.”

The answers were measured and did not remove the possibility that Kreutz had set the route in motion. His uncertainty was entered as a limitation, not accepted as exculpation.

Ishikawa showed him the blank registry card. Kreutz’s eyes remained on the redacted image. He said the line had been altered before the archive received it. The image could establish abrasion, but not the identity of the person who made it.
