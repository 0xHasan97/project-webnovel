# Chapter 141: The Sealed Comparison

---

Kreutz’s counsel submitted a request for a private verification procedure.

It did not ask the archive to disclose the erased name. It asked whether a neutral officer could determine if Kreutz’s claimed recognition was consistent with the original intake record, then report only yes, no, or indeterminate. The archive’s privacy counsel reviewed the request with Ishikawa, the district chief, and Nia’s appointed supervisor. Nia was not asked to participate; the request concerned a protected record and a witness’s claim.

The first proposed procedure failed immediately. Kreutz would be shown the card, but the card’s name field was blank in the authorized copy. The original was too fragile to put in front of him, and no readable image of the name existed in the archive file. A private procedure could not compare his memory to information no one could read.

The second proposal used a sealed envelope held by the former Lantern House administrator. Kreutz had once been the medical director, not the records custodian. The administrator’s successor could not confirm that the envelope contained the same name field. The chain-of-custody record began six years after the fire. That option could produce an answer, but not a reliable one.

Ishikawa asked whether Kreutz possessed another record with the name. Counsel said the sealed letter mentioned in the old interview remained with Kreutz, but he would not submit it for inspection without a protective order. The letter’s date, condition, and custody history had not been documented by the archive.

“Then a yes-or-no test would test whether he recognizes what he remembers,” Nia said, “not whether it matches the intake card.”

The adults around the table looked at her. She had been invited to review the public procedure note, not the protected material. Endo confirmed that her observation was within scope. Nia repeated the distinction for the record and asked that the note not call the result authentication.

The district chief crossed out “verify the identity” and wrote “assess the witness’s claim of recognition.” Ishikawa accepted the revision. It was a smaller question, but one the procedure could actually answer.

Kreutz agreed to a controlled session with conditions. He would view a sealed original only if a conservator approved the handling. The name would not be read aloud or transcribed. A privacy officer, the conservator, and his counsel would witness the session. The report would say whether he selected the field as familiar, unfamiliar, or uncertain. No one would infer that familiarity established the name’s accuracy.

The archive’s counsel asked whether Nia should be excluded from the public debrief. Ishikawa said she could see the public report after review; she would not see the card or hear any sound made during the recognition test. Nia agreed. She asked to receive the procedure’s limits in writing before the session, and the officer sent them the next day.

The privacy officer added a technical limit: the comparison could not be blind if Kreutz knew which card he was being shown, but it could not be double-blind if no archivist could verify the source object. They settled on a two-person chain. The conservator would confirm the card’s accession number without showing its personal fields; the privacy officer would record Kreutz’s response and seal it before anyone compared it with another record. That method would protect the name, not authenticate it. Nia asked that this distinction appear in the public summary before the session was scheduled.

The school custodian found no seal letter in the institutional boxes. The school’s copy of the transfer list mentioned one personal envelope returned to Kreutz after Lantern House closed, but did not describe its contents. The recipient line contained his title, not his signature. It was not the letter’s chain of custody.

Kreutz’s counsel supplied a short statement that he believed the name remained in his possession. He did not attach it. The archive logged the statement and asked whether he would allow a conservator to examine the envelope without opening it. Counsel requested another week.

Hoshina asked Ishikawa whether the procedure would settle the identity. He said it could not. She asked whether Kreutz’s familiarity would still be news. “It may be news about his memory,” he said. “It is not proof of the registry entry.”

She published that distinction in the weekly update. The headline called it a “name test.” Nia asked her to change the phrase because no comparison against a verified source was possible. Hoshina contacted her editor, who changed it to “Archive Weighs Private Recognition Procedure.” The headline was quieter than the article’s photograph, but it did not overstate the result.

The district chief delayed the session until the conservator had seen the physical card and Kreutz’s envelope. No date was set. Counsel’s request remained open.

At the end of the day, Nia read the proposed public report. It had one blank where the name would have gone and a footnote saying that no authorized copy contained legible text. She returned it with a correction: the blank did not mean the archive had confirmed erasure by any particular person; it described only the surviving image and the abraded card.

The question was no longer whether Kreutz would say the name. It was whether any surviving record could show that he remembered the same one.

# Chapter 142: The Missing Sender

---

Aster’s transfer sheet had a sender field after all.

The page surfaced in a box from the retired department administrator’s office. The former administrator’s daughter had found it among labeled school papers and returned it through counsel. The envelope was opened under the preservation order, photographed, and inventoried. The sheet carried the same transfer number as the workshop packet, but the name in the sender field had been covered by a narrow strip of correction tape.

No one peeled it back. The conservator photographed the tape under side lighting and described its edges. The layer had yellowed, but that did not date its application. A shallow impression beneath the strip was visible in one frame. It could be letters or pressure from another page in the stack.

The district chief asked the conservator whether the impression could be read.

“Not without treatment,” she said. “And even if we recover letters, we may not know whether they were written on this sheet or transferred from a page above it.”

The former administrator’s daughter said the papers had been kept in a desk drawer since her parent’s retirement. She could not say when they had entered the drawer or whether anyone else had handled them. The archive recorded her account, not an unbroken chain.

The sheet’s other fields were legible. It had been routed from Aster’s central office to the regional workshop. The initiating department was “special projects,” as in the earlier copy. A checkbox indicated that a return copy was expected. No return copy appeared in the transferred box.

Nia updated the chronology. She wrote that an institutional transfer sheet with the sender field covered had been found; the visible transfer number matched the workshop packet; the person or office beneath the tape was unknown. Endo asked whether “sender” could be an office rather than a person. Nia added that qualification.

The institute searched its old mail index for the transfer number. It found a receipt record: one sealed packet accepted by a workshop volunteer, one copy routed to Dr. Sagara. The receipt did not name the person who delivered it. The volunteer’s initials matched neither of the two clerks who had logged the grant files.

The search also found a calendar entry for the workshop, but no attachment and no list of packet recipients. It confirmed that the session took place on the date printed on the transfer receipt. It did not show who had sat at Sagara’s table or whether the packet had been delivered before the event. The institute preserved the calendar entry and noted that it came from a shared department calendar, not a personal account.

The surviving volunteer was contacted through the institute. She remembered a courier carrying a document wallet but could not identify the courier. She recalled being told not to open the envelope because it contained a student exercise. Her statement was recorded with a note that the event had occurred years earlier and her memory was not supported by a contemporaneous personal record.

Nia asked whether the separate dates and clerks made the packet and grant independent. The archivist said they showed separate handling, not independent origin: both envelopes could still have been prepared under one grant instruction. Nia added that sentence to the chronology’s source note. She did not call the chain a single conspiracy or a coincidence. The records could show branching paths without showing who stood at their start.

The courier could have been a staff member, a contractor, or a workshop participant. The institute’s visitor log for that room had not survived. The source chain now had one more human account and no confirmed sender.

Ishikawa asked whether the transfer strip should be examined for indentations. The conservator said a non-destructive imaging pass might improve the impression, but she could not promise legibility. The administrator’s daughter consented to the examination of the sheet. She did not consent to removing the correction tape.

Hoshina requested access to the original. The archive refused, offering the reviewed summary and images of the visible fields. Hoshina asked whether the tape itself was a sign of concealment. Nia said it was evidence of a covered field, not evidence of why it had been covered. Hoshina quoted the distinction, though the headline again emphasized the tape.

The school’s counsel asked Nia not to publish the former administrator’s name. Nia agreed; the person’s identity did not help readers assess the record. The public chronology referred to “a retired records administrator” and linked to the source description.

At the end of the week, the conservator reported that the pressure impression contained at least two vertical strokes and a curve. The marks were not enough to identify letters. A faint line continued past the edge of the tape, but the sheet beneath it had no matching entry in the visible transfer copy.

Ishikawa entered the result as “unreadable impression under correction tape.” Mori asked if the original sender might now be discoverable. Ishikawa said there was a chance, not a lead they could responsibly name.

Nia added a question to the public chronology: “Who prepared and delivered the packet?” The answer cell read “not established.”

The sender field had survived in the form of a covered space. It still did not have a name.

# Chapter 143: A Choice Outside the File

---

Nia declined the first press invitation and accepted the second.

The first had asked her to sit across from a commentator who planned to debate whether she had been planted at the archive. The second came from a local public-records forum. It wanted her to explain how a chronology could distinguish documents, testimony, and inference. Nia asked Ms. Endo to review the invitation and her proposed remarks. The forum agreed not to discuss her private fellowship file beyond the already published redacted findings.

She prepared three examples. The visitor-log mark: an image with an ambiguous code. The camera work order: an intermittent fault and disconnection pending parts, with no proof of repair. The R-12 records: a prepared exercise and a typed contact field, with no verified typist. She included the limits with each example, even when the moderator suggested saving them for questions.

“People won’t stay for a list of things you don’t know,” he said during rehearsal.

“Then they should know what we do know before they decide what it means.”

At the forum, the first question asked whether the archive had found a conspiracy. Nia said it had found records that raised questions about how a youth placement was funded and routed. It had not found a complete author chain or evidence that the anonymous donor controlled the archive. She asked the moderator to correct the word “conspiracy” in the event notes.

A second question asked whether Ren Aizawa had engineered her fellowship. Nia answered that one Aster routing slip typed “R. Aizawa,” and the R-12 file linked her placement to a pre-existing exercise condition. She did not know who typed the contact or whether the recipient had been chosen before she solved the exercise. She was willing to say the route was prepared; she was not willing to claim every step had been directed by one person.

The moderator asked whether she felt manipulated.

“I feel that a decision about my future was made with information withheld from me,” Nia said. “I’m still deciding what that means.”

She did not call herself a victim. She did not say the route had been harmless. She described a choice she had made since learning the records: to continue under conditions she set, with the right to stop.

Hoshina sat in the back row and took notes. She did not ask a question. Afterward, she told Nia she had been clear.

“Clear is not the same as safe,” Nia said.

“No,” Hoshina replied. “It isn’t.”

The forum organizers offered to publish her slides. Nia reviewed them, removed the image of the abraded registry field, and kept the redacted citations. She approved publication with the note that she spoke as a student archive observer, not on behalf of police or the institute.

The talk changed how local outlets described her. Some called her the “new investigator.” She requested corrections. The records forum added her role line to its recording. Two outlets corrected their captions; one did not.

Afterward, the forum offered to list her direct contact address. Nia declined and asked them to route future questions through Endo. The moderator agreed to remove the address before posting the recording. Nia also asked the event staff to retain the original questions, including the ones they had not selected, so no edited clip would imply she had answered a different question. The archive received the recording and the moderator’s transcript as separate files.

At the station, Ishikawa watched the recording with the district chief. The chief asked if Nia’s public role would complicate the inquiry.

“It could,” Ishikawa said. “So could silence. She has not released protected material or claimed police authority.”

“Do you trust her?”

“I trust the terms she signed. I verify the documents.”

The chief accepted the answer. The preservation order remained narrow. The public forum did not authorize a search of Ren’s personal records.

Ren watched the recording later in the school library, according to the librarian. He sat at a public terminal and watched the full talk once, without pausing. The librarian did not see what pages he opened before or after it. The observation was added to the school’s public-area log because the terminal had been under routine maintenance review; it did not become evidence of his intent.

The next morning, a folded note appeared in Nia’s archive mailbox. It contained no threat. It read: “You are right to keep the columns separate.” There was no signature, postmark, or envelope. The mail clerk could not say who had placed it there; several staff had access to the slot.

Nia reported the note to Endo and asked that it be logged as an unsolicited message, not attributed to Ren or Kreutz. The archive photographed it, preserved it in a sleeve, and did not circulate the wording beyond the supervisor.

She could have taken it as praise, warning, or coincidence. She entered none of those as fact.

Her forum slides remained online. Beside the video, the forum posted her approved role and the correction policy. The audience had heard what she knew and what she would not pretend to know.

The note stayed unsigned. Nia continued anyway.

# Chapter 144: The Return Receipt

---

The school received a request for the notebook page Ren had offered after Hara’s interview.

Ishikawa’s office had not requested it before. The new request asked only for the page concerning the interview, through the headmistress, and cited the preservation order’s limits. The school forwarded the request to Ren. He replied that he would provide the page if the archive accepted a condition: it could be copied and reviewed, but not separated from the notebook until a conservator assessed the binding.

The headmistress accepted the condition. The page was not a bound leaf; the notebook’s rear section had a removable paper sleeve. Ren would bring the sleeve to a supervised review, with the entire notebook remaining in his possession. The police had no authority to seize it. The archive would record the movement and photograph only the designated page if Ren consented.

At the review, Ren arrived with the same plain black notebook seen in the corridor footage. Ishikawa, the headmistress, a conservator, and Ren sat at the table. Nia did not attend. Her placement file was unrelated, and she had asked not to be placed in a role where her presence might be read as an accusation.

Ren opened the sleeve to a page bearing the date, “Hara interview,” and three short entries: G2—meaning not confirmed; clerk remembers cabinet slot; ask for source record. The entries matched the page he had submitted after the death. There was no visible name, medical detail, or instruction about Hara.

The conservator inspected the paper, the sleeve, and the surrounding pages without removing them. She found no visible evidence that the page had been replaced, but said the examination could not prove when it was written. The camera footage showed Ren writing in the notebook during the interval; it did not show the page. The note could have been written then, earlier, or later.

Ishikawa asked Ren whether he had written the entries during the break.

“I wrote notes after the interview,” Ren said.

“Before or after Hara collapsed?”

“I don’t remember the exact order.”

“Did you write anything else?”

“Other school notes. They are not part of this request.”

The answer was recorded without treating his refusal to produce unrelated pages as evidence of guilt. The page’s language was consistent with the statement he had already given, but consistency did not establish the timestamp.

The archive made a color scan with Ren’s consent. He watched the file hash generate and signed the receipt. The original notebook never left his hands. The scan was stored beside, not inside, the medical-event file. A cross-reference allowed investigators to retrieve it without merging the medical timeline and the key search.

The district chief asked if the note settled the interval.

“No,” the conservator said. “We have a page consistent with the earlier submission and no physical date marker.”

“Does the corridor image show writing?”

“Yes.”

“Can we say he wrote this page then?”

“No.”

Ishikawa approved the public summary: Ren voluntarily provided a page of notes about the interview; its contents did not identify the cause of Hara’s collapse, and its exact writing time was not established. The summary did not mention the ledger’s other pages.

The headmistress asked Ren why he had offered the page at all. He said the interview note belonged to the inquiry and he did not object to sharing it. She asked why the notebook had to remain with him. He answered that it contained private school work unrelated to the case. She accepted the condition as consistent with the request.

Hoshina asked for the scan. Ishikawa released the public copy with Ren’s unrelated material excluded. The article’s headline read “Ren Shares Hara Interview Note.” The body explained that the scan did not timestamp the writing. Hoshina did not use the photograph of the black notebook from the corridor camera.

At school, students began repeating that Ren had “shown the notebook.” The headmistress corrected the rumor: he had shared one page, not the ledger’s contents. She made no statement about what the notebook otherwise contained.

One student claimed that the police had copied the whole black notebook. The headmistress issued a correction to the student bulletin: one page had been imaged with Ren’s consent; the notebook remained in his possession; no other pages had been reviewed. She did not describe what the notebook contained. The correction reduced one rumor and produced another, but it kept the public record aligned with the actual receipt.

Ren placed the notebook back into his bag. The headmistress asked whether he wanted a signed copy of the receipt. He did. He read the line describing the page as “provided voluntarily for limited copying,” then corrected “provided” to “made available for imaging.” He signed beside the change.

The record had a copy of the page. It still had no clock that could say when Ren wrote it.

The conservator’s note also described the sleeve: it had not been sealed when Ren first offered to share the page, and the page had no independent time stamp. The scan preserved its visible contents and condition on the review date, not its creation date. The clerk added that distinction to the receipt before Ren signed it. He read the addition and made no objection.

# Chapter 145: The First Draft

---

Nia published the chronology only after the source table survived a hostile review.

The reviewer was not a journalist or a police officer. She was a retired records manager appointed by the archive’s oversight board to test the public document for unsupported links. She had authority to flag language, not to change Nia’s conclusions. Every annotation had to cite a source or identify a missing source.

Her first mark was beside “R-12 led to Nia’s placement.” She wrote: “The file links the grant condition and later offer; do not imply a single continuous handler.” Nia accepted the comment and changed the line to “The R-12 condition shaped the placement route; the individual routing chain remains incomplete.”

The second mark was beside the Aster contact field. The reviewer asked whether “R. Aizawa” could be a department code. No key for the field survived. Nia changed “Ren Aizawa was listed as the contact” to “The typed field reads ‘R. Aizawa’; the record does not identify the typist or establish whether the entry names an individual or a code.”

The third mark was beside the blank intake name. The reviewer said “erased name” might overstate what the card showed. The microfilm established that the line was already blank; the physical card showed abrasion. Nia changed the heading to “Altered or abraded name field; identity unknown.”

The fourth mark concerned Hara. The reviewer said the chronology placed the notebook writing and collapse side by side, inviting readers to infer cause. Nia separated them into different sections and added a note: “Temporal proximity is documented; causal relationship is not established.” She linked the medical file only through a restricted cross-reference, not the public page.

The retired manager asked if Nia was willing to publish a document that could make her appear evasive. Nia said it was better to be accused of caution than to claim a fact she did not have.

The chronology went live with a version number, change log, and source index. It named the limits alongside the findings. It described the sealed envelope’s exterior without implying its contents. It stated that Kreutz claimed recognition of the blank name but that the question had been withdrawn before any disclosure or verification.

The response was immediate. One reader posted that the archive was hiding the truth. Another said the public record proved Ren had chosen Nia years earlier. A third accused Nia of protecting him. The moderation team removed personal threats but left criticism visible.

The archive’s moderation queue received more than a hundred comments in its first hour. Most disputed the wording, not the sources. Endo asked Nia whether she wanted the page taken offline until the attention passed. Nia said no, but asked that the safety contact be moved to the top and that the source index remain downloadable without her visitor schedule. The archive made both changes. The chronology stayed public; her routine did not.

Nia read the comments once. She did not answer them from her personal account. She submitted one formal correction where a site claimed the index card contained a recovered name. The site changed the sentence to “The archive has not disclosed a name.” It did not change its headline.

The district chief read the chronology and asked whether it would interfere with the inquiry. Ishikawa said the source index could help the public distinguish what the team had and what it did not. The chief approved the release but prohibited linking to protected images. Nia’s name appeared in the credits as “student chronology coordinator,” not as an investigator or source witness.

The public page also had an “open questions” section. It listed the sender of the workshop packet, the meaning of R-12, the source of Kreutz’s recognition, the date and purpose of the resident-intake index, the G2 visitor-log mark, and the camera repair status. The names of former residents were absent.

Nia checked each question against the source table. She left the birth-name entry blank. She did not replace it with “unknown person” or a guessed initial. The label read: “Identity withheld in the surviving copy; no verified transcription.”

Himeno called after reading the page. She said Nia had made the unknowns visible without making them dramatic. Nia thanked her. Then she asked whether Mio still wanted the east-door recollection kept out of the public chronology. Himeno said Mio had not given permission to quote her. Nia left it out.

Kreutz’s counsel sent a request to correct the phrase “claimed recognition,” arguing that Kreutz had answered yes to Ishikawa’s question. The archive replied that the wording described the record: Kreutz had claimed familiarity, but no matching source was available. Counsel did not appeal.

That evening, the server’s access log recorded an anonymous download of the chronology and one failed request for the restricted card image. The log did not identify a person or show whether the attempt came from inside or outside the school. The archive preserved the entry and notified its security officer.

Nia read the log the next morning. She asked that the failed request remain separate from the contents of the card. The security officer agreed. No one knew who had tried to open the image.

The first draft had been revised, checked, and published. Its most important line was still the one it did not fill in.
