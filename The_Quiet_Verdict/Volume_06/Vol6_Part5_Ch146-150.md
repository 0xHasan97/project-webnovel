**Current-State Recap: The Monster**

The lower corridor lies across the parcel boundary and has not been established as a residence. The 88-step route, alcove, capped drain, paper strip, cut name, and photograph gap remain unresolved. The count books and fragments are room-level records, not transfer lists; no resident outcomes are established. G2 is a shared program/cabinet code, not a confirmed interpretation of the visitor-log mark. A work order records the east camera’s intermittent fault and disconnection, but its repair status remains unknown. Former facilities clerk Hara Tadao died after his interview; recorded camera and recorder ranges place his collapse 24–39 seconds after Ren was observed writing, but no page content or cause of death has been established in-world. Nia continues by choice as a supervised public-chronology coordinator. The R-12 exercise and grant records show a prepared route and a typed Aster contact, while authorship, donor, and recipient-selection timing remain unknown. Kreutz claims to recognize the abraded registry name, but it has not been disclosed or authenticated. The six-week inquiry is extended narrowly; a new placement-receipt submission has been claimed but not verified.

# Chapter 146: The Unsigned Note

---

The archive did not discard the note after Nia’s chronology went public.

It had been photographed and placed in a sleeve, with the date and mailbox logged. Several people had routine access to the slot: staff, students in the supervised program, and maintenance personnel carrying outgoing forms. The note had no envelope or fingerprint-quality surface. No one had observed it being placed there. The security officer’s review could not identify a writer or even establish when it had arrived, beyond the interval between two checks.

Nia asked to include the note in her personal file, not the public chronology. Endo approved. Its sentence—“You are right to keep the columns separate”—might refer to the chronology, the inquiry, or something else. Without a source, it did not belong in the public evidence table.

The archive’s security officer added a second page to the incident log: one failed request for a protected card image, following an anonymous download of the public chronology. The system could not identify a person from the request. A security analyst explained that the request may have been made by someone with a mistyped link, a saved address, or a routine browser retry. The archive had no evidence of a breach or of access to the image. The attempt had failed.

The security review also checked the retention settings for the failed request. The image server had returned a denial before opening the file, and the request contained no valid session token. The access log did not retain a full network address under the archive’s privacy policy. A technician confirmed that enabling more detailed tracking would require a policy change and notice to every user, not an ad hoc setting for Nia’s page.

The board declined to change the policy. It asked the security officer to preserve the existing event record and monitor for repeated attempts through the same route. There had been none. Nia said she did not want a special surveillance rule built around an event no one could attribute. The board minutes recorded her position and the decision to use the same access controls for all restricted files.

A student volunteer asked whether the failed request should be named in the chronology at all. Nia said the public page needed to explain why its access log showed a denied event, but it did not need to imply that someone had tried to steal a name. Endo agreed. The note was filed under archive security, and the chronology included only the fact that an attempted request had been denied, with no attributed actor or motive.

“Can we know whether it came from the school network?” Ishikawa asked.

“The retained log doesn’t include that detail,” the analyst said. “We can preserve the event, not locate a person.”

The chief asked if the public chronology should be taken down.

Nia said no. “If there’s a specific safety risk, we can remove a specific detail. But the public page contains no protected names or live schedules.”

She asked the archive to confirm that the restricted image remained inaccessible and to review whether any other files shared the same link. The analyst checked the permissions with the archive officer. The image had never been available at that address; the request failed at the access check. No other files were exposed. The officer documented the review and changed nothing about the case evidence.

Hoshina asked Nia if she felt someone was watching her.

“I don’t know who wrote the note,” Nia said. “I don’t know who made the request. I’m not going to turn two unknowns into one person.”

Hoshina put down her recorder. “That’s not the answer people want.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

The reporter respected the boundary. Her story described the note and failed request as unassigned, and quoted the archive confirming that no protected image had been accessed. A commentary site called them a warning. Another called them a message from Ren. Neither could cite a source.

The school counselor offered Nia a change in her travel arrangements. Nia accepted the offer to have a staff escort after evening sessions, but declined to alter her class schedule. She asked that the escort not be described as a security response unless the school had a specific incident to report. The counselor agreed.

At the archive, the custodian updated the visitor procedure for everyone using the public records room: a sign-in sheet, a visible staff member, and no unsupervised access to the mail slot. The change applied to all observers. It did not single Nia out or identify the note’s author.

Ren passed the new sign-in table after school. He wrote his name in the student visitor column, checked “public chronology,” and left the time blank until the clerk asked him to complete it. He entered the time and signed. The clerk confirmed that the entry was correct. No one asked him about the note.

The clerk entered Ren’s visit in the ordinary log and scanned the row with the day’s records. The archive did not create a separate report about his presence. Nia saw the scan later while checking dates and asked whether the row had been added before or after the note appeared. The clerk confirmed it was a routine entry made on the date shown. The note itself had arrived earlier, during an interval in which multiple people had access to the slot. The two events remained separate.

The public log showed that Ren had visited the room. It did not show what he had read or why he came. Nia saw the entry during her next review and asked that it remain in the visitor record, not be copied into the source chronology without a reason.

Endo asked whether Nia was concerned the entry could be interpreted against her.

“It can be interpreted either way,” she said. “That doesn’t make it evidence of either.”

She saved the revised file. The note stayed private, the failed request stayed an unassigned security event, and the page remained public.

A message could be aimed at her without being attributable to anyone. The record did not let her choose which possibility to fear.

# Chapter 147: The Recognition Procedure

---

The conservator examined Kreutz’s envelope without opening it.

The examination took place at the archive, with Kreutz, his counsel, the privacy officer, and two conservators present. The envelope had been brought inside a locked case. Kreutz stated that it had remained in his desk since the year Lantern House closed. No witness had monitored it during that period. The archive logged the statement as his account of custody.

The envelope was folded once and addressed in a hand Kreutz said he recognized as his own. A seal covered the flap. The paper had a date in one corner, but no postmark or office stamp. A corner crease and a small tear were visible beneath the seal. The conservator could not determine when either mark had formed.

“Is this the document that contains the name?” the privacy officer asked.

“I believe it does,” Kreutz said.

“Can we establish that without opening it?”

“No.”

The conservator photographed the exterior, measured the paper, and checked the seal under magnification. The seal appeared intact, though a conservation examination could not prove it had never been opened and resealed. Kreutz declined to authorize opening. His counsel said the envelope was a private record and that any disclosure required a court order or a separate agreement.

Ishikawa did not ask for the contents. He asked whether Kreutz would permit a neutral reader to confirm only whether the name inside matched the blank registry card. Counsel said that would still require a reliable comparator. The card carried no readable name. A confirmation against Kreutz’s memory would only show that the memory and the letter agreed, if they did; it would not authenticate the original registry field.

The recognition session was postponed. The archive created a status note: envelope present; custody history reported by Kreutz; contents unverified; seal not opened. The public summary would not refer to it as a letter containing the name.

Kreutz asked if he could withdraw his earlier answer. Ishikawa said he could correct or clarify his statement, but the transcript would preserve the original exchange. Kreutz said he was not withdrawing it. He was saying he could not prove it.

Nia received the public summary after Endo reviewed it. She changed “Kreutz’s letter may confirm his recognition” to “Kreutz reports that a sealed personal envelope may contain the name he claims to recognize.” Endo approved the wording. It attributed both the object and the claim.

The distinction angered Kreutz’s counsel, who said the summary made his client sound unreliable. Ishikawa replied that reliability could not be inferred either way from the available file. The public notice remained unchanged.

The archive also found an older inventory card for personal effects returned to Kreutz after the closure. It listed “one sealed envelope, personal.” There was no description of the contents and no signature beside the return. The date was six weeks after the fire. That record supported the existence of an envelope at that time, but not the current envelope’s identity or uninterrupted custody.

Kreutz said it was the same envelope. He could not prove that from the inventory. The conservator noted a difference in paper stock between the older inventory’s generic envelope description and the object now examined, but the inventory did not specify the stock of the returned item. No comparison could be made.

Hoshina asked if the old card changed the story. Nia said it changed the custody question, not the identity question. The archive’s public note included the date and the lack of a signature, without identifying any staff member who might have handled the transfer.

The recognition procedure remained suspended. A court order could compel production, but the police had not sought one. Ishikawa said the inquiry would first determine whether the envelope was material and whether less intrusive sources remained. Kreutz’s counsel asked for the request to be put in writing if that changed.

At the end of the review, Kreutz placed the envelope back in its case. The archive did not retain it. He signed a receipt acknowledging that the photographs showed its exterior only. The privacy officer signed as witness to the inspection, not to the envelope’s contents.

The conservator compared the envelope’s dimensions to the general description on the older effects card. They were consistent with several common stationery sizes. No unique watermark or seal impression linked the present object to the one returned after the fire. Kreutz’s account was not contradicted, but neither was it independently confirmed.

The privacy officer proposed a later review only if a new source became available: a contemporaneous inventory, a witness to the envelope’s transfer, or a lawful order allowing the contents to be examined. Without one, another recognition session would repeat the same limitation. Counsel agreed to take the proposal to Kreutz. No date was scheduled.

Outside, Hoshina asked him why he had said “yes” in the earlier interview.

“I knew what I believed,” he said. “I did not know what I could prove.”

“That’s a careful answer.”

“It is the only one I have.”

He left with the envelope. The archive kept the image, the custody note, and the blank registry field. None of them could supply the missing name.

# Chapter 148: The Codebook

---

The R-12 code had been used for more than one project.

The county archive located a grant-code index in the records of the former regional administrator. The index listed R-12 as a program-routing category, not a unique donor or account. Four initiatives had used it over five years: a school reading project, a clinic outreach fund, a youth placement program, and a records-analysis exercise. The ledger separated the grants by subcode, but the last digit was missing from the Nia files.

The archive did not assume the entries belonged to a single sponsor. The codebook used R for “regional” and 12 for the program cycle. A handwritten key on the inside cover said the subcode identified a budget line, but the associated pages were held under a separate privacy restriction.

Nia revised the chronology’s R-12 description. The code linked the documents through an administrative category; it did not identify the donor. The exercise, the conditional grant, and the resident-intake index still shared the same code, but the common label could have been assigned centrally to multiple projects.

The county archive found a payment authorization for the youth placement fund. The payer was a nonprofit administrator, not a named private donor. That administrator had distributed money from several pooled sources. The R-12 entry showed the grant leaving the pool, not who contributed it.

Ishikawa asked whether he could request the pool’s donor schedule.

The privacy officer said a legal basis and a precise date range would be needed. The old administrator’s records included beneficiaries unrelated to Lantern House. A broad request could expose private financial information. Ishikawa narrowed his application to the entries linked to the Aster routing slip and the placement condition.

The request was accepted for review but not yet approved. The administrator’s counsel asked for the original transfer number, a summary of the public-interest basis, and a commitment not to disclose unrelated contributors. Ishikawa supplied all three.

Nia asked to be excluded from the donor schedule if it was produced. The request would expose her application route, but not give her authority over financial records. She said she wanted to know how the chronology described the grant, not to inspect every person who had funded the pool. The privacy officer recorded her preference.

The Aster records custodian found a second subcode on the back of the grant addendum, partly obscured by a folded corner. The number differed from the one on the intake index. The scan had originally flattened the paper and lost the corner. The conservator photographed the fold without pressing it. The subcode remained incomplete, but the visible digits showed that the documents were not identical in their internal coding.

The records could still be connected through the overall R-12 category and the language of the placement condition. They could not be treated as entries from the same account line.

The grant administrator’s counsel supplied a short explanation of the codebook. R-12 marked the administrative cycle, and the following digits would identify separate projects. The full suffix did not appear on the public copies. The administrator would release the relevant suffix only after the archive showed that the request did not expose unrelated beneficiaries.

Ishikawa amended the request to list the three record numbers already in evidence. He did not ask for the full donor pool or all four initiatives. The revised scope allowed the administrator’s counsel to review only the entries that could connect the exercise, the conditional placement, or the resident-intake index. The review would not establish authorship by itself; it might show whether the records shared an account line.

The explanation was not treated as a sworn statement. Ishikawa asked the administrator to provide the codebook page, the applicable retention schedule, and a signed account of how the category had been used. Counsel agreed to locate those records. Until the source material arrived, the public chronology labeled the explanation “administrator’s description, supporting page requested.” Nia added a date for follow-up rather than letting the temporary account stand as a final answer.

Wataru asked whether this weakened the evidence that Nia’s path had been designed.

“It weakens the shortcut,” Ishikawa said. “It doesn’t erase the grant condition or the later offer.”

Nia agreed. “The route was prepared. The same person may or may not have controlled each document.”

She added a note in the public chronology: “R-12 is a shared program code. Do not use it alone to identify a donor or prove common authorship.” A link to the correction history remained beside the earlier, less qualified version.

Hoshina requested the codebook. The county archive released a redacted scan of the public key, with no beneficiary details. Her article corrected an earlier reference to “the R-12 fund” and described it as a program category. The correction appeared below the original article rather than replacing it.

At the school, a student asked Nia if the codebook meant she had been wrong. Nia said the original wording had been too broad. The grant and exercise still had a documented route; the code did not tell them who paid or who arranged it.

The student asked if she was disappointed.

“I prefer a smaller true statement to a larger false one.”

The county archive’s review date was set for the following month. Until then, the donor remained behind the pooled account, and the last subcode remained folded out of sight.

A label had linked the papers. It had never named the hand behind them.

# Chapter 149: The Public File

---

The public chronology acquired its first outside editor.

The archive oversight board appointed two former records officers to review the source index and recommend whether it could remain online. Neither had worked at Aster or Lantern House. They checked the citations against the release copies and flagged three issues: one newspaper article linked to a protected photograph; a transcript excerpt lacked the correction history; and a summary described Kreutz’s answer as “confirmed.”

Nia removed the image link, attached the transcript’s version note, and changed “confirmed” to “claimed.” She added an explanation that the archive had not compared Kreutz’s memory with a legible name source. The second reviewer verified each change before the board approved the page.

The oversight board asked Nia to chair the public chronology group. The title carried no investigative authority. She could set meeting agendas, propose corrections, and invite public records questions. She could not interview witnesses, access sealed records, or direct Ishikawa’s team. Endo would remain her supervisor; the privacy officer would review each release.

Nia accepted after requesting that the role be renewable month by month and that she retain a right to decline any topic. The board added both conditions. Her guardian signed the updated placement consent. Nia signed beneath him.

The first public meeting drew more people than the archive room could hold. Several were former Lantern House staff; others were reporters and relatives of people listed in old placement files. The board held the meeting in a public hall with a remote option. Personal names were not displayed. The moderator read the privacy rules before Nia opened the source table.

A former clerk asked if the thirty-one on the loose count fragment was connected to the thirty-one-name relocation schedule. Nia said no record had established that link. The clerk said the office sometimes used the same total in weekly summaries. The archive asked if he would provide a statement and any document supporting that practice. He said he would search his papers but did not want his name published before he decided.

The answer became a lead, not a confirmation. Nia added “former clerk’s recollection offered; documentation pending” to the private question log. She did not add it to the public findings.

The clerk later sent a short message confirming only that he had used weekly summaries during his employment. He did not identify the fragment, the date, or the institution. He asked to see the public copy before deciding whether to search his papers. The archive sent the same redacted chronology available to any reader and did not show him the restricted image. Nia noted that his recollection could suggest a routine use for a repeated total, but it could not establish that the particular thirty-one had been a weekly summary.

Another attendee said the east gate was used by a night guard. The moderator asked for a date or record. The attendee said he had heard it from a relative. Nia thanked him and logged the source as secondhand. No one asked for the relative’s identity in the room.

The meeting changed the archive’s work plan. The records office would create a public intake form for documents and recollections, with separate consent choices for attribution, preservation, and publication. Nia would review submissions for routing, not truth. Ishikawa’s office would receive only items relevant to its authorization.

Before the next meeting, the board tested the new intake form with three submissions already in the queue. One included a photocopy with no source; one was a recollection offered without permission to publish; the third named a former resident and attached a page that had not been redacted. Nia routed the first for provenance review, held the second under private consent, and asked the privacy officer to restrict the third before anyone opened it. The sequence was logged so the submitters could see what happened to their material.

After the meeting, Hoshina asked if Nia had become the head of the investigation.

“No,” Nia said. “I coordinate the public chronology. Ishikawa leads the police inquiry.”

“Will you ever call yourself an investigator?”

“Not unless I have the authority and the training. Right now I have a source table and a list of questions.”

Hoshina smiled. “That may be more useful.”

The archive posted the meeting minutes after removing one attendee’s personal address and a reference that could identify a former resident. The minutes included the former clerk’s offer only as an unattributed lead. The oversight board retained the unredacted intake privately.

At the station, Ishikawa read the minutes. The chief asked whether the public group was drawing records out of people the police could not reach.

“It may,” Ishikawa said. “But we cannot convert a public submission into evidence without checking its source and consent.”

“Then what does the group do for us?”

“It makes the gaps visible. It may help identify where to ask next.”

The chief approved continued coordination, with no direct witness contact by Nia. The rule was written into the inquiry plan, not left to memory.

By evening, the public file had a new section: “Submissions received; verification pending.” It contained no names and no conclusions. The former clerk’s recollection waited there, beside a blank space for the document he might find.

Nia had been given a chair. She had not been given the case.

# Chapter 150: The Line Forward

---

The six-week inquiry ended with a request for more time.

Ishikawa presented the closing summary to the district chief: the east-boundary camera work order, the unresolved visitor-log mark, the G2 cabinet code, the restricted count fragments, the R-12 placement trail, and Hara’s interview. The evidence had clarified some records and complicated others. It had not established the fire’s cause, the residents’ outcomes, the identity behind the abraded name, or the source of Kreutz’s recognition.

The chief asked whether Ishikawa had enough to expand the inquiry.

“Enough to preserve the next records and test the leads,” Ishikawa said. “Not enough to state a conclusion.”

The district chief approved a second, narrower review period. It would focus on the missing Aster transfer sheet, the pooled grant’s subcode, the original camera repair file, and any independent documentation the former clerk might provide. No new search powers were granted. Any broader request would require separate grounds.

The extension included three conditions. Ishikawa would report what had been requested and what had been received; the privacy officer would review every new resident-related record; and no one would expand into a general inquiry about all former Lantern House children without new authority. The chief also ordered a review date at the end of the month, so the extension would not become indefinite by habit.

Mori asked whether the narrow scope risked missing a connection between the gate, the counts, and the grant. Ishikawa said the files could be cross-referenced, but the authority still had to match each request. “A shared code is a lead,” he said. “It isn’t permission to search everything that carries it.” The chief included that sentence in the minutes.

Ishikawa assigned Mori to the camera and facilities records, the archive specialist to the transfer files, and the evidence clerk to the resident-register access review. Wataru would remain an outside adviser on record provenance. Nia would continue leading the public chronology under her own terms, with no access to the police team’s protected files.

The chief asked why the public chronology needed a coordinator if the police had a records specialist.

“Because the police file is not the public record,” Ishikawa said. “And because the person making the public claims should be able to correct them.”

The chief signed the extension. The inquiry’s authority remained limited. The time line was another month, not an open mandate.

Nia learned about the extension through the public briefing, not a private police message. She updated the chronology’s status to say the inquiry had requested further preservation and review. She did not list the internal assignments or imply that she had directed them.

At the oversight board’s meeting, she proposed a handover standard for the next phase: every public statement would cite a source, identify who had verified it, and carry a revision date. If the source were private or restricted, the public version would describe the limit without copying protected content. The board approved the standard.

The board asked what would happen if Nia left the role before the month ended. She said the source table should be usable by someone else without requiring access to her private notes. Endo suggested a shared methods page with examples of how to label testimony, copied records, and derivative summaries. Nia accepted, provided the page contained no resident names and did not reproduce restricted images.

Two volunteer reviewers agreed to test the draft. One was a retired municipal clerk; the other was a graduate student in public history with no connection to Aster. They each marked three claims without being told Nia’s interpretation. Their disagreements revealed two phrases that could be read as conclusions. Nia changed them and added the reviewers’ notes to the version history. The board kept her chair, but the method no longer depended on her judgment alone.

The retired clerk suggested a blind exercise: give each reviewer the claim and the cited source without Nia’s confidence label, then compare what the document actually supports. The graduate student proposed keeping the confidence label but showing the exact wording that prompted it. The group combined the ideas. Their trial table had four fields: claim, source text, independent verifier, and unresolved alternative. No score implied truth; it showed where reviewers disagreed.

Nia chaired the discussion and did not cast the deciding vote on her own claims. The board’s secretary recorded that safeguard. She would remain responsible for organizing the work, while the source judgments could be challenged by people who had not followed the case from the beginning.

Hoshina asked whether Nia would continue to be the public face of the file. Nia said she would chair the chronology group for the next month and then review the role. “I don’t want the work to depend on my name,” she said. “If someone else can verify the table, they should be able to take over.”

The answer made the board’s minutes. It also made the next meeting easier to plan: Endo would train two additional student observers, both over the age required for the archive’s youth program, in source labeling and privacy handling. Nia would not be asked to supervise them alone.

On the last evening of the period, the archive posted the updated chronology. The R-12 code was described as a shared program category. The sender field remained unreadable. The failed image request remained unassigned. Hara’s notebook interval remained separate from his medical cause. The 31 totals remained unmerged. Kreutz’s recognition remained a claim.

Nia checked each line against the release copy. She found one old caption still calling the lower room “the Lantern House cellar.” She changed it to “the lower corridor beyond the east boundary,” then logged the correction and notified the board.

The update was public before midnight. No one could say the room lay under Aster’s classrooms or that it had housed residents. The smaller description was less vivid. It was the one the survey supported.

Ishikawa arrived at the archive just before closing. He left a sealed notice with Endo, addressed to the records group, not to Nia personally. The notice said the district office had received a new submission from a source claiming to possess an original placement receipt. The submission had not been authenticated. The sender requested a meeting under the condition that no press attend.

The notice contained a submission number and the channel through which the item had arrived: a sealed envelope delivered to the public records desk, then transferred unopened to the district office. The intake clerk had recorded the time and the name of the courier service, but the sender field was blank. The source requested a meeting without press and asked that the receipt not be copied before its authenticity was assessed.

Ishikawa would not confirm that the item was original. He had seen only the intake record, not the envelope’s contents. The district office had placed it in temporary evidence storage and would ask the archive conservator to review the packaging before opening. The source had supplied a callback code, not a person’s name. The code had not yet been tested.

Nia asked whether her public group was expected to attend. Endo said no. The submission belonged to the police inquiry until Ishikawa determined its relevance and the source agreed to a handling procedure. Nia asked that the public chronology state only that an unverified claim had been received, without describing the supposed receipt as a document already in evidence. Ishikawa agreed.

The extension had begun with four questions. The envelope added a fifth, but not yet an answer.

Nia read the notice and asked whether the source had provided a name.

“Not in the public notice,” Endo said. “Ishikawa is checking the channel.”

“Then it stays a claim until the record is seen.”

Endo nodded and locked the notice in the restricted cabinet.

The extension gave Ishikawa another month. The public chronology had its own rules. The next record, if it existed, would have to survive both.
