*Chapters 151 to 155 (Part 1 of Volume 7)*

## Arc 4 Recap: The Collapse

Aster’s records now connect a prepared youth placement route to a school contact typed “R. Aizawa,” but no surviving source identifies the typist, the donor, or the intended recipient. Nia has chosen to continue as a supervised public-chronology coordinator, keeping her placement file separate from the police inquiry. Ishikawa’s authority remains narrow: he may preserve and test specified records, not treat a repeated code as permission to search everything. Lantern House’s counts, the residents’ outcomes, the fire, the visitor-log mark, and the abraded registry name remain unresolved. Kreutz claims to recognize the erased name but has disclosed none, and no comparison authenticates his memory. Hara’s collapse followed Ren’s observed notebook writing in the recorded time range, but no character has confirmed a cause. A purported transfer receipt has arrived anonymously. The sender and the document’s custody are unknown; the office has not authenticated it.

The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event follows within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the writer’s own handwriting and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not state how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows.

# Chapter 151: The Delivery

---

The district office did not call the anonymous submission a placement receipt.

That wording had come from the sender, through the callback code written on the outer envelope. The office had repeated it in the first intake notice before anyone had seen the contents. Ishikawa corrected the public briefing the next morning. The item remained an unverified submission, and no one could yet say whether it concerned a placement, a transfer, or something unrelated.

The source’s condition against copying was not a legal restriction, but the district chief honored it for the first inspection. The conservator photographed the envelope’s exterior, logged the seal, and opened it in the presence of Ishikawa, the archive specialist, a privacy officer, and the evidence clerk. Hoshina observed from behind a marked line. Nia was not invited; the item belonged to the police inquiry, not the public chronology group.

Inside was one folded sheet and no cover note. The paper was thin, ruled, and carbon-marked on its reverse. At the top, a preprinted heading read “Transfer Receipt.” Below it, an entry in blue-black ink listed one sealed packet, routing code R-12/04, sent from the regional records workshop to Aster Institute. The contact field read “R. Aizawa.” A second field recorded “placement materials.”

The last line was a receipt acknowledgment. It bore a date and two initials. Neither was immediately legible. There was no name of a student, no donor, and no list of the packet’s contents. A box for “recipient’s signature” remained blank.

The receipt did not prove that Nia had been chosen before she solved the clock exercise. It did show, if the document was genuine, that an item described as placement materials had been routed to a school contact bearing the same typed name in the earlier Aster file.

“Can you date the paper?” Hoshina asked.

“Not from a visual examination,” the conservator said. “The stock is consistent with forms used by the institute, but it was commercially available. The carbon marks tell us the sheet was pressed against another page when the writing was made. They do not tell us when.”

Ishikawa asked whether the initials could be read under magnification. One looked like a short vertical mark. The other might be an M, or a fold crossing the line. The conservator refused to expand the image in a way that would make the guess appear more certain.

The evidence clerk assigned the sheet a provisional exhibit number and sealed it in a transparent sleeve. The chain-of-custody record began at the public records desk. Nothing on the outer envelope named the source. The courier could confirm delivery but not identify who had handed the package to the courier.

The district chief asked whether the restriction against copying had been honored. The clerk said no image had been made before the source’s condition was read, and no reproduction would be distributed beyond the authorized examination. The conservator’s photographs were preservation images, not copies for investigators to annotate. A checksum and access record would be entered before any later viewing. The source’s request could not govern police procedure indefinitely, but the chief wanted the initial handling to be transparent.

The privacy officer checked the sheet for names unrelated to the inquiry. None appeared, though the initials might identify staff. The office masked those marks in the public summary while preserving them on the original. The distinction mattered: the police could examine the exhibit under authority, but the public chronology could not publish a private identifier merely because it had arrived in an anonymous envelope.

At the afternoon briefing, Ishikawa chose his words carefully. “The submitted sheet is a transfer receipt that appears to reference an R-12 packet and the school contact field. Its date, authorship, and custody before delivery have not been authenticated. It does not name a placement recipient.”

The district chief asked if the office would test the callback code.

“We’ll use the code once, with the source’s condition explained,” Ishikawa said. “We will not promise secrecy beyond what the law allows, and we will not negotiate for the document’s contents.”

The clerk called the number associated with the code from a recorded line. A neutral voice answered. It did not give a name. It said the receipt had been kept with other papers, that the person did not know whether it was the original, and that the term “placement receipt” had been copied from a note found beside it. The voice said it would consider a supervised interview if no press attended.

Ishikawa asked whether the note could be preserved and delivered through counsel.

The caller said no. The note was not in their possession now. They ended the call before the clerk could ask when it had last been seen.

The recorded exchange changed no field on the receipt. It did change the public description. The sender’s label could not be treated as the document’s title or as proof of what it acknowledged. The office updated the notice: “A purported transfer receipt has been submitted; the submitter’s description of its source is incomplete.”

At the archive, Nia learned about the correction from the public briefing. Endo asked whether she wanted to suspend the chronology until the police could verify the sheet.

She and the volunteer reviewers separated what the notice said from what the page might mean. One reviewer circled “placement materials” and asked whether it described an attachment or a category. Another pointed out that the sheet itself listed a sealed packet, not the packet’s contents. Nia added both readings to the private review notes and asked Endo to keep them out of the public findings until the custodian could identify the form’s fields.

Endo told her the press had already requested the image. Nia said she would not approve access to a police exhibit she had never seen. The board could publish the released transcript and explain the limits. It could not make a second, unofficial evidence room for reporters. Endo agreed to reply through the same records-request channel used for other materials.

“No,” Nia said. “We can add the receipt as a submission and state what it does not show.”

She did not ask to see the image. The public notice contained the visible fields, with the uncertain initials and protected routing detail omitted. She added a linked question: whether an independent copy of the transfer record existed in the institute’s own files.

The first version of the story had called it a receipt for Nia’s placement. The corrected version did not. It called it a sheet that named a route and a school contact, with no student named at all.

The difference was small enough to fit in one line. By evening, it had changed the headline twice.

By midnight, the first headline remained searchable in several feeds. The district office could correct its own briefing, but it could not erase screenshots made by others. Nia asked the archive to preserve the first version of its notice as well as the correction, with timestamps. If the public record showed only the corrected phrase, it would hide how the claim had entered circulation. The archive logged both versions, and the chronology linked them without repeating the misleading headline.

# Chapter 152: The Duplicate

---

The institute’s archive found a second record with the same serial number.

It was not another loose receipt. It was an entry in a bound dispatch ledger kept by the regional records workshop, the office named at the top of the submitted sheet. The ledger had been cataloged during an unrelated retention review. Its pages had not been examined in connection with the R-12 inquiry before Ishikawa submitted a narrow request for the single date and serial.

The archive custodian scanned the page in the presence of two staff members and sent a certified copy to the district office. The ledger listed the same date, the same serial number, and a packet sent to Aster Institute. The destination field read “student contact: R. Aizawa.” A note beside it said “placement materials; contents sealed.” The initials in the sender field were different from those on the submitted receipt, but the ledger’s clerk could not identify the writer.

The record independently confirmed that the institute had logged a packet under that number for that destination. It did not prove the loose sheet was the original receipt. It did not prove what the packet contained. And it did not establish who had typed the contact field or selected the person who would receive the placement.

The serial sequence also gave the archive a way to test whether the ledger page had been clipped into the wrong section. The custodian checked the prior and following entries, the book’s index, and the binding. The serial belonged in that week’s dispatch sequence. No gap or replacement leaf was visible around the page, though the examination could not establish who had written the entry or whether the ledger had been altered before its cataloging.

Ishikawa asked for a certification that described only the custodian’s actions and observations. The institute provided it. The archive specialist compared the certified scan with the district office’s image of the loose receipt. The type and ruling were consistent with the same series of forms, but the scan quality and paper wear prevented a source-level match. The two documents agreed on a transfer record, not on the physical identity of the submitted sheet.

Ishikawa asked the institute to preserve the original ledger and any related catalog cards. The custodian confirmed the preservation hold in writing. No broader search was authorized. The request concerned one transfer and the records directly connected to it.

Nia received the certified public summary through the archive’s ordinary release process. The names of the clerks and the unredacted ledger page stayed restricted. She compared the summary to the earlier Aster contact record. Both carried R. Aizawa, but the wording differed: one typed contact field, one dispatch destination. She marked them as separate sources that converged on a route, not as two signatures by one person.

She opened the version history before changing a word. The first summary had used “packet addressed to R. Aizawa.” The certified copy showed that the institute ledger called the destination a student contact and the loose sheet called it a school contact. Neither used the word “addressed.” Nia replaced the phrase and left a note identifying the change. The reviewer who had approved the earlier table checked the underlying public copy and agreed that the correction was necessary.

The archive did not label the initial wording a lie. It had been a reasonable but unsupported inference from the form’s layout. The version note explained that a field positioned under “destination” could indicate routing without proving personal receipt. Readers could see both the original phrasing and the reason it had changed.

Sagara answered Ishikawa’s request in a short written statement. He remembered receiving exercise materials from a colleague at the institute, but not the envelope or the date. The colleague had retired. Sagara’s old email confirmed that he had been asked to supervise a public-source exercise and that the placement would depend on a student identifying a clock reset. It did not say who designed the exercise. He had recommended Nia after she solved it.

The email provided a second limit and a useful point of comparison. Sagara had not selected her before she completed the exercise, according to his account and the surviving correspondence. The packet had been routed to a contact at Aster before her application. Those statements could both be true: someone could prepare a route without deciding in advance who would occupy it.

At the public archive, the oversight group debated whether to call this “a link to Ren.” Nia said the source used his school identity but did not name Ren personally. The group settled on: “An institute dispatch ledger records a placement-materials packet sent to an Aster contact typed as R. Aizawa. The record does not identify the typist, the recipient’s actions, or the student intended to benefit.”

A volunteer reviewer objected that the last clause sounded too cautious. Nia showed the group the actual fields. The ledger had no student name, signature, or candidate number. The reviewer withdrew the objection but asked that the source of the phrase “placement materials” be specified. Nia added that the phrase appeared in the ledger itself.

The correction did not satisfy the people who had already decided what the document meant. A campus account posted a photograph of the public summary with the caption “Ren ordered Nia’s life years ago.” It omitted the sentence about the unidentified typist and recipient.

A second reporter asked Nia whether she was protecting the school because her placement depended on it. She replied that the archive had published the same source limits it applied to every submission, and that she would leave the role if the school made it conditional on a particular conclusion. The reporter asked if that was a threat to resign. Nia said it was a description of her consent form. Endo ended the interview when the questions turned from the records to Nia’s private file.

The board’s chair later asked whether the exchange should be entered as an incident. Nia said it was a press interaction, not a safety event. The chair accepted the distinction and asked Endo to retain the recording under the board’s ordinary media policy. No special security report was opened.

The chair asked whether the press interaction had changed Nia’s mind about continuing. Nia said it had changed what she wanted from the board: every invitation should state the topic, audience, and recording plan before she accepted. She would not be expected to answer questions about her family, her placement consent, or private contact with Ren. The chair added the conditions to the next meeting’s public protocol. Nia signed the attendance sheet only after the revised version was circulated.

Nia asked the board to keep the public briefing recording in its regular retention schedule. If the quotation was challenged later, the full recording could establish what she had actually said. The privacy officer agreed to store it with the minutes and to publish only the excerpt requested under the ordinary access rules. The board did not create a special file about the reporter or Nia’s placement.

The archive did not remove the post. It placed a link to the complete source note beneath its own public copy and submitted a correction request to the account. The account refused. Hoshina’s article carried the full qualification, but the shorter claim spread faster.

Hoshina asked the account administrator for a correction using the certified summary and the source note. The administrator replied that the post summarized the broader pattern rather than one ledger. Nia did not argue about motive. She asked that the board’s response link to the exact record and state the difference between a routed packet and a named recipient. The board posted the response; it did not ask the platform to remove the account or suppress commentary.

The response triggered a second request from a local paper: could Nia provide the chronology group’s internal notes to show how it had reached its wording? Endo said the notes were working documents and might include personal information from submitters. The paper could file a formal request for any releasable record. Nia supported that answer. She asked the board to preserve the notes under its ordinary schedule, not to destroy them and not to make them public as a gesture of confidence. The distinction was entered in the meeting minutes.

At the district office, the chief asked whether the receipt justified a search of Ren’s school materials.

“It justifies preserving the institutional route records,” Ishikawa said. “It doesn’t justify seizing a student’s private property.”

The chief authorized a request for the school’s remaining R-12 routing slips, preservation notices, and mail logs. Counsel would review the scope before it was served. The order did not include Ren’s notebook, personal devices, or private correspondence.

That afternoon, Aster’s records custodian confirmed that the old central mail log had been moved to off-site storage before the preservation notice arrived. The custodian could not yet say whether it survived. The school would report back after the transfer inventory was checked.

The receipt had found a duplicate. The duplicate had found a missing log. Neither had found a student’s name.

# Chapter 153: The Public Claim

---

Nia asked the oversight board to publish the correction before the school meeting.

The public claim about Ren had outrun the certified summary. A student paper called the R-12 packet “proof of a planned target.” A national account repeated that headline without linking the archive. The district office had made no finding about authorship, and the ledger named no candidate. Nia wanted the archive’s exact qualification visible wherever the receipt was discussed.

The correction did not answer every question from the room. One former staff member asked whether “sealed” referred to the packet or to the receipt. Nia checked the certified summary and explained that the dispatch ledger described a sealed packet; the loose receipt was folded and sent in an envelope, but its earlier seal history was unknown. The staff member thanked her and said he had been repeating the shorter headline. The archive added the distinction to its frequently asked questions, without naming him.

The board’s chair worried that a correction would give the rumor another day of attention. Nia replied that leaving the claim uncorrected would make silence look like agreement. The board approved a short statement, then asked Nia to read it at the afternoon briefing. She agreed, with Endo beside her and a right to stop if questions moved beyond the public record.

She read the text exactly: the institute’s ledger supported a packet transfer to an Aster contact typed R. Aizawa; the author, intended recipient, and contents remained unverified. The transfer alone did not establish that Ren had designed Nia’s exercise or selected her in advance.

Before the briefing, Endo asked Nia to review the room layout and the exit she would use. Nia asked why the board had announced her attendance before confirming whether she wanted to speak. The chair apologized; a staff member had assumed her role meant she was available. Nia told the board she would still read the statement, but requested that future invitations be cleared with her first. The secretary amended the meeting procedure on the spot.

She also asked whether the statement could be read by another board member if she chose to stop. The chair said yes. The text belonged to the archive, not to her. Nia kept a copy in front of her and placed the source note beside it, so she could point to the exact language if the questions drifted toward what she personally believed.

A reporter asked whether Nia believed the route had been built for her.

“I know the route was prepared before I applied,” she said. “I don’t know whether I was chosen before I solved the exercise. Those are different claims.”

“Do you think Ren sent the packet?”

“The records don’t identify who sent it.”

“Could the contact be someone else?”

“It could be a person or an administrative label. The file does not include a key.”

Hoshina asked whether Nia planned to continue leading the chronology if the attention became personal. Nia said her role was renewable and she would review it at the end of the month, as agreed. Endo reminded the room that Nia was a student observer, not a police source. The board’s statement included the boundary in writing.

Afterward, an adult reporter approached Nia without the camera crew. He asked if she would share her private placement file “just to settle the story.” Endo stepped between them and told him to submit a formal request through the archive. Nia did not answer. The incident was logged as an unapproved approach, not a threat.

The school counselor offered to suspend Nia’s public appearances. She declined the blanket restriction but accepted that Endo would attend every meeting and that no interview would be arranged during school hours. She asked to keep her regular classes and the evening archive escort. The counselor made those arrangements without describing them as a security response.

At Aster, the headmistress held a closed meeting with counsel and the records custodian. Ren attended as a student whose school identity appeared in the transfer summary, not as a suspect or witness. Ishikawa joined by invitation to explain the preservation request. He did not ask Ren questions about the receipt.

The custodian confirmed that the central mail log was still unlocated. A transfer box index showed that the log might have been moved to a storage room during renovation, but no item-level checklist survived. The school could request the box from the storage contractor. Counsel asked for a written scope so other students’ records would not be swept into the search.

Ishikawa agreed. He asked only for the R-12 entries, their surrounding pages, and the box’s movement record. The surrounding pages mattered because a single row without its headings or neighboring entries could be misread. The school would not copy unrelated student names into the police file.

Ren looked at the draft request. “The surrounding pages may contain names.”

“They will be reviewed by the custodian first,” Ishikawa said. “The police request is for the relevant entry and enough context to understand it.”

“Will the custodian redact before making the preservation copy?”

“No. The original will be preserved as found. A separate working copy can be redacted after the source is logged.”

The headmistress added that distinction to the minutes. The meeting ended with a two-day deadline for the storage contractor to confirm the box’s location. Ren signed the attendance sheet but did not sign the school’s response.

Outside the conference room, Hoshina asked him for a comment. Ren said the archive’s statement was accurate: the ledger identified a contact field, not an author. He did not speculate about who had prepared the packet. Hoshina wrote down the sentence and asked if he wanted to add anything.

“No,” he said.

The public claim remained larger than the record. The official correction remained smaller. Aster had two days to find the log that might explain which one had come first.

# Chapter 154: The Mail Log

---

The storage contractor found the box before the school board’s deadline.

It was in a climate-controlled facility three towns away, filed under the renovation project rather than the registrar’s office. The box seal was intact and its transfer label matched the school’s movement index. The contractor photographed it at the shelf, then handed custody to the records custodian and a second school employee. Ishikawa observed the opening under the preservation order; he did not touch the contents.

The box held meeting minutes, blank forms, and three bound mail books. One was marked with the year of the R-12 transfer. Its cover had water damage, but the binding remained intact. The custodian located the index page and photographed it before turning to the listed sequence.

Before opening the volume, the custodian read out the authority and the serial range. The preservation order permitted imaging and custody documentation, not a free search through every year in storage. Ishikawa verified that the cover label matched the year and that the index referred to the correct book. The custodian turned the pages while the second employee logged each movement. When the damaged cover began to flex, the conservator adjusted the support cradle and the examination paused until the binding rested flat without pressure.

The entry matching the institute’s serial number was present. It described one incoming packet from the regional workshop, with an attachment marked “placement materials.” The recipient column was not filled in. In the margin, someone had typed “route via student contact.” No name appeared on the surviving line.

The book had an index in the back, written in the same hand as several other headings but not necessarily by the person who entered the individual dispatches. The index listed the serial and page number. It did not name a receiver. The custodian photographed the index, the full ledger spread, and the cover, then recorded which surfaces were visible and which remained obscured by the damaged binding. The archive did not flatten the book to obtain a better image.

A records analyst checked the serial against the institute’s published retention schedule. The series was supposed to be kept permanently, but the schedule did not certify that every volume had survived. This one had. The analyst made no claim that the loose receipt had been kept in the same file or that its anonymous sender had taken it from the workshop.

The entry confirmed that Aster had logged a packet under the same serial and date as the institute’s dispatch ledger. It did not establish who inside the school handled it, whether it reached Ren, or what it contained. The school’s record and the institute’s book corroborated that a transfer was recorded at both ends. They did not provide an unbroken custody chain for the submitted loose receipt.

The evidence clerk sealed the original mail book in its box after imaging the relevant page and adjacent context. The copy showed three neighboring entries for unrelated programs. Their names were masked in the public version, but the original remained intact. The custodian signed the seal log and retained the key.

The board’s counsel asked whether the margin note could have been added later. The conservator said the type was printed on the page, but could not date the impression. The mail book’s original ink entry and its later typed note were distinguishable. The typewriter used by the school had not been preserved. No comparison could identify the person who made the note.

One of the neighboring entries also used the phrase “student contact.” The records office had not treated the phrase as a person’s name in either case. That did not make the R. Aizawa field meaningless; it made its function less certain. The school would search only for a field key or policy that explained how contacts were assigned.

Nia received the public summary that afternoon. She revised the chronology to say two institutional records independently logged the same transfer. She added that the Aster recipient field was blank and that the accompanying route note did not identify a student. The loose receipt’s authenticity remained unproven even though the transaction itself now had support in both ledgers.

A public comment accused her of “walking back the evidence.” Nia added a version note: “Additional records corroborate dispatch and receipt of a packet; they do not resolve authorship, contents, or intended beneficiary.” She did not remove the earlier entry or pretend the new limit had been known before.

At the police office, Ishikawa compared the mail book with the grant addendum already in the file. The dates were close but not identical. The grant addendum authorized a conditional placement; the mail entry recorded a packet transfer. No document proved that the packet contained the addendum. The serial number connected the entries, but the grant page carried a different subcode.

The chief asked if they could now say Ren had received the packet.

“No,” Ishikawa said. “We can say the school recorded a route through a student contact. The recipient is blank.”

“Can we ask the school who that contact was?”

“We can ask whether a key or routing policy survives. We cannot make a blank field into a name by asking harder.”

The chief approved a targeted request to the registrar’s former staff for any key, desk manual, or duplicate routing slip from the same period. The request would not ask them to identify a student from memory. If someone offered a recollection, it would be recorded as a recollection and checked against documents.

The school board released a short notice about the mail log. It did not mention Ren by name. A reporter asked whether that was because the school was protecting him. The headmistress said the school had released the fields the records supported and withheld unrelated student information. She added that no finding had been made about the intended recipient.

By the end of the day, the two copies agreed on a packet. The margin note still pointed toward a student route, but not toward a particular student. The public case had gained a verified transaction and lost its simplest explanation.

# Chapter 155: The Notice

---

The district chief sent Ren a notice before the school could issue its response.

It was not an arrest notice or a finding of wrongdoing. It told him that records bearing his school identity had been preserved under a narrow inquiry and that he could consult counsel before any voluntary interview. The letter listed the institute dispatch entry, the Aster mail-book entry, and the typed R. Aizawa contact from the earlier transfer file. It stated plainly that the records did not prove he had received or authored the packet.

The chief asked Ishikawa why the notice named Ren if the school’s recipient column was blank.

“Because the institute ledger has a typed contact field using his school identity,” Ishikawa said. “He should know the records are being reviewed. It does not make him a suspect by itself.”

The notice went through the headmistress, who gave it to Ren in her office with the school counselor present. Ren read each page, then asked for a copy of the preservation scope. The counselor offered to pause the meeting. Ren said he understood the offer and chose to finish reading before responding.

The counselor explained that Ren could read the notice alone, with counsel, or with a staff member present. Ren chose the headmistress and the counselor, but asked the counselor not to take notes on his expressions or pauses. The counselor agreed; the school’s written record would describe only the documents handed over and the questions Ren asked. It would not speculate about his reaction.

When he asked for the preservation scope, the headmistress explained that it covered institutional materials and not his private notebook. Ren read the exception twice, then asked whether the school had preserved the original mail book before giving the police a copy. The headmistress said yes. He asked for that assurance to be included in the receipt acknowledging delivery of the notice. She added it, and Ren signed for the notice only.

He did not sign an interview consent. He did not refuse one either. He asked that any future request identify whether it concerned his school records, his personal property, or his recollection. The headmistress recorded the request and told him he could take the notice to counsel.

That afternoon, the school’s former registrar replied to Ishikawa’s written inquiry. She remembered that student-contact fields were used when outside offices sent sealed packets to a faculty or student liaison. She could not recall who assigned the code, and she did not recognize the R-12/04 serial. Her desk manuals had been discarded during the renovation. She offered no identification of the intended recipient.

The evidence clerk recorded the statement as a memory given years after the event. The former registrar agreed to sign it after checking that no exact quotation had been attributed to her beyond the notes she had reviewed. Her account supported the function of the field in general; it did not explain this packet in particular.

Nia updated the public chronology from the released summary. The independent ledgers confirmed the packet’s route from the workshop into Aster’s records. The school’s former registrar described how “student contact” fields could be used. No record showed the packet reaching Ren. No source identified who designed the exercise or named Nia as its intended recipient.

The archive reviewers disagreed about whether the public table should include Ren’s school identity. One said the contact was already public. Another worried that repeating it would turn a routing label into an accusation. Nia asked them to keep the entry but add the source limitation beside it, not several paragraphs later. The board approved the change.

At the next school board meeting, the press waited outside. The headmistress announced that the school had preserved the mail books, released a redacted summary, and referred all additional questions about individual students to counsel. She would not say whether Ren had accepted an interview. The answer was no one else’s to disclose.

The retired registrar’s account was reviewed by a second records officer before it was added to the file. The officer checked the phrase “student contact” against the surviving manual index, which listed a section with that title but no procedure pages. This supported the former registrar’s description of a general practice without corroborating her memory of the specific transfer.

Ishikawa sent her the summary for correction, not approval of the inquiry’s conclusions. She changed one phrase from “student liaison received” to “a student contact was listed.” The alteration reduced certainty and was initialed by both the former registrar and the reviewing officer. It did not identify who collected any packet.

The retired clerk’s offer to meet with Ishikawa was scheduled for the following week. She asked that her daughter attend and that no reporter be present. Ishikawa agreed, but explained that her statement might be preserved and could be disclosed if required by law. She asked for the scope in writing before confirming. The district office sent it: questions about the mail-book procedure, the student-contact field, and the record’s retention. The interview would not ask her to guess who had collected the packet or to identify a student from memory.

A reporter asked Nia whether the public record now proved Ren had arranged her placement.

“No,” she said. “It proves that an R-12 packet was routed through an Aster student-contact field before my application. The field contains a name matching Ren’s school identity in one document. No record identifies the person who typed it or establishes who took delivery.”

“Do you still believe your path was prepared?”

“The route was prepared. I’m still separating that from whether I was the person it was prepared for.”

Ren stood at the other end of the hall with the notice folded inside a clear folder. He was not scheduled to speak. When another reporter asked him if he had received the packet, the headmistress told the reporter the school had not authorized an interview. Ren did not answer over her.

The board approved a further preservation request for the remaining regional routing records. The chief set a review date ten days away. Until then, no one could access the stored materials outside the custodian and the records officer. The two institutional books would remain sealed between supervised reviews.

As the meeting ended, the district office received a message from the institute’s retired dispatch clerk. She had found a personal calendar from that year and believed it might identify who collected the packet at Aster. She would not send a photograph or discuss it by phone. She asked to meet Ishikawa, without press, and with her daughter present.

The message did not identify a collector. It offered a possible witness, years after the transfer, with a private record not yet examined.

Ishikawa added the request to the inquiry log and made no public claim. Nia left the chronology unchanged except for one line: “A former clerk has offered an unreviewed personal record; its contents and relevance are unknown.”

The next name might be in the calendar.

The public chronology’s status line remained unchanged until the clerk’s meeting took place. Nia added a reminder to the internal review schedule, not a finding: “Potential personal record offered; inspect only with owner consent and documented scope.” Endo countersigned the note. The calendar had not been received, and no one could yet say whether it concerned the packet, the school, or a different appointment altogether.

Or the calendar might only contain another date.
