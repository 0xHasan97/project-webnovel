*Chapters 156 to 160 (Part 2 of Volume 7)*

**Current-State Recap: The Collapse**

The regional workshop dispatch ledger and Aster’s bound mail records independently establish that an R-12 packet reached the school’s student-contact routing process. The counterfoil identifies the assigned contact as R. Aizawa, matching Ren’s school identity, but does not prove who wrote the entry or whether he collected, read, or authored the packet. An anonymous loose receipt remains unverified despite agreeing with several institutional fields. Ren says he cannot place the packet and does not speculate about its author. Nia remains a supervised public-chronology coordinator by choice, not a police investigator. A newly located workshop master shows the three-clock exercise was prepared as a conditional placement tool before Nia applied, and that its student version excluded a sealed witness statement. The master names no candidate or author. The archive’s public case records what each source establishes—and what it does not. The separate Lantern House, Hara, and erased-name mysteries remain unresolved.

The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event follows within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the writer’s own handwriting and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not state how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows.

# Chapter 156: The Calendar

---

The retired dispatch clerk arrived with a daughter on either side of her.

She had asked for both to be present: one to help her remember the route from the records workshop to Aster, the other to witness what happened to her calendar. Ishikawa met them in a public archive room, not at the station. The district chief had approved a voluntary interview with a written scope. No one would ask the clerk to identify a student from memory.

The calendar was a pocket diary with a cracked green cover. Its pages were soft at the corners. The clerk said she had kept it in a desk drawer after retiring, then moved it to a kitchen cabinet. Her daughter confirmed the cabinet and said she had seen the diary there before the police inquiry began. Neither could establish that no one had opened it in the intervening years.

The conservator photographed the cover, binding, and the week of the R-12 dispatch. A small note sat beside the date listed in the institute ledger: “Aster — 3:40 / R.A. — packet.” The handwriting appeared consistent with the clerk’s other calendar entries, but the conservator would not date the ink or call the notation contemporaneous on appearance alone.

The clerk read it twice. “I wrote that I had an appointment. I can’t tell you who I met.”

“Do you remember going to Aster?” Ishikawa asked.

“I remember the front office. I remember bringing an envelope. I do not remember the person who took it.”

Her daughter asked if the initials meant Ren Aizawa.

“They could,” the clerk said. “They could also be the office or a room. I used initials when the page was crowded.”

The clerk signed a statement describing only what she remembered. She would permit the calendar page to be imaged for the inquiry, but not circulated publicly until she reviewed the privacy redactions. Her daughter signed as witness to the inspection, not as a witness to the old delivery.

The conservator found a separate appointment card tucked inside the back cover. It carried the same date, a workshop address, and a time slot at Aster’s front office. The card’s serial matched an entry in the institute’s appointment ledger, which had been preserved in a different collection. The appointment ledger described a “sealed R-12 packet” and “student contact.” It did not include the initials from the calendar.

The card had been folded into the diary’s rear pocket. The daughter said she had never noticed it and had not removed the pages. The clerk said the pocket might have been used for loose reminders, but she could not place when she put the card there. The conservator photographed it as found, then lifted it with a paper support. Its fold crossed the address, leaving one line partly unreadable.

The institute archive checked its appointment ledger without opening any other personal calendars. It found an entry for the same day and time, but the ledger’s visitor field was blank. The description “R-12 sealed packet” had been typed in the purpose column. A clerk had initialed the entry in the margin. That record supported a scheduled visit and a packet expected at the workshop office; it did not establish that the retired clerk made the visit or that the packet left the office with her.

The independent ledger made the appointment real. It did not prove that the clerk’s note had been written that week or that the packet had reached a student.

Ishikawa asked the clerk whether she remembered the envelope’s appearance.

“Brown paper. String, maybe. I carried several like that.”

“Did you take it back with you?”

“I don’t think so.”

“Could you say you left it?”

“No. Not from memory.”

Her daughter touched the edge of the diary but did not turn another page. “Do you want to stop?”

The clerk said she wanted to finish the interview. The recorder was paused while the archive specialist checked the next question against the agreed scope. The pause and its reason were noted in the custody log.

The clerk then offered to let the conservator examine the page under transmitted light. The pressure marks showed a short line below the appointment note, but the words could not be read. No one pressed the page flat. The image was added to the preservation set, not treated as a recovered signature.

Nia received the public summary two days later. It said a former workshop clerk recalled bringing an envelope to Aster but could not identify a recipient. Her calendar contained an ambiguous “R.A.” notation; a separate appointment ledger corroborated a scheduled visit and a sealed R-12 packet. The archive did not release the calendar image.

Nia wrote “delivery recalled, recipient not remembered” in the public table. One reviewer proposed “Ren’s packet.” Nia rejected it. The existing sources gave them a visit, a packet, and an ambiguous initial. They did not supply a person at the handoff.

At the district office, Ishikawa added the appointment ledger to the evidence list and left the calendar under the clerk’s custody. The old note had not identified a recipient. It had narrowed the time and made the question harder to avoid.

# Chapter 157: The Desk Register

---

A school storage index listed a second box for the year of the R-12 transfer.

It was not in the box containing the bound mail book. The index called it “temporary desk routing,” without identifying the department. The headmistress authorized a targeted retrieval under the existing preservation notice. The school custodian and counsel opened the box first, recorded its seal, and screened the contents for unrelated student information. Ishikawa observed; he did not select pages himself.

Inside was a thin counterfoil book and a stack of blank delivery slips. The counterfoil book had a heading: “Internal handoff—student contact.” A key on the facing page explained that the field recorded the person assigned to carry a sealed packet from the office to its destination. The contact was not necessarily the addressee or the placement candidate.

The entry for the R-12 serial was dated the same day as the institute dispatch. It recorded a packet received by the school registrar and forwarded to “student contact: R. Aizawa.” The delivery acknowledgment line had a single initial, then a time. The name field for the candidate remained blank.

The counterfoil book also included a daily closing mark. The staff member who closed the desk had checked that each outgoing packet had either a recipient’s mark or an unresolved-status notation. On the R-12 line, the box contained a short stroke but no explanation. The records specialist could not determine whether it meant “sent,” “returned,” or simply “reviewed.” The book’s surviving instruction page did not define the mark.

Ishikawa asked the school custodian to preserve the original counterfoil and to search the adjacent pages only for the relevant workflow key. The custodian agreed to review the surrounding entries for context and mask unrelated names. The chief approved that scope. The records did not justify asking every former student about an old packet.

The entry supported more than the earlier mail book: it placed the packet in a process assigned to a named school contact. It did not say whether the contact collected it, delivered it onward, or ever read the enclosed materials. The counterfoil did not establish who wrote the initials. The key narrowed the meaning of “contact”; it did not identify the student’s role in designing the exercise.

The custodian made a preservation image of the complete spread, including its heading, key, and neighboring entries. The working copy masked other students’ names. The unredacted book stayed sealed at the school. A second records officer verified the page numbers and the seal before Ishikawa signed the receipt.

The former registrar was invited to review the key. She did so with her daughter present and a copy of her statement in front of her. She confirmed that the phrase described a routing function. She did not remember this packet, did not recognize the initials, and could not say whether the book’s key was used consistently in every office.

Nia corrected her chronology. The first version had said the Aster field might be a department label. The counterfoil book’s key showed that, in this series, it referred to an assigned student contact. She changed the entry to say so, then added: “The surviving record does not establish whether the contact personally collected the packet or was its intended beneficiary.”

A reviewer said that distinction seemed too fine to matter.

“It is the difference between a person named in a routing field and a person who chose the route,” Nia said. “Those aren’t the same action.”

The reviewer kept the objection in the notes. The board published both the revised language and the field key’s source reference.

The board’s second reviewer tested the revised table against the source by hiding the interpretation column. Asked only to read the counterfoil key and entry, she wrote: “assigned carrier; completion not shown.” She then compared that reading with the institute ledger, which named a destination field but no individual handoff. The two reviews reached the same narrow point independently. Nia attached the method note to the chronology so readers could see how the wording had been chosen.

The school’s counsel asked whether the police now considered Ren a suspect. Ishikawa said the inquiry was testing what the school and workshop records could establish. The contact field matched Ren’s school identity, but no evidence yet showed who wrote the entry or who took possession. He would not announce a status that had not been formally determined.

The chief asked whether the counterfoil justified an interview.

“It justifies offering one with counsel and a clear scope,” Ishikawa said. “It doesn’t justify calling the answer a confession before we ask.”

The office sent Ren’s counsel a request for a voluntary interview about the R-12 routing record. The letter attached the public summary, not the protected page, and made clear that Ren could decline. It did not include questions about the notebook or the Lantern House counts.

Ren’s response arrived the next morning. He would attend with counsel. He asked the police to identify the exact documents before the interview and not to show him names from unrelated entries. Ishikawa agreed.

The field had been defined. The person attached to it had not yet explained what he did with the packet.

# Chapter 158: The Correction

---

The correction went online at noon. By one, Nia’s name was trending beside Ren’s.

The archive’s page stated that a contemporaneous counterfoil listed R. Aizawa as an assigned student contact for a sealed R-12 packet. It also stated that the record did not identify the writer, confirm personal collection, or name a placement candidate. The article that copied the first sentence omitted the qualifications.

The oversight chair called an emergency meeting. One member proposed removing Nia from the public group to protect her from pressure. Nia asked whether the proposal was about her safety or about discomfort with the attention. The chair said both concerns had been raised. Nia asked for the concrete risk the board had identified. No one had received a direct threat. The reporters had asked for interviews, and a stranger had approached her without an appointment.

“I’ll keep the escort and the interview rules,” Nia said. “I won’t let the board decide that I have to leave because the records are getting difficult.”

Her guardian supported her request. The board agreed to review the role after two weeks rather than ending it that day. Endo would attend all public appearances. Nia retained the right to cancel any meeting without losing the placement.

A student at Aster sent Nia a message through the archive intake form. The student said that Ren had once asked where the old mail room was. No date, context, or witness was provided. The student did not want a name attached. Nia did not forward the message to Ishikawa as a factual lead. She asked the archive to reply that the submission needed a date or source before it could be evaluated.

Nia showed the intake to Endo before deciding whether to answer. The message was unsigned, but the archive’s form stored a private callback address for follow-up. Nia asked Endo to make the first contact, not to forward the address to the police. Endo sent a neutral reply requesting a date, the setting, and whether the student had heard the statement directly.

The reply came from the same channel and said the question might have been asked while staff were sorting renovation boxes. The student did not remember who else was present. Nia marked it as an unverified recollection and retained it under the intake policy, but did not elevate it as evidence. She also documented that the student had been offered the option to withdraw the submission. The student chose to leave it in the archive’s private queue.

The student answered that it might have been a routine question during the renovation. The message was then marked “unverified recollection—insufficient detail” and excluded from the public chronology. Nia’s choice cost the board a dramatic new lead. It also prevented an anonymous rumor from being stacked on top of the routing record.

At the police office, Ishikawa prepared for Ren’s interview by laying out the records in chronological order. First, the workshop appointment ledger. Second, the dispatch entry. Third, the Aster mail book. Fourth, the student-contact counterfoil. The anonymous loose receipt came last, in a separate sleeve, because its prior custody was unknown.

Mori asked why the receipt had not been excluded altogether.

“Because the independent records now help us test its fields,” Ishikawa said. “They don’t repair its custody. Both facts belong in the file.”

The evidence clerk compared the serial, date, paper ruling, and entry order across the copies. The receipt matched the institution records in several fields. The clerk could not say whether it had been copied from one of the books, whether it was a contemporaneous carbon, or whether the sender had access to another source. It remained an unverified exhibit that was consistent with the records, not a source that authenticated them.

Nia’s public group added the same distinction to the methodology page. “Consistent with” could describe a match without deciding how the sources were related. A source could copy another source; two could share an origin; or one could be wrong. The reviewer asked Nia to include an example. She did, using the R-12 receipt without reproducing private details.

That evening, a reporter asked her whether she believed Ren had received the packet. Nia said the counterfoil made it plausible that he had been assigned to carry it. Whether he collected it was not established. The reporter asked whether that sounded like a retreat.

“It sounds like the sentence the document supports.”

The reporter published her full answer. A different outlet used the phrase “Nia backs away from accusation.” The board sent no correction request. It posted the underlying source note and let the contrast stand.

In the interview room, Ishikawa placed the copy of the counterfoil beside the dispatch ledger. He left the original sealed. Ren’s counsel confirmed the scope: routing, the student contact, and Ren’s recollection, with no questions about private medical or placement records.

The recorder light turned on. Ren entered with a clear folder and a slim notebook. The notebook stayed closed on the table.

Before the interview, Ishikawa read the source list into the recorder. The dispatch ledger was a certified copy; the mail book and counterfoil were school preservation images; the calendar remained with its owner; the loose receipt was still provisional. Ren’s counsel asked that no one refer to the loose sheet as the original. The clerk confirmed that the exhibit label read “anonymous submitted receipt, unverified provenance.”

Ren set the clear folder on the table, opened it, and removed the notice he had received. He had written one question in the margin: “Does contact mean delivery, or assignment?” Ishikawa said the field key answered only that the contact was assigned to carry a sealed packet. It did not document completion. Ren placed the notice beside the source copies and waited for the interview to begin.

Ishikawa began with the date.

# Chapter 159: The Interview

---

Ren did not dispute the name in the student-contact field.

He looked at the certified copy of the counterfoil and said the entry matched his school identity. He could not authenticate the handwriting or the initials. He had been a student at Aster and had signed for school materials before. The counterfoil did not include a signature that he could confidently claim as his own.

“Do you remember receiving an R-12 packet?” Ishikawa asked.

“I remember receiving documents through the school office. I cannot place this packet from the page.”

“Did you deliver it to someone?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did you read the contents?”

“I can’t say.”

His counsel asked that the questions remain tied to the documents. Ishikawa showed him the public description of the conditional placement but not Nia’s private file. Ren said he knew that Nia had been selected through a clock exercise. He had not seen the original dispatch ledger before today, according to his statement. The police could verify only that the copy had been released that morning.

Ishikawa asked whether Ren had designed the exercise.

“No comment,” counsel said.

Ren looked at the recorder. “I’ll answer the question about what I remember. I won’t speculate about who designed a document I haven’t seen.”

Ishikawa asked whether the R-12 packet had been addressed to him personally.

“The records you showed me don’t say that.”

The answer was correct. It did not answer whether he had carried the packet. Ishikawa asked whether Ren recognized the field key. Ren said he had not seen it at the time and could not say which office used that version.

The two interviewers then asked the questions in a different order. First, whether Ren remembered a sealed envelope; then whether he remembered any unusual school delivery; finally, whether the R-12 code meant anything to him at the time. His answer did not change. He recalled ordinary documents moving through the office, not this packet in particular. Counsel asked that the transcript preserve each version of the question so the repeated answer would not be condensed into a stronger denial or admission.

Ishikawa paused the interview and reviewed the scope with both counsel and Ren. The counterfoil authorized questions about a routing role, not about every contact Ren had with the placement program. Ren agreed to continue. He declined to examine a redacted neighboring entry, saying it could not help him identify his own action and might expose another student. The clerk recorded the refusal as a privacy choice, not as evidence about the packet.

The interview lasted thirty-two minutes. Ren did not ask for the audio to be stopped, and he did not ask for any answer to be removed. At the end, he read the typed summary line by line. He changed “Ren received school packets” to “Ren recalled receiving documents through the school office.” The clerk recorded the edit and had him initial it.

Outside the room, Hoshina asked Ishikawa whether Ren had refused to answer.

“He answered what he could place in memory,” Ishikawa said. “He declined to speculate about authorship. That is not the same as admitting or denying authorship.”

The summary released later that day described the interview’s limits. It did not publish a clip of Ren’s voice. The station confirmed that the records had not established personal receipt of the R-12 packet.

Before release, the clerk sent the proposed summary to both counsel. Ren’s counsel corrected the phrase “declined to identify the packet” to “could not place the packet from the page shown.” Ishikawa accepted the change because the recording supported it. The public copy included the correction history and the interview scope. It did not include the full audio, private pauses, or questions about unrelated student records. Ren received the same copy as the district office; the release did not depend on whether he agreed with the inquiry’s next steps.

Nia added the interview to the public chronology as a statement, not as corroboration. She included the exact distinction: Ren acknowledged that R. Aizawa matched his school identity, recalled receiving school documents generally, and could not place this packet. He did not authenticate the entry or identify its author.

The board’s lawyer asked if that wording made Ren appear evasive. Nia said the chronology was not a character assessment. The public response could make its own judgment from the source.

At Aster, the records custodian located the original school mail book and compared the counterfoil copy to the page in place. The entries matched. The seal remained intact after the inspection. The custody log showed no unsupervised access during the preservation period.

The district chief asked Ishikawa whether the interview had changed the inquiry.

“It narrowed the question. We know what he says he remembers. We still need a source for who entered the contact and whether the packet went beyond the office.”

The chief asked if the matter should be closed as an administrative routing issue.

“Not until we check the original transfer records and the exercise file. The repeated route is significant, but it doesn’t prove a crime.”

Ren left the station with counsel. He did not speak to the cameras waiting outside. A reporter called his silence a refusal; another described the interview as a denial. Neither account matched the transcript.

In the records room, the evidence clerk resealed the copies. The line in the counterfoil still named a student contact. The next question was who had entered the name and what instruction had accompanied the packet.

# Chapter 160: The Master Copy

---

The workshop archive found a master packet under a different catalog number.

It was not in the dispatch series. It had been preserved in a teaching collection as a “three-clock chronology exercise,” with an accession date two months before Nia’s application. The workshop custodian reported the discovery through counsel and asked the district office to submit a narrow request before any page was opened. The collection belonged to the institute, and the master included participant notes unrelated to Aster.

Ishikawa’s revised request named the title, accession number, and date range. The archive agreed to a supervised examination by a conservator and the records specialist. No pages would be removed or copied until the custodian determined how the unrelated notes could be protected. Nia received no advance access; she would wait for a public summary like any other reader.

The folder contained a printed exercise, a scoring guide, and a one-page placement addendum. The exercise presented three conflicting clocks and asked the student to identify a reset. Its source note referred to a public archive packet about a coastal-station disappearance. A revision line in the scoring guide said: “Use only the released chronology; do not include the sealed witness statement.”

The exercise was not a full police file. Its pages summarized three times from the coastal-station chronology and included an instruction to compare clocks before ordering witness statements. The scoring guide awarded credit for identifying the reset and citing the timestamp that showed it. It did not award points for naming a suspect or proposing a theory about the disappearance.

The source list included an archived public article, a weather bulletin, and a station maintenance notice. The sealed witness statement was referenced by a catalog number but not reproduced. The conservator checked the folder’s page sequence and found no missing leaf indicated by the original staple holes. That did not prove the copy had never been rearranged, but the surviving master was complete as cataloged.

The line did not prove that the case was fabricated. It did show that whoever prepared the exercise knew a sealed statement existed and intentionally excluded it from the student version. The public packet was not the entire file.

The archive’s method note explained that the exercise had been built from public records and one withheld reference. It did not classify the underlying disappearance as true, false, or staged. The police had not obtained the sealed statement, and the workshop had not produced it. Nia insisted that the public chronology say “the student version excluded a sealed statement,” not “the author hid evidence.” The distinction kept the document’s design from becoming a claim about its creator’s motive.

The placement addendum repeated the conditional award: a youth participant who independently identified the reset could be referred to a supervised records placement. Dr. Sagara was listed as mentor. The candidate field was blank. The donor field was held on a separate page that was not in the folder.

The archive compared the addendum with the copy Sagara had retained from the placement office. The wording was the same, but his copy carried a receipt stamp one week later than the master’s accession mark. That sequence was consistent with distribution after the master had been filed, although the two dates came from separate record systems. The institute did not have a signed transmittal connecting the folder to Sagara. He remembered receiving the exercise; the documents showed a route; the point where those accounts met remained unrecorded.

The accession record showed the master had arrived from the workshop in a sealed teaching collection. It had not been submitted by the anonymous source. The catalog number and accession date predated the public dispute. The archive custodian certified those facts from the collection’s own ledger, while noting that the physical folder could not be tied to one author.

A second sheet, a routing checklist, included an instruction: “Forward the completed exercise and score to placement office; do not preselect a candidate.” The last clause could be a compliance rule, a safeguard, or evidence that someone had considered the possibility. It did not prove that the rule was followed.

The district chief asked whether the file established a staged cold case.

“No,” Ishikawa said. “It establishes an exercise built from a public chronology, with an instruction to exclude a sealed statement. We don’t know who wrote it, whether the public case was real, or whether a candidate was selected in advance.”

“Then what does it establish?”

“That the exercise was designed and routed as a placement tool before Nia applied. The candidate field was blank in the copy we reviewed.”

The archive prepared a public summary. The redactions concealed unrelated participant notes and the donor sheet reference. Nia read it at the records room with Endo present. Her public chronology did not call the exercise fake or call Ren its author. She added the surviving instruction and the limits beside it.

Hoshina asked Nia whether knowing that a sealed statement had been excluded changed how she viewed the exercise.

“It changes what the exercise claimed to measure,” Nia said. “It doesn’t tell me who chose the records or why.”

The reporter asked if Nia would keep working on the chronology.

“For now. The board reviews my consent in two weeks.”

The archive’s public record gained a new fact: the placement exercise deliberately used a released chronology while withholding a sealed statement. It still did not identify its author or establish a preselected student.

The master copy had been cataloged before Nia applied.

The discovery also changed the request for the donor record. Ishikawa asked the institute to preserve the separate page referenced by the placement addendum, but not to disclose its contents until privacy counsel reviewed the scope. The page could identify a funding source or contain unrelated contributors. The district chief approved preservation only; any access would require another decision.

The workshop custodian confirmed that the master folder’s access log contained two catalog consultations before the placement office received Sagara’s copy. The log listed staff IDs, not personal names, and did not record whether anyone photographed or transcribed the contents. The two users would be contacted through the institute, but no one would assume they were the author or donor. The history had narrowed to a small set of dates. It had not yet narrowed to a person.

Its candidate field was blank. The instructions were not.
