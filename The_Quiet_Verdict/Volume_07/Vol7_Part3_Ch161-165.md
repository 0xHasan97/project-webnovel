The coastal archive had no disappearance file under the number in the exercise.

It did have a number. A records officer found it in a bound index for the year cited in the public-source packet. The code belonged to a weather-station maintenance drill, not a missing-person investigation. The archive had no case file, no report of a missing witness, and no sealed statement attached to that code.

The officer did not claim that no disappearance had ever occurred in the region. The search was narrower: the named station, the stated date range, and the case number printed in the exercise. Those references did not match the public packet’s description.

Ishikawa asked for the search method in writing. The archive provided its index terms, neighboring entries, and the applicable retention schedule. It also located the real maintenance drill report. That report used three time sources—the same kind of discrepancy the exercise asked a student to solve—but none involved a missing person. Its purpose was to compare station clocks after a reset.

The exercise’s three times had been altered. One sequence matched the maintenance drill; another matched a public weather bulletin from a different date. The scoring guide combined them into a chronology that had never appeared in the source documents.

The records specialist verified the matches independently, then asked the archive to confirm that the pages were complete. The maintenance report had a signed issue sheet and a transfer stamp. The weather bulletin was available in a dated public collection. The altered chronology was not found in either source.

The archive also compared the exercise’s date labels with the originals. The maintenance drill took place in late spring; the weather bulletin concerned a storm several months later. In the student packet, both were presented as observations from the same night. A careful reader could detect the discrepancy by comparing source dates, but the exercise’s cover did not tell students that its materials came from separate records.

The scoring guide praised the student for finding a clock reset and reconstructing the order of events. It did not award points for noticing that the underlying sources had been combined. That omission did not prove malice, but it changed the task: the intended answer assumed one coherent event where the cited materials documented two unrelated records.

The coastal archive checked its accession ledger against the exercise citation one more time. The catalog number used the right prefix for its collection, but the suffix pointed to a facilities series. No separate witness statement appeared under that reference. The archive could not rule out a miscataloged or lost page elsewhere; it could say the cited source, as written, did not support the exercise’s story.

Its cover called the packet “public-source case study,” but the scoring guide cited the maintenance drill code as the source of the supposed disappearance. A separate revision slip instructed staff to remove “simulation” from the cover sheet before distribution. The slip carried no signature. Its paper and type were consistent with the folder’s other administrative sheets, but neither feature identified an author.

The sealed-statement reference in the student version had no corresponding statement in the coastal archive. The catalog number was formatted like an archive reference but pointed to a shelf series about facilities maintenance. It might have been a deliberate disguise or an administrative error. The records alone could not decide.
