**Current-State Recap: The Collapse**

Ren Aizawa was arrested under a narrow warrant based on a signed, student-number-verified instruction to remove “simulation” from the cover and withhold the source key, alongside the coastal archive’s certified mismatch between the cited case number and its records. The warrant excludes the anonymous receipt, unattributed image request, Hara’s death, and Lantern House. The arrest at the school forum was captured on the livestream despite the plan for private service; arrest is not a finding of guilt. In court, the charge is limited to alleged fraud in the R-12 placement process. Evidence so far links Ren’s school identity to submission of the cover-change instruction, but does not establish that he authored the exercise, chose Nia, knew when it was assembled that the cited number was false, or that a particular candidate received the retained specimen. Nia attends some public proceedings voluntarily but is not a police witness; her private placement file remains separate. Ren has made no statement in court.

The Mechanism: a name written with a clear face in mind. The writer must see the face. A name alone fails. A heart event follows within forty seconds, or the manner written within six minutes and forty seconds. Each use is recorded in the writer’s own handwriting and cannot be erased. The ledger exists in one place only. Anyone who holds it may read it. It does not state how many times it has been used. Only the writer knows. This entry is internal continuity only; the court and characters have not connected it to Hara’s death.

# Chapter 166: The First Appearance

---

The first hearing began behind a closed door, then reopened when the clerk finished checking the gallery.

The restriction concerned the display of Ren’s face, not the evidence. The courthouse had received copies of the school livestream before the arrest report was filed. The presiding judge ordered the court’s own cameras turned away from the defendant’s table and prohibited the gallery from recording. The published docket would remain available, as would the certified transcript after review for protected student information.

Outside, the district had already divided the arrest into two stories. One headline called Ren the author of a fabricated case. Another called the warrant a retaliation for speaking at the forum. Neither paper had published the signed request in full. The court clerk posted a notice that the proceeding concerned one placement-fraud count and that no finding had yet been made.

Ishikawa sat behind the prosecutor. He was not the arresting officer assigned to describe the paperwork; that witness had been scheduled for the evidence hearing. The prosecutor read the count as filed: that Ren knowingly submitted a cover-change instruction in the R-12 placement process, directing the office to present a composite chronology as a public-source case study and to withhold its source key from the candidate, in a way alleged to have misrepresented the exercise’s character for placement purposes.

The wording was narrow. It did not accuse Ren of creating the exercise, choosing Nia, causing Hara’s death, arranging the Lantern House fire, or forging the anonymous transfer receipt. The prosecutor said those matters were not before the court. The archive’s certified mismatch and the signed request were the two central exhibits proposed for the next hearing.

Ren stood beside counsel as the clerk read the count. He did not speak. When the judge asked whether he understood that the charge was an allegation and that he was entitled to contest each part, his counsel asked for a brief consultation at the table. They spoke quietly, beyond the gallery’s hearing. Ren returned to his place without a statement.

Counsel entered a plea of not guilty on his behalf. The judge confirmed that the plea was being entered by counsel and that no inference would be drawn from Ren’s silence. The clerk recorded the response. No one asked him to explain the cover request in the hearing room.

The prosecutor asked for a ten-day evidence schedule and a protective order covering the candidate’s private placement file. Defense counsel agreed to protect unrelated student records but objected to sealing the source documents as a whole. The public, counsel argued, should be able to see the basis of the charge without seeing Nia’s fellowship records or another student’s number.

The judge approved a document-by-document review. The signed instruction, the relevant page of the front-office ledger, the archive’s search certification, and the workshop master could be discussed in open court. Personal identifiers, unrelated student names, and the donor sheet would remain sealed unless a later motion established a reason to disclose them.

Nia sat in the last row with Endo. She had chosen to attend after the board posted the schedule. Neither the prosecutor nor Ishikawa had asked her to appear. Her chronology group had issued a public notice that its work did not support either claim that Ren authored the entire exercise or that the arrest proved the inquiry had been fabricated. The note did not endorse the charge. It linked readers to the docket and the archive’s search statement.

A reporter turned around to ask whether Nia had come to testify. Endo stood between the aisle and her seat. Nia answered that she was attending a public hearing, not joining the case. She did not discuss her placement file. The court officer asked the gallery to stop directing questions at spectators.

At the table, defense counsel raised the livestream. The arrest had occurred in view of families, students, and reporters, despite the stated plan for private service. The defense asked that the court prohibit the prosecution from using the footage as evidence of guilt or as a substitute for proof of the alleged instruction.

The prosecutor agreed that the clip showed only the public arrest. It could establish when the warrant was served and what the audience saw; it did not prove the charge. The court ordered the parties not to play it before the fact-finder unless a specific evidentiary purpose was established. A copy would be preserved with its original sound and operator log.

The judge set the next hearing for the source archive’s records officer and the workshop conservator. The prosecution would have to establish the scope of the source mismatch. The defense could test what the archive had searched and what it could not claim. The signed request would be considered later, after the court heard how the school preserved and identified it.

At adjournment, Ren left through the courthouse’s public corridor with counsel. He did not stop for the microphones. The officers did not pose him for photographs. A court attendant held the door while the press waited outside the barrier.

The docket recorded the charge, the plea, and the next date. It did not record an explanation from Ren. By the time the courthouse doors closed, the online argument had reached the school’s forum clip again. The court’s narrower question was now fixed in writing: what did the signed instruction mean, and what could the records prove about the person who submitted it?

The clerk’s office issued a corrected public index before closing. An early draft had described Ren as the “exercise author,” repeating language from one of the headlines. The clerk removed it and replaced it with “defendant in placement-fraud proceeding.” The correction log preserved the earlier draft and the reason for the change. The court would not ask the public to trust an invisible edit.

Defense counsel requested confirmation that no witness would use the livestream as proof of the charge. The prosecutor confirmed that the recording concerned only the manner and time of service. The judge’s minute order repeated both limits.

# Chapter 167: The Citation

---

The archive’s director brought no missing-person file to court.

She brought the index, the maintenance report, the public weather bulletin, and a certification describing the search. Each item had its own accession label. The prosecutor placed them on the evidence table one at a time rather than stacking them beneath the exercise cover.

The director explained that the case number printed in the student packet used the archive’s correct regional prefix. Its suffix, however, pointed to a facilities-maintenance series. The bound index identified a weather-station clock drill. The archive had no missing-person report or witness statement under that citation.

The prosecutor asked whether the archive had found the person described in the exercise.

The director said the archive had not searched every local hospital, school, family register, or newspaper. Its holdings were not a complete record of everyone who had lived near the coast. It had searched the cited collection, date range, title, and adjacent index entries. The precise result was that the exercise’s stated source did not correspond to a missing-person case in the archive.

Defense counsel stood with the index open. The adjacent entries included several facilities reports that used similar number formats. Could a clerk have copied the wrong suffix? The director said it was possible. Was there a separate regional register she had not searched? Yes. Could a private statement have been filed without the same number? She could not rule that out. Could an event have occurred without entering the archive’s records at all? Certainly.

The judge asked counsel to keep the question within the certification. The defense withdrew the broadest wording and asked only whether the citation supported the exercise’s claim. The director said no. It identified a clock-maintenance drill, not a disappearance inquiry.

The prosecutor then placed the public weather bulletin beside the drill report. The bulletin came from a later date. In the exercise, times taken from both documents had been presented as observations from one night. The archive’s copy and the workshop master carried different labels, but their matching figures were visible in the approved public exhibits.

The director could verify the source records, not who had selected them. She had not attended the workshop and had no knowledge of who assembled the packet. No name appeared in the archive’s catalog field for the exercise’s author. The source citation could demonstrate a mismatch; it could not identify the person who introduced it.

Defense counsel asked whether the exercise might still have been intended as a fictional puzzle. The director said that was not a question for her. Her office had no record establishing what the workshop intended. She could say that the underlying documents were authentic and that the combined chronology was not found in either source.

Nia’s public chronology had used the same language. She sat in the gallery, not at the witness table. During a recess, a reporter asked whether she felt vindicated. Nia said the archive had answered the question it was asked. She would not turn a source mismatch into a conclusion about the writer. Endo reminded the reporter that she was not a witness in the hearing.

The judge admitted the index and both source records for the limited purpose of showing what the citation referred to and what the archive had found. The ruling excluded any claim that no disappearance had ever occurred. The prosecutor’s opening statement would have to observe that limit. The defense retained the right to argue that a citation error alone did not prove intent.

Before the witness left, the defense asked for the archive’s handling history. The index had been kept in a climate-controlled room, with each request entered in a retrieval log. The maintenance report carried an issue stamp and a return notation. Neither record had been held by the school before the inquiry. The director could account for their custody from the archive’s own records, but could not say who had handled copies outside that institution. The judge allowed the certified chain records as foundation and reminded both sides that intact custody authenticated the archive pages, not the exercise’s authorship or intent.

The workshop conservator testified next. The master packet’s accession ledger showed it entered the teaching collection before Nia applied to Aster. The packet included a three-clock exercise, scoring guide, and placement addendum. The conservator could identify the folder’s page sequence and the date on its catalog entry. He could not identify who wrote the questions or who selected the sources.

The defense examined the blank candidate field. It had not been filled in on the master copy. The placement addendum described a conditional referral if a youth participant independently identified the reset. That language was present before Nia’s application, but did not say who would later be considered. The conservator could not exclude a separate document outside the folder.

The prosecutor asked about the line instructing that the sealed witness statement not be included in the student version. The conservator said the reference was present, but the archive did not have the statement. The line showed that the workshop copy knew of a withheld item; it did not show whether that item existed in the cited collection or why it had been excluded.

When the hearing ended, the court clerk issued a transcript notice and listed the admitted exhibits by number. The archive’s certification was now evidence with a defined boundary. It was not proof of Ren’s authorship, and it was not a universal proof of absence. The next session would address the cover instruction that the prosecution said crossed that boundary into a deliberate placement deception.

The court clerk listed each exhibit by its evidentiary purpose. The archive certification was marked for the citation and search scope. The workshop master was marked for its contents and accession date. Neither sleeve was marked “author.” A reporter asked whether the distinction was a technicality. The conservator said it was the difference between knowing what a document contained and knowing why it had been made. The court had established the mismatch within a narrow boundary; it had not yet identified the person who put the cover-change request into the process.

The defense then asked the conservator to separate the accession date from the date of composition. The folder had been cataloged before Nia applied, but the accession ledger did not state when each sheet had been printed or assembled. The conservator said the folder’s order was consistent with its catalog description and the staple impressions showed no missing leaf. Neither observation established that every page had entered the collection on the same day.

The prosecution did not claim the catalog date identified an author. It offered the date to establish that the exercise existed before Nia’s application. The judge admitted the ledger for that limited purpose and barred counsel from saying the candidate field proved either a prior selection or a lack of one. A blank space could be consistent with no candidate chosen; it could also be consistent with a name stored elsewhere.

The archive director returned briefly to identify the certification number on the source search. Defense counsel asked why the report named adjacent catalog series but not every box in the regional repository. The director said the request had named a specific source citation, title, and date. Staff had searched the immediate index and adjacent entries because the suffix pointed to that series. The court’s earlier ruling would not expand the report into a search of every record in the region.

A juror was not present; this was a judge-heard proceeding. The judge nevertheless asked counsel to avoid the phrase “the archive proved the case was fake.” The record showed a citation mismatch and a combined chronology unsupported by the cited sources. Whether that amounted to knowing fraud depended on the instruction and the evidence of the signer’s knowledge. No archive witness could answer that question.

The hearing concluded with a joint exhibit list. The parties agreed to describe the master as a workshop exercise with an accession entry predating Nia’s application, a blank candidate field, and unidentified authorship. The archive search statement would be described as limited to the specified collection and cited number. Neither item would be summarized as proof that a candidate was selected in advance or that no local disappearance had occurred.

# Chapter 168: The Cover

---

The placement officer described the cover as the first thing a candidate received.

She had worked in the office for eleven years and handled the forms for supervised youth placements. Her role was administrative. She had not helped design the three-clock exercise and did not know who had selected its sources. The signed correction request had been found in a school response file and shown to her only after the records custodian located it.

The prosecutor asked what the word “simulation” meant on a student exercise cover.

“It tells the candidate that the case is constructed,” the officer said. “The exercise can still use real records. It means the sequence may have been assembled for instruction rather than copied as one real event.”

“What did ‘public-source case study’ tell a candidate?”

“That the cited materials came from public sources. It did not, by itself, certify that the chronology was an authentic case file.”

Defense counsel noted the distinction. The signed request did not say “authentic police file.” It directed staff to use “public-source case study.” The officer agreed that those words could describe a teaching exercise built from public records. A cover label alone could not establish what a student would infer.

The prosecutor asked why a request to remove “simulation” mattered. The officer said the placement process awarded a supervised referral to a participant who independently identified the clock reset. If the student believed the events formed one actual chronology, that belief could affect how the task was approached. The office had not kept a written rule explaining whether “simulation” could be removed after scoring. She could not say whether the request violated an internal policy.

The request also said, “Do not give the candidate the source key.” The officer confirmed that the scoring guide was ordinarily kept separate from student copies. A candidate was expected to solve the exercise without the answer key. That part of the instruction was consistent with ordinary testing practice, unless the source key was needed to disclose that the records came from separate dates.

The defense asked her to define “source key.” The phrase could refer to an answer sheet, a list of citations, or a teacher’s guide. She said the office used more than one form of key. The request did not attach a definition. Did she know which key the writer meant? No. Had she personally withheld a key from Nia? She had not handled Nia’s application and could not answer from personal knowledge.

The court marked the placement officer’s testimony as limited. She could explain common office practice but not what Ren intended. She had no memory of receiving his request. The contemporaneous ledger and document would be considered separately.

On cross-examination, the officer acknowledged that the master folder itself used the phrase “public-source case study.” That label had been typed before the unsigned correction carbon was found. The exercise drew from authentic public records; the false chronology emerged only when the records were combined. The officer could not say whether the person who requested the cover change knew the sources came from different dates.

The prosecutor returned to the reason field on the carbon: “Eligible for supervised placement after independent score.” The officer said that condition linked the cover change to the placement process. It still did not identify who wrote the carbon or what that person knew about the source mismatch.

A member of the press gallery wrote the phrase “candidate was tricked” on a notepad. The judge saw it and warned the gallery that the hearing was not a press conference. The evidence established no delivery of a particular version to Nia and no statement from her about what she believed at the time. The court would not use the candidate’s later success as proof of what the author intended before the exercise was distributed.

Outside the courtroom, the institute issued a statement that its usual practice was to separate the answer key from student packets. The placement officer had not testified that the request was routine in every respect; the institute’s statement did not address the removal of “simulation.” The archive group asked that both statements be linked to the transcript rather than quoted separately.

Nia reviewed the group’s draft note with Endo. A volunteer had written that the testimony proved a candidate had been deceived. Nia crossed out “proved.” The office’s practice explained what the labels could mean. The archive established that the cited records did not support one continuous disappearance chronology. Neither source showed what Ren knew when he signed, whether a particular candidate received the cover, or how that candidate understood it.

The group published the correction before the afternoon session. It did not mention Nia’s own placement experience. Her file remained separate, as she had asked. The notice linked the admitted exhibit list and the officer’s transcript so readers could check the language themselves.

The judge set the next witness: the school records custodian who had found the signed request. The court would hear how the file was preserved, how the signature was linked to Ren’s school identity, and whether the front-office ledger documented the same delivery. The meaning of the cover had been argued. The hand that submitted it was next.

The court also requested the placement office’s blank templates from the same year. The officer identified two versions. One used “simulation” for constructed exercises; another used “public-source case study” for assignments that asked students to analyze archival materials. Neither template defined when a case study stopped being a simulation. The office had used the phrases inconsistently in other classes, though no other example combined records from different dates under one disappearance number.

The defense offered the templates to show that the alternate wording was ordinary. The prosecutor agreed they were relevant but argued that ordinary wording could still misdescribe this particular packet. The judge admitted them with that limitation. Their existence did not explain the request’s reference to placement eligibility or the separate direction to withhold the source key.

The placement officer testified that the independent score was calculated before any candidate was referred. The award was conditional, not automatic. Her office retained scores under restricted access, and the court had not ordered the production of Nia’s file. A different candidate might have been eligible; the surviving addendum did not name one. The office could not say that any candidate had been promised the placement before completing the exercise.

The parties disputed whether the term “candidate” in the signed instruction meant one selected person or any student who might receive the packet. The officer said the office used “candidate” for any eligible youth under consideration. The word alone did not mean a final selection. This narrowed what the request established: it addressed an intended student version in a placement process, but did not name Nia or prove she had already been chosen.

The clerk recorded the templates as separate exhibits. Nia’s chronology group linked the transcript and did not reproduce the candidate scores or application material. The court’s decision to hear about ordinary office usage had not opened the private placement file. The judge set a further witness to clarify how the request entered the records system, not to infer its author’s state of mind from vocabulary alone.

# Chapter 169: The Signature

---

The records custodian began with the box, not the signature.

It had been stored in the placement office’s locked records room and transferred to the school archive after the office was reorganized. The response file was listed on an inventory made during that transfer. No one had opened the inner envelope until the school received the district’s preservation request. The custodian photographed it in place, recorded the seal condition, and had a second staff member witness the opening.

The signed request was inside. So was the front-office ledger page for the same date. The custodian had not found the anonymous transfer receipt there. The two school records came from the response file and the bound daily ledger; neither had been supplied by the anonymous sender.

The prosecutor asked how the office identified the person who delivered the form. The ledger recorded a name, student number, time, and the words “cover correction for R-12.” A clerk had noted that the person presented a school card. The student number matched the registrar index entry for Ren Aizawa. The signature on the request read “Ren Aizawa.”

The clerk who made the ledger entry had since retired. The custodian had not personally watched the delivery. The school’s card-check policy required clerks to compare the number on the card with the front-office index, but no photocopy of the card was attached. The witness could establish what the surviving records said and how the school stored them. He could not testify from memory to Ren standing at the desk.

Defense counsel asked whether the ledger might have been copied from the correction request after the fact. The custodian said the ledger was bound, numbered, and used daily. It showed ink entries on neighboring dates, and its page sequence matched the archive transfer inventory. He had no evidence that this entry was added later. He could not prove no one had altered it before the school received the file.

The signature comparison had not been made by a handwriting examiner. The front-office employee recorded the student number; the registrar verified that number against the enrolled student. The request’s signature and the ledger’s identity entry corroborated one another, but the custodian could not independently say who held the pen.

The prosecutor asked why the records were treated as authentic. The custodian explained that the request remained in the file where the placement office had kept it, folded around the routing copy. The ledger remained in the bound volume. The school’s inventory predated the inquiry. The district’s photographs, seals, and transfer receipts documented the later chain. The judge admitted the documents, reserving the weight of the identity link for the final ruling.

On cross-examination, the defense focused on the word “submitted.” The ledger said the person at the desk delivered a cover correction. It did not say that person typed the request, drafted the language, designed the exercise, or understood the source mismatch. The custodian agreed. The student card linked the desk entry to Ren’s school identity; it did not supply a motive.

The request’s date was the same date as the front-office entry. The school could not establish when the form had been written. The ink had not been chemically dated. The file location supported that both papers had been kept together, not that one had been created at the moment the other was signed.

The prosecutor asked whether any record contradicted the delivery entry. The custodian knew of none. The defense asked whether the retired clerk could be called. The prosecution had listed her as unavailable due to illness, and the court ordered the parties to submit the necessary notice and prior statement before relying on her account. The ledger would not become a live eyewitness merely because its handwriting was legible.

Ren remained at the table. He did not address the court. Counsel rose twice to object to questions that treated the signature as proof of the exercise’s authorship. Each objection was sustained in part: the document could be considered as evidence that a cover-change request was submitted under Ren’s identity, not as proof he wrote every page in the R-12 folder.

During recess, Nia left the gallery before reporters reached the corridor. She had not been named as a witness. The court had not admitted her private placement file, and neither side had asked to use it. When a reporter asked if the request had changed her account of the exercise, she said the hearing had not established what she personally received. She declined to add anything and walked with Endo toward the public records desk.

The court clerk uploaded the admitted ledger excerpt with the student number masked. The full copy remained in the sealed exhibit file. The transcript described the witness’s limits: the record was contemporaneous, the identity check followed school procedure, and the clerk who made the entry was not present to testify.

The signature had become more than an unexplained mark. The school’s records placed Ren at the office and linked his identity to a request delivered there. What remained contested was what he meant by it, what he knew about the constructed chronology, and whether the request satisfied the charge the prosecutor had chosen.

The defense asked the judge to distinguish the strength of the two school records. The signature appeared on the request; the ledger recorded a person presenting a school card and delivering a correction. The registry linked the number to Ren’s enrolled identity. Together they supported the prosecution’s account of submission. But neither document contained a statement that Ren had assembled the exercise, examined the archive’s original sources, or intended to deceive a candidate.

The prosecutor agreed that the documents did not establish those broader facts by themselves. The charge did not require the court to find that Ren wrote every page, the prosecutor said. It required a finding about the specific instruction and the placement process. The defense responded that the same distinction made knowledge crucial: a signature on a cover request could not prove that Ren understood the chronology’s source mismatch.

The judge declined to decide the issue during the foundation hearing. The records were admitted; their weight and the inference of knowledge would be argued after the remaining witnesses. The ruling was entered in the transcript, where neither side could later describe authentication as a verdict.

The parties also agreed that the second interview transcript could be considered only to show what Ren had been asked before the school file was discovered. His statement that he could not place the packet had not been a denial that he submitted the separate request. The prosecutor would not call it a contradiction. The defense would not use the interview to argue that the request was impossible.

The court ordered both parties to submit a short list of propositions each exhibit could support and propositions it could not support. The judge wanted final arguments to distinguish a record’s provenance from an inference about knowledge. The exercise master, source certification, signature, and front-office entry would be considered in combination, but combination did not erase their individual limits.

The next witness could identify the ledger entry from personal memory. Until then, the school’s documents would carry the case only as far as their own lines.

# Chapter 170: The Candidate Copy

---

The candidate copy was not Nia’s file.

It was a sealed duplicate retained by the placement office as a specimen of the student version. The school produced it under the court’s order with unrelated applicant names and scores removed. The conservator compared its staple pattern and page sequence with the workshop master. The court allowed the comparison but prohibited the gallery from photographing the unredacted sheet.

The copy contained the three-clock exercise and the public-source cover. It did not contain the scoring guide or the source key. A checklist attached to the office’s packet log recorded that the candidate version had been assembled without the instructor materials. The records did not identify who had removed those pages, when they were separated, or whether this exact specimen had ever been handed to a student.

The prosecutor argued that the signed request gave the exclusion a different meaning. The instruction did not merely say to keep an answer sheet out of a test packet. It paired the withheld key with a request to remove “simulation” and call the chronology a public-source case study. In the prosecution’s reading, the combination hid the fact that the apparent case was a constructed sequence assembled from unrelated records.

The defense said the key’s contents were not in evidence. The court had not seen a document defining the “source key,” and the checklist used the broader term “instructor materials.” The placement officer had testified that answer keys were normally kept from candidates. The candidate specimen could show what pages were absent, but not why or who took them out.

The judge asked the parties to separate three propositions. First, the candidate copy was missing the instructor materials. Second, Ren’s signed request directed that the source key not be given to the candidate. Third, the instruction was intended to conceal the source mismatch. The first two had documentary support. The third remained an inference.

The prosecutor accepted the distinction. The request’s context, the archive’s mismatch, and the condition for supervised placement could support an inference of intent, the prosecutor said. The defense replied that a possible inference was not yet proof beyond the standard required at trial. The court said it would decide that question after all the evidence and arguments were complete.

The conservator testified that the candidate specimen’s cover had no “simulation” label. Its paper and printing were consistent with the school’s retained packet stock, but the conservator could not date the printing to the day of distribution. The workshop master and the office copy were similar, not identical. A school copy could be reproduced without leaving a mark in the workshop archive.

Defense counsel asked if a missing page could be the source key. The conservator said the specimen had no page sequence gap; it had been assembled as a student version with fewer leaves. That answered what the retained copy contained, not whether any particular candidate had been given the same version.

The school clerk’s handwritten checklist included a box marked “key retained.” The clerk had initialed the box but could not be called in person; the parties agreed to admit the routine record after reviewing the foundation. The defense preserved its objection that the initials established an office action, not Ren’s knowledge or the candidate’s receipt.

From the gallery, Nia watched the exhibit number appear on the court screen. She did not ask to see the sealed pages. Her public chronology had never claimed that the candidate copy was hers. A volunteer from the group messaged her that reporters were calling it “Nia’s test.” She asked Endo to post a correction: the record was a retained specimen, its recipient was unknown, and the public had no basis to identify it as Nia’s personal copy.

The correction went up while the judge was still hearing arguments. The group did not publish the page image. It linked the redacted exhibit description and the transcript. Nia’s separate placement inquiry remained outside the criminal case, as agreed when she renewed consent to chair the chronology group.

At the end of the day, the prosecutor asked to call one more records witness: the clerk who had received the correction request and entered it in the ledger. The clerk’s health had improved, and she was expected to testify in person the following week. The defense requested the original ledger and all available prior statements. The judge ordered production before testimony.

Ren left the courtroom without speaking. Cameras waited beyond the steps, but the court’s recording rule remained in effect. Reporters asked counsel whether silence meant the signature was uncontested. Counsel said no inference could be drawn from it; the documents would be tested through witnesses and the court’s rulings.

By evening, the candidate copy had produced a more precise dispute, not a resolution. The instruction to withhold a key was signed. A retained student specimen lacked that key. No record yet proved that this specimen reached Nia, that every candidate received the same pages, or that the person who signed knew the cited case number belonged to a maintenance drill.

The next hearing would bring the distribution clerk to the witness stand. The docket gave it a single line: testimony concerning the four student-set entries and the retained instructor materials.

The defense asked the school to produce the full retention rule for candidate specimens. The admitted checklist showed that a key had been retained, but did not state how long specimens were kept or whether the copy had been created before or after any particular exercise. The judge ordered the school to provide the rule under seal first. If it revealed unrelated student information, the court would prepare a redacted public excerpt.

The prosecutor objected that the request might delay the hearing. The judge said provenance was central to the weight of the specimen. The school would not be presumed to have delivered this copy to anyone merely because it had kept it. The parties could still argue from the signed instruction and the office’s routine, but they could not turn a specimen’s existence into proof of a completed handoff.

Nia saw the updated docket that evening. She asked Endo to add a line to the group’s correction log: “Candidate version is a retained specimen; no recipient is identified.” Endo said that wording might be mistaken for a finding that no one else had received the exercise. Nia revised it: “The specimen’s recipient is not established by the admitted record.” The distinction mattered. Absence of a named recipient was not proof that no candidate had received a copy.

The group adopted the sentence and cited the exhibit number. It did not speculate about Nia’s own materials or whether she had seen a key. Her personal file remained outside the court’s order, outside the chronology’s evidence set, and outside the story the group was willing to tell in public.

Ren remained silent as the court recessed. The record grew around the instruction, but the candidate it was meant to reach remained unnamed.

The sealed retention rule had not yet been released to either party. The judge set a return date and directed the school to preserve the original checklist, the specimen, and any distribution log without further annotation. The order did not authorize access to Nia’s separate application or score.
