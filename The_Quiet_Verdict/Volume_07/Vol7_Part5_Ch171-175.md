*Chapters 171 to 175 (Part 5 of Volume 7)*

**Current-State Recap: The Collapse**

Ren Aizawa has pleaded not guilty through counsel and remains silent; the court has said that silence cannot support an inference. The narrow placement-fraud charge concerns his signed, student-number-verified instruction to remove “simulation” and withhold the source key. A retired front-office clerk recalls receiving the form from a student who presented Ren’s card, but did not see the signature written and cannot testify to the visitor’s knowledge or motive. The retained instructor key lists the separate source records and dates, but no access record places it in Ren’s hands. A redacted distribution register records four student sets issued with the key retained; it does not identify destinations or prove that Nia received a copy. Her private placement file remains separate. The court denied dismissal at this stage but has made no finding of guilt. The clerk now recalls an unproduced attachment; its contents remain unknown pending a documented search.

The Mechanism is internal continuity only. No witness, court record, or character has connected it to Hara’s death. Keep the external narration and ground court-proceeding details in public transcripts, admitted exhibits, or official docket entries; do not narrate sealed discussions as if Kuze witnessed them.

# Chapter 171: The Clerk

---

The clerk remembered the card before she remembered the face.

The public transcript began with her oath and the judge’s warning that she must distinguish memory from the ledger. The court had admitted the bound front-office book the day before. Its entry recorded Ren Aizawa, a student number, a time, and “cover correction for R-12.” The current hearing would test whether that line came from a routine action or a person the clerk could identify.

She had retired from Aster’s front desk the previous spring. The records custodian had first contacted her after the school found the response file. She had not been shown a photograph before the interview. She had read the ledger entry, then described what she recalled: a student who presented a school card, waited while she checked the number, and handed over a folded request.

The prosecutor asked whether the student had given his name.

“He said Ren Aizawa. I checked the card against the student index.”

“Do you see that person in court?”

The clerk looked toward the table. “I believe that is the student who came to the desk.”

The judge asked how certain she was. The clerk said the visit had taken place months earlier, during a crowded school week. She remembered it because the visitor had asked her to keep a “source key” out of the student packet, and she had written a short note on the routing line. She could not promise that no other student had looked similar. She did remember the card and the name displayed in the register.

Defense counsel asked whether the prosecutor had shown her Ren’s name before she made the identification. She said the ledger entry included it. The lawyer then asked whether she had been shown the signature on the request before she spoke with the custodian.

“No. They told me the school found a request. I did not see the signature until after I had described the visit.”

“Had you seen Mr. Aizawa’s school photograph?”

“No.”

“Had you participated in a lineup or a photo array?”

“No.”

The identification was therefore not a blind test. The clerk had seen Ren in the courtroom before she was asked to identify him. The judge noted the limitation for the record. The prosecution did not ask the court to treat the in-court recognition as independent proof by itself.

The prosecutor showed her the ledger entry. She confirmed the short note in the margin: “key remains office.” She had made it at the time, she said, because she needed to tell the placement staff which materials were not to go into the student packet. The entry did not say who wrote the request, but she testified that the visitor passed her the paper and asked that the instruction be carried out.

The defense asked what exact words the visitor used. The clerk could not recall a full sentence. She remembered “keep the key in office” and “for the candidate.” The signed form had similar wording, but she could not say whether her memory came from the conversation or from reading the ledger after the school located it. She agreed that the margin note might have refreshed the memory rather than merely confirming it.

The prosecutor asked whether the visitor had told her why the key should be held back.

“No.”

Had he told her the cited case number was a weather-station drill? No. Had he described the source dates? No. Had he said Nia’s name? The clerk did not remember it being mentioned. She could testify to receiving an instruction under a student identity, not to the visitor’s knowledge or motive.

The defense then challenged the original record. The clerk had written in the ledger, but the student number was copied from the card, not dictated. The office did not photocopy student cards. She had compared it with the index and returned the card. No second employee stood beside her for that check. She had made the entry before the later preservation inventory, in the ordinary course of the desk’s work.

The court asked the parties to keep three facts separate. The clerk remembered receiving a card and a form. The contemporaneous ledger linked the visit to a student number. The signature on the form was a third item; she had not watched it being written. The prosecutor agreed. The defense reserved its challenge to how much the records corroborated one another.

During a recess, reporters waited at the courthouse steps. Nia came out with Endo and did not answer questions. A volunteer in the chronology group sent her the public transcript page number. She replied that the group would wait until the certified copy was posted before quoting it. Her own placement file remained outside the hearing. Nothing in the clerk’s testimony established which candidate received any retained specimen.

When the hearing resumed, defense counsel returned to the phrase “source key.” The clerk said staff usually kept answer materials away from candidates. She had not known whether this key contained an answer, a list of sources, or both. She had not opened it. The instruction could fit ordinary handling practice, although the cover change and the source-key line appeared together on the form.

The judge admitted her testimony about the delivery and routine handling, subject to the limits she had described. The court did not treat the in-court identification as conclusive, and it did not allow the clerk to infer the writer’s knowledge from the words on the page. Her account supported that a student using Ren’s school identity brought the instruction to the office.

At adjournment, the clerk asked whether she could have her own copy of the transcript. The judge approved a redacted version that omitted student numbers and unrelated staff names. Her testimony would remain public, but the school’s other records would not become public by association.

The next witness would examine the signature itself. For the first time, the court would hear whether the handwriting could connect the person in the ledger to the paper in the file.

# Chapter 172: The Key

---

The source key arrived in a sealed evidence sleeve.

The court had ordered the placement office to produce the retained instructor copy, if it could be identified without opening Nia’s private file. The school’s custodian found a thin blue packet in a locked cabinet. Its outer label carried the R-12 serial and a stamp reading “instructor material.” A second seal had been added when the district took custody. The witness listed each transfer from the cabinet to the courtroom and confirmed that the seal was intact.

The judge allowed the sleeve to be opened in public. No candidate names appeared inside. The packet contained a scoring guide, a source table, and a short instructor note. The papers were cataloged as one unit in the placement office’s retained file, but no page named an author.

The source table listed the weather-station maintenance drill and the later public weather bulletin as different records from different dates. Beside the exercise’s three-clock problem, the guide awarded points for identifying a reset and comparing the dates attached to the underlying documents. It did not identify a missing person, a witness, or a single real night on which all three times occurred.

The prosecutor asked the records specialist whether the source key matched the R-12 master packet.

“The serial and exercise title match,” she said. “The table uses the same three clocks. The pagination is consistent with the office’s instructor packet.”

Could she establish when the key had been assembled? No. The cabinet catalog entry predated Nia’s application, but the individual pages had no print date. The packet’s outer sleeve bore an office label, not a creator’s name. She could not say who wrote the guide or whether its contents had been complete when it was first filed.

The prosecutor asked whether the key explained the exercise’s source mismatch. The specialist said it made the source dates visible to an instructor and provided a scoring route for identifying the reset. It did not itself state that a student copy should be labeled a simulation, and it did not name the person who had changed the cover.

Defense counsel stressed that distinction. The key showed what an instructor could know, not what Ren knew. The signed request told staff not to give the key to a candidate; it did not say Ren had read the key. The specialist agreed. No access log placed the packet in Ren’s hands. No receipt showed he had opened it.

The defense also asked whether withholding an instructor guide was ordinary. The placement officer had already testified that candidate versions did not usually include answer materials. The specialist said the source table was more than an answer list, but the office had no written rule requiring it to be given to candidates. She could not say whether a student would have been expected to compare the sources without seeing it.

The judge asked the prosecutor why the key mattered to the charge. The prosecutor said the combined evidence could support an inference: the signed request removed the “simulation” label and directed the office to keep a document showing the separate source dates away from the candidate. The judge replied that the inference was for the final decision. Admission of the key did not prove Ren had seen it or knew its contents.

The source key was entered as a limited exhibit. The public copy showed the serial, the source titles, and the differing dates. A teacher’s extension number and a staff-only storage code were masked. The full original remained in the court file. The records specialist signed a receipt before returning it to its sleeve.

Nia’s chronology group did not publish an interpretation. It added the exhibit number and a short description: “The retained instructor key lists separate source records and dates; the key’s author and any access by Ren are not established.” One volunteer proposed that the word “key” proved a deliberate concealment. Nia struck the sentence. The signed instruction and the key’s contents could be considered together, but the record still did not show that Ren had read the source table.

Outside the courtroom, a reporter asked the records specialist whether the key proved the exercise had been designed to catch a candidate. She said the score rewarded recognizing the reset and checking the source dates. It did not say who the candidate would be, or what outcome the designer expected. The reporter published the narrower answer with the exhibit link.

The defense requested the office’s distribution register to determine whether the instructor key had traveled with the exercise or remained separate. The prosecutor agreed to produce the R-12 page with all candidate identities sealed. The judge ordered a redacted copy for the next session and prohibited any party from using the request to obtain Nia’s private placement application.

The custodian returned the key to the sleeve under the court’s supervision. Before sealing it, both parties were permitted to inspect the page edges and compare the listed source titles with the workshop master. No one was allowed to remove a staple or test the ink. The conservator said the pages were consistent with office copying stock but had no feature that could date them to the day of the signed request. The court’s exhibit photograph showed the packet’s condition, not its entire history.

The prosecutor asked whether the source table’s presence in the cabinet meant the signed request referred to this exact sheet. The records specialist said the title and serial made it a plausible match. There was no page number or cross-reference on Ren’s instruction identifying this version. The phrase “source key” could describe the packet, a duplicate, or a separate teacher’s sheet not preserved here.

The judge directed the transcript to use “the retained R-12 instructor packet” rather than “the key Ren withheld.” That wording preserved the link without assuming access or identity beyond the records. Defense counsel accepted the description, reserving the argument that no witness had shown Ren saw the packet.

As the clerk placed the blue sleeve back in the evidence cabinet, a line of small print on the register copy caught the court’s attention: “student sets issued: four.” The line named no recipient. The instructor key had remained in the office. The four copies had gone somewhere else.

# Chapter 173: The Register

---

Four student sets had been issued under the R-12 serial.

The placement office’s distribution register was brought into court by the records custodian. Its original pages included candidate identifiers, which remained sealed. The public excerpt showed the serial, the number of student sets, the date range, and a column for “key retained.” The records custodian confirmed that the public excerpt was a true copy of the relevant lines and that unrelated entries had been masked.

The prosecutor asked whether the four sets had all been issued on the same day. The custodian said the register gave a range covering two afternoons. It did not list exact times. The register did not identify where the sets went, only that they had been checked out from the placement office. The destination codes were stored in a separate index, which the school had not produced.

The defense asked why the code index was separate. The custodian said the office maintained a privacy key for candidate records. Names and application files were not written in the main register. The separate key was retained under a stricter schedule. Its production would reveal unrelated student information and potentially Nia’s private file.

The prosecutor said the names were not necessary to establish that multiple sets had been issued. The defense agreed, provided the court did not infer that any set had reached Nia. The judge accepted the redacted page for the limited fact that four student copies were logged as issued and the instructor key was marked retained. The identity and outcome of each transfer were outside the public exhibit.

The distribution officer testified that “issued” meant a packet left the locked cabinet for an authorized school or placement office. It did not mean a candidate accepted it. No receipt from an individual student appeared on the public copy. The school sometimes returned packets to storage after a session; the register did not track those returns on the same page.

“Can you tell the court which of the four sets was intended for Nia?” defense counsel asked.

“No. Not from this register.”

“Can the office tell us without opening her private application?”

The officer said the separate index might link the codes to cohorts, but the office would need a privacy review. She could not say whether the index could identify a specific recipient without consulting the protected files. The judge directed the school not to conduct that search for this proceeding unless a later motion showed it was necessary and the court could protect Nia’s consent boundaries.

The prosecutor asked whether the four sets made the exercise a group placement tool rather than an exercise prepared for one named candidate. The officer said four copies were issued. She could not say whether all were used at once, whether one was a spare, or whether the copies went to one site or several. The register did not state the number of eligible candidates. It did not say that four people were under consideration.

The defense asked whether the four sets could have gone to four separate schools. They could. Could they have been issued for the same workshop session? They could. Could a packet have been copied after the logged issue? The register would not show that. The testimony established the cabinet’s recorded output, not every copy in circulation.

The judge reminded the parties that the phrase “four student sets” could not be converted into four named candidates or proof that Nia was one of them. The prosecution agreed. The defense agreed that the number did not disprove a plan involving one intended recipient. The register supported neither conclusion by itself.

Nia watched the hearing from the rear gallery. She had not asked the school to search the separate index. After the session, Endo showed her the proposed public summary. It said four sets had been recorded as issued, with destinations and recipients unknown. Nia approved the wording and asked that the record’s privacy restriction be mentioned, so the blank destinations would not be mistaken for careless omission.

The summary group also added a note that Nia was not a witness and that her private application had not been opened. The note did not claim she was or was not among the possible recipients. The board published the exhibit reference without the sealed code index.

The school’s counsel requested a conference about whether the separate index had any relevance to the count. The judge denied an immediate search and said the prosecution already had the signed request, the retained key, and the redacted distribution total. If it wished to identify a particular recipient, it would have to explain why that identity mattered and why a less intrusive method would not suffice.

The judge also asked the custodian to explain the difference between an issue entry and a receipt. The custodian said the office used the former when a staff member took a packet from storage for an approved session. A separate sign-back line was used when materials returned. The public excerpt showed four issue entries but did not include a completed sign-back field for each one. That could mean the packets remained out, were returned under another code, or were logged on a page not included in the redacted copy. The custodian could not choose among those possibilities.

The defense argued that the incomplete return information made it impossible to tell whether the copies reached any students. The prosecutor replied that the register still showed the office released four sets for placement use. The judge said both statements could be true: an issue record documented movement from storage, while the missing destination data limited what could be said about final delivery.

A second clerk checked the relevant page against the bound volume during a short recess. She confirmed the excerpt preserved the columns for date range, serial, number of student sets, and key-retained status. The masked section contained destination codes, but the court did not review the code index or names. The check verified that the public copy had not omitted a second entry about the number of sets; it did not resolve where they went.

The prosecutor did not make that request. Instead, the office confirmed that the key-retained notation applied to the same date range as the four student-set entries. It did not show who entered the notation or whether it described every packet in circulation.

The register had broadened the route and narrowed the claim. Four copies existed in the office’s distribution record. The court still did not know who received them, whether Nia had ever seen one, or whether the copies had all carried the same cover.

# Chapter 174: The Limit

---

The defense asked the court to dismiss the charge before the next witness.

Counsel did not dispute that the request carried Ren Aizawa’s signature or that the school’s front-office ledger linked his student number to its delivery. The motion argued that the prosecution had shown no proof that he knew the composite chronology’s source dates or intended to deceive a candidate. The archive could establish a mismatch. The key could expose the mismatch to an instructor. The distribution register could show that four student sets left the office. None established what Ren knew when he submitted the instruction.

The prosecutor answered that knowledge could be inferred from context. The request did not simply ask to keep a routine answer sheet from a student. It paired removal of “simulation” with an instruction to withhold a source key that listed distinct source records and dates. The placement office connected the request to a conditional opportunity. The prosecutor argued that a fact-finder could reasonably view the combined instruction as intended to make a composite chronology appear more authentic than its sources warranted.

The judge asked whether the prosecution claimed that Ren had personally read the instructor key.

“No direct record shows he did,” the prosecutor said. “We rely on the request’s contents and circumstances, not on a claim of documented access.”

The court asked the defense whether the ordinary separation of answer materials from candidate packets fully explained the request. Counsel said it might explain the source-key clause, but not necessarily the cover change. That did not make intent established; it left competing interpretations for the final decision.

The judge denied dismissal at this stage. The signed request, the front-office identity entry, the archive certification, and the key could be considered together. A reasonable fact-finder could infer knowing misrepresentation, but was not required to do so. The ruling was not a finding that Ren had acted fraudulently, authored the exercise, or known every source discrepancy. The burden remained with the prosecution through the end of the case.

The written order explained that denying dismissal meant only that the state had produced enough admissible material for the case to continue. It did not shift any burden to Ren to explain the request, identify the exercise’s author, or prove he lacked knowledge. The court would weigh the prosecution’s evidence only after the remaining testimony and arguments. The defense could still challenge the clerk’s recollection, the school’s identity procedure, and the link between the instructor packet and the wording on the signed page.

The order separately addressed the phrase “source key.” Because no access record showed Ren had seen the retained instructor packet, the prosecution could not state as fact that he knew its exact contents. It could argue that the request’s wording and context supported an inference, and the defense could argue that ordinary testing practice supplied another explanation. The judge would not resolve that dispute in a preliminary motion.

The ruling also narrowed what the prosecutor could say. The court barred any suggestion that the four logged sets had gone to Nia or that she had received a particular cover. It barred a claim that the signed request proved Ren designed the original exercise. The prosecution could argue that his instruction altered how the placement office presented it; it could not convert the unknown author into Ren by implication.

Defense counsel asked the judge to exclude the livestream from any later argument about guilt. The court renewed its earlier order. The recording documented public service of the warrant and the camera operator’s actions, not the underlying charge. Neither the prosecution nor the defense could use the clip to imply that Ren’s composure, silence, or lack of resistance demonstrated innocence or guilt.

The judge then reviewed the remaining schedule. The prosecutor had one distribution witness left: the clerk who entered the four-set issue and “key retained” notation in the R-12 register. She was not the retired front-desk clerk who had testified about receiving the signed request. The defense reserved cross-examination on the sequence of the register entries and how the student packets were assembled. After that testimony, the prosecution would close its case.

The court asked whether the sealed destination index was needed. The prosecutor said no. The defense said it should remain sealed unless the prosecution later relied on a specific recipient. The judge agreed. The separate index would not be opened merely because Nia’s name had appeared in the public discussion. Her case file remained outside the proceeding.

During lunch, the district’s public-information office drafted a summary of the ruling. Its first version said the judge found evidence that Ren “intended to mislead.” Defense counsel objected. The actual ruling said a fact-finder could draw that inference, not that the judge had drawn it. The office corrected the statement before publication and kept both drafts in the change log.

Nia added the correction to the public chronology’s source notes. She did not summarize the ruling as progress toward conviction. Her note said the court had denied dismissal and left intent unresolved. The archive linked to the judge’s written order and the redacted exhibits.

Outside, Ren’s counsel declined to answer whether he would testify. The question was premature. The defense had not yet decided whether to call witnesses, and Ren had not made a statement in court. The press repeated the question in several forms, but counsel gave the same answer.

When the hearing resumed, the prosecutor called the distribution clerk. She had worked in the placement office before transferring to records. The file showed that she had entered the “key retained” notation beside the four student-set count. She confirmed the initials were hers. She had not received Ren’s signed request and could not identify the person who delivered it. Her role was to prepare and log student packets after the front desk routed approved instructions.

The judge asked what the clerk could identify without looking at the file. She said she could identify the request’s route through her desk, but not its author. Then she looked at the signed page displayed on the court monitor and stopped.

“There was another sheet with it,” she said. “I remember a page that wasn’t in the school file.”

# Chapter 175: The Missing Sheet

---

The distribution clerk said the extra page she recalled was an attachment to the packet-preparation materials, not a signature page. She had not received Ren’s request and could not say that the attachment had accompanied it at the front desk.

The court asked her not to guess at its contents. She had been called to establish how the request entered the placement office, and the parties had not received notice of a newly recalled document. The judge ordered a brief recess so counsel could confer and the clerk could review her prior statement without seeing any unproduced record.

The prosecutor told the court the school had searched the placement file and found no attachment. The defense said the distribution clerk’s recollection made a broader search appropriate, but not an assumption that the page was connected to Ren’s form. The clerk’s contemporaneous register entry recorded “key remains office,” but did not mention a second sheet. The prosecutor asked to continue with her existing testimony and reserve the recollection for a properly disclosed search.

The judge agreed. The clerk could describe the request and the routine note she entered. She could not tell the court what the missing page said or treat it as evidence. Her memory was not a substitute for a document that no one could locate.

The prosecutor returned to the four-set register. The distribution clerk confirmed that her initials beside “key retained” meant she had placed the instructor materials back in the locked cabinet after candidate copies were prepared. She had not counted the pages in every student set. She could not say which person carried each packet away. The four-set total came from the register, not her memory.

The defense asked whether the recalled page had been attached to the cover-change request when the front desk routed it to her.

“I remember an attachment with a table,” the clerk said. “I can’t tell you that it was the key. It might have been a routing sheet.”

“Did you read it?”

“I looked at the top line, not the contents.”

“Do you remember seeing Mr. Aizawa bring it?”

“I remember the student at the desk. I remember the school card. I did not see him write the signature.”

The prosecutor asked whether the attachment was still in the office when the records were transferred.

The judge ordered both parties to submit a joint inventory of every page currently held under the R-12 serial. The order did not authorize a search of Nia’s private application or the sealed destination index. The school would search the original placement carton, the instructor-material cabinet, and the bound register’s adjacent pockets. Any additional page would be preserved, photographed in place, and disclosed to both sides before use.

The court also asked the distribution clerk whether she had made any notes at the time.

The prosecutor closed the state’s evidence subject to the court’s ruling on any recovered attachment. The defense reserved its right to reopen cross-examination if a document appeared. The judge accepted the closing with that condition and directed the school to report the search result on the record.

The ruling on the dismissal motion remained unchanged. The evidence admitted so far could support competing inferences about the instruction’s purpose. The missing sheet could not be used to strengthen either side until it was found and authenticated.

Outside the courtroom, a headline described the witness as having “revealed Ren’s secret attachment.” The public transcript showed no such revelation. Nia’s chronology group posted the exact sequence: the clerk remembered a page, could not identify it, and had no document to produce. The board linked the judge’s order to search under joint custody rather than speculate about what the page might contain.

Endo asked Nia whether the recalled sheet could concern the placement process. Nia said it might, but the clerk herself had been unable to identify it. It could be a routing form, key table, or something else. Nia declined to open her own placement records to help search. She had not consented to that, and the school had other records under the court’s limited order.

The search began after the hearing with the distribution clerk and the custodian present.

The distribution clerk was asked to stand back while the custodian inspected the cabinet shelves.

The school’s counsel watched the team compare the inventory with the cabinet index. Three folders used the R-12 code, but only one was the exercise file already disclosed. The other folders contained routine program forms and stayed sealed. Their presence showed that R-12 served multiple administrative functions; no one claimed those folders had anything to do with the exercise.

The custodian also inspected the register’s front pocket and spine, then compared the binding with the transfer photographs. The back cover had not been opened during the first inventory because the inventory listed bound volumes, not every paper sleeve attached to them. The search log marked that omission as a scope limit, not as proof that someone had hidden the sleeve.

In the register’s back cover, the custodian found a narrow sleeve that did not appear on the transfer inventory. It held one folded sheet and a blank receipt stub. The custodian did not unfold it. The judge’s order required the item to be photographed in place, sealed, and reviewed by both parties before any description was given.

The distribution clerk looked at the sleeve through the clear evidence bag.

The court would see it in the morning.

The evidence officer placed the clear bag inside a rigid folder so the sleeve would not bend during transport. The clerk signed only to confirm she had watched the bag sealed; she did not initial the contents or identify the paper. The custodian recorded the register volume’s cover, spine, and page count before it left the room. Each person received a copy of the custody receipt, and the original stayed with the sealed item. No one opened the fold, read the sheet, or compared its writing with Ren’s signature. The court had asked for preservation first and disclosure to both parties second. Whatever the page contained, it would not enter the record through an untested recollection.
