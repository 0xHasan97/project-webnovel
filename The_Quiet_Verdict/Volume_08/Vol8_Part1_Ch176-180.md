*Chapters 176 to 180 (Part 1 of Volume 8)*

**Current-State Recap: The Collapse**

Ren pleaded not guilty through counsel and remained silent. At trial, the judge found him guilty on one narrow placement-fraud count: knowingly submitting the signed instruction to remove “simulation” from the student cover and withhold the source key. The court did not find that he authored the exercise, read the instructor key, selected Nia, or that any student received a particular set. Four sets were logged as issued, not delivered to named recipients. The folded control sheet and blank receipt stub were unprovenanced and did not support the verdict. Nia’s private placement file and the sealed destination index remain separate. Sentencing is pending.

The court’s public findings address only the placement-fraud count. The archive’s unresolved questions, Nia’s separate chronology, and sealed student records remain outside that judgment. Ren has not spoken during the proceedings, and no inference may be drawn from his silence. Sentencing is pending.

# Chapter 176: The Sleeve

---

The judge ordered the sleeve opened in the courtroom, not in the records room.

The sealed evidence bag sat inside a rigid folder. The court clerk read its seal number aloud, and the custodian compared it with the number on the receipt signed the previous night. The distribution clerk watched from the witness bench. She did not claim to recognize the sleeve. The records custodian cut the outer evidence tape, photographed the register’s back cover and the sleeve in place, then lifted it with paper forceps.

The folded sheet was a carbon copy of a table. The blank receipt stub lay behind it. Neither item bore a signature. The table’s heading read “R-12 student set control.” Four rows had columns for issue, return, and key retained. Three rows contained a check in the issue column. A fourth had a faint mark that the records specialist could not identify. The return boxes were blank. “Key remains office” appeared in the final column, but no name or staff number accompanied it.

The clerk who had recalled a table said the format looked familiar. She could not say this was the page she remembered. The office had used similar grids for other programs. She could not identify the writer or say when it had been placed inside the register.

The prosecutor asked the records custodian whether the serial matched the student-set register already admitted. It did. The paper size and carbon pattern were consistent with other forms used in the placement office. The custodian could not date the sheet more precisely than the period in which that office used the form. Its location in the back cover had not been recorded in the transfer inventory. No witness knew who had placed it there.

The judge asked whether the blank stub belonged to the same table. The custodian said it had been folded with the sheet but bore no matching serial. It had a perforated edge and two empty lines: “received by” and “date.” It could have been a detached receipt, a spare, or unrelated paper stored in the sleeve. The physical proximity was documented; a common origin was not.

The defense objected to admitting the table as proof of distribution. Its placement in the register was unexplained, and the row marks had not been attributed. The prosecutor said the matching serial and the form type made it relevant to the office’s recordkeeping, even if it could not identify a recipient.

The judge allowed the sheet to be photographed and marked for identification but reserved whether it would be admitted for the truth of its entries. The parties had not seen it before the previous night. Each would have time to inspect the original, question the custodian, and request an independent paper examination. The blank stub would remain a separate item unless later evidence connected it to the table.

Nia sat in the gallery. Her chronology group had not been invited to view the sealed item. She had chosen to attend the public session, but did not ask to see the exhibit. The judge’s order protected the student-destination index and her private placement file. She told Endo that the group would wait for the public ruling before adding a line to its chronology.

During recess, a reporter asked whether the page proved the four sets had gone to four candidates. Nia replied that the visible sheet did not name four recipients. The reporter asked whether the faint mark identified a fifth set. Nia said the examiner had not identified it as a check. Endo directed the reporter to the transcript rather than offer an interpretation.

The prosecutor returned to the table’s last column. Could “key remains office” refer to the instructor key previously admitted? It could. Could it refer to some other program key? The custodian said yes. The R-12 code was shared by administrative uses, and the table did not define the column. Was the page made at the time the four sets were issued? The office could not establish that. The marks might have been entered then or later.

The judge admitted the table only to establish its present condition, its wording, and its relationship to the register’s physical location. The entries would not prove that a candidate received a set or that the key was kept back on a particular date. The blank stub was admitted for the same limited purpose. Neither item identified Ren, Nia, a clerk, or an author.

The custodian placed both items in separate transparent sleeves. The court clerk recorded the point at which the paper was removed from the register, the photographs made, and each transfer between counsel and the evidence table. A new preservation image showed the fold lines and the empty receipt fields. No one added an annotation to the original.

At adjournment, the judge ordered the state records laboratory to compare the paper and carbon impression with the school’s routine forms. The examiner could assess format, sequence, and whether the faint marks were legible. The examiner could not manufacture a date or writer from an unsigned sheet.

The table made the four-copy register harder to summarize, not easier. Three issue marks could be read. The fourth remained unclear. Every return box was blank. The receipt had no recipient. The court’s temporary exhibit label said only: “R-12 control sheet, location and date unverified.”

The judge directed the clerk to read the label into the transcript exactly as written. The press gallery had already begun calling it a lost delivery record. The court would not adopt a description that implied the page had once been logged and later misplaced. The custodian testified only that the sleeve was physically attached to the register when the search team found it; no earlier inventory documented it there.

The court also asked whether the blank receipt stub’s position behind the sheet showed that the two had been stored together for a long time. The conservator said no. They could have been placed together the previous day or years earlier. The fold pattern did not indicate a fixed sequence. The blank lines carried no pressure impression that could be read as a name or date.

Defense counsel requested permission to inspect the spine and inner cover under magnification, arguing that the sleeve might have been attached after the register was transferred to the archive. The judge allowed a non-destructive examination by both parties’ conservators. They found adhesive residue consistent with office repair materials, but could not date it or distinguish original assembly from a later repair. The transfer photograph showed the back cover closed and intact, not the inside of the sleeve.

The prosecutor said the location and shared serial justified retaining the page for a later decision. The defense said preservation was proper but evidentiary weight had to remain low. The judge agreed to preserve it while reserving admissibility beyond the limited descriptive purpose. The unresolved origin would remain in the record beside the sheet itself, not in a footnote added after argument.

The prosecutor asked whether the four rows were intended to correspond to the four issues already recorded in the bound register. The custodian said the row count was the same, but the sheet contained no dates, initials, or cross-reference to the page where the four-set total appeared. It might have been a working tally for that session, or a template that had never been completed. The match in quantity could not settle which. The judge instructed the clerk to include the question and answer in the exhibit transcript, because the numerical resemblance alone was likely to attract attention beyond what it could prove.

# Chapter 177: The Mark

---

The examiner did not call the faint mark a check.

She had compared the sheet with three routine control forms from the same office. They shared the same printed heading and column widths, but the surviving examples came from different years. The copy found in the register used a paper stock the office had kept in general supply. Nothing on it narrowed the date beyond the period in which the form was in circulation.

Under magnification, the third-row mark resolved into two short strokes. They could have been a check, a correction, or pressure transferred from a sheet above it. The fourth row carried no readable entry. The carbon had been folded against another paper, and some graphite-like smudging crossed the issue column. The examiner could not determine whether those marks were made when the sheet was completed.

The prosecutor asked if the three clearer checks could be matched to the distribution register’s four issues.

“They are consistent with three issues,” the examiner said. “They are not individually numbered or dated.”

Could the table have been made after the register? Yes. Could it have been made before and used as a working sheet? Yes. Did the paper fibers show that it had been concealed in the binding for a long time? No. They showed only that it had been folded and stored there. The court clerk added the examiner’s answer to the record without converting “consistent with” into “same event.”

The defense asked whether any handwriting on the table matched Ren’s known school writing. There was no full signature to compare. The headings were printed. The marks were too brief and too faint for reliable attribution. The examiner had not examined Ren’s original writing samples for this purpose, and she did not claim an identification.

The source key was compared with the control sheet. Both used the R-12 code, but the code appeared on multiple program forms. The key had a catalog sleeve and an office inventory history. The control sheet had neither. The examiner could say they belonged to a related administrative vocabulary; she could not say they had been created together or handled by the same person.

Defense counsel asked the judge to keep the juryless hearing grounded in the item’s limits. The proceeding was being decided by the judge, but the distinction mattered just the same. The prosecutor agreed not to argue that the sheet proved four deliveries or identified Ren’s access to the key. The court admitted the laboratory report as a description of paper and marks, not as attribution.

The blank receipt stub underwent the same inspection. Its perforations matched common school-office stock. There was no surviving serial, date, or impression from a filled-in line. The examiner could not prove it had once been attached to the control sheet. The two had been adjacent in the sleeve when found; the sleeve itself had no inventory history before the discovery.

The judge asked whether the documents could still be considered together as circumstantial evidence. The prosecutor said the court could consider their location and matching program code. The defense said the connection was only a possibility. The judge ruled that the items could be considered as part of the register’s condition and the school’s file search, but not as proof that the entries reflected actual delivery. Their evidentiary value would be weighed with the full record.

Nia’s public group prepared a short update. Its first draft called the sheet “the missing distribution page.” Nia changed that to “an unverified control sheet found in the register’s back cover.” The word “missing” suggested the office had once logged the page and then lost it. No inventory showed that. The word “distribution” suggested completed delivery. The table did not establish that either.

Endo asked whether the group should state that the page was unrelated. Nia said no. The matching code and format made it relevant enough to preserve, but not enough to assign it a history. The revised update linked the court’s identification label and explained that the date, writer, and recipients remained unknown.

The prosecutor sought leave to ask the retired front-desk clerk whether she recalled the table. The judge denied the request. She had not seen it when she received the signed instruction and had no basis to identify it now. Calling her again would invite a guess based on the public hearing. The two clerks’ roles stayed separate: one accepted the cover request; the other logged student sets and remembered an unidentified attachment.

The prosecution rested its case on the authenticated signed request, the front-office ledger, the archive’s source certification, the retained instructor key, and the office testimony. The control sheet and blank stub remained marked for identification, with their limited findings. The prosecutor did not ask the court to treat the control sheet as the missing proof of a completed handoff.

The judge asked the defense whether it would present evidence. Counsel requested another day to decide whether to call the records custodian or rely on cross-examination and the prosecution’s exhibits. No one asked Ren to speak. The sealed sheet was returned to the evidence room under the same logged custody.

The next court date would begin with the defense’s decision. The new paper had a number, a table, and three readable marks. It still had no author, no reliable date, and no name in the receipt field.

Before the courtroom cleared, the judge asked the examiner to state the distinction once more for the transcript: a form can be consistent with a practice without proving that the practice occurred in this instance. The examiner confirmed. The prosecutor did not object, and defense counsel asked that the laboratory’s qualification appear on the exhibit index as well as in the report.

The judge granted that request. The clerk added a notation that neither the form comparison nor the mark analysis identified a writer, recipient, date, or completed delivery. The notation did not alter the original sheet. It described the scope of the admitted report so a later reader would not mistake a technical comparison for an identification.

The parties received certified copies of the report and the new exhibit index. Each was given one day to file corrections limited to transcription errors. No party could add a new interpretation without seeking leave. The index of candidate names remained under seal, and the court reporter left the hearing with the same public record available to everyone else.

The examiner also clarified that the matching R-12 code was printed on the forms, not handwritten beside the marks. There was no distinctive stamp, punch, or ink sequence tying the page to the four-set register entry. The office had reused the heading across more than one placement cycle. That made the page compatible with the general file category, but it did not tell her whether this sheet belonged to the trial’s specific exercise. She had compared the available exemplars, not reconstructed a missing chain of custody.

The prosecutor asked whether the table’s “key remains office” phrase could still be read as consistent with the distribution clerk’s notation. The examiner answered that the phrase was legible and the forms used similar language, but meaning and provenance were outside her specialty. She could not say which key, what day, or what act the phrase recorded. The judge noted that a technically legible entry could remain historically ambiguous. The parties would receive the same unenhanced photograph so neither side relied on a contrast-adjusted copy alone.

# Chapter 178: The Index

---

The sealed destination index stayed sealed.

The prosecutor’s office submitted a request to have the school’s privacy officer review the four coded issues and report whether the register identified any individual recipient. The request did not ask for names. It asked whether the separate code key could confirm that a student had accepted one of the sets.

Defense counsel opposed even that limited review. The four-set register did not establish that the copies had reached candidates, and the state had already closed its case. A new search risked exposing Nia’s private placement file without a showing that it was necessary. The defense asked the court to decide the count on the admitted record.

The prosecutor argued that a neutral reviewer could answer a binary question without disclosing identities. The signed request explicitly referred to “the candidate,” and the state said the separate index might clarify whether the instruction was carried out. The judge asked whether knowing that any set reached any candidate was an element of the charge or necessary to prove Ren’s state of mind.

The prosecutor said it would be corroborative, not essential. The defense replied that a corroborative search was not enough to justify opening protected records after the state rested. The judge agreed with the distinction. The school would preserve the index but not search it for names or recipients on the state’s request alone.

The order did not say the index would never be examined. It said that any further application must identify a specific question, explain why the existing records could not answer it, and propose safeguards for unrelated student information. No request would be granted merely because a public discussion had attached Nia’s name to the exercise.

Nia learned of the motion through the docket. She did not file a personal objection. Her consent form already specified that the placement file was separate from the Lantern House chronology and any police inquiry. Instead, she asked Endo to confirm that the public group had not downloaded or retained any protected court documents. The archive checked the access log and found only the redacted exhibits already released.

A volunteer worried that saying nothing would make readers assume Nia was one of the four candidates. Nia said the group could correct that assumption without revealing her file: no admitted record identified the recipients. She would not answer a question the evidence could not answer by opening a private file she had not offered.

The group posted the exact court order with the sealed portions removed. Its note said the state had requested a limited review, the defense objected, and the judge required a more specific need before any privacy review. It did not say the index supported either side. Nia signed off on the language, then left the room for the remainder of the meeting.

The defense’s notice arrived the next morning. Ren would not be called as a witness. Counsel cited the court’s instruction that no inference could be drawn from silence or from the decision not to testify. The notice did not state whether Ren would make a later statement at sentencing. That question belonged to another proceeding and another day.

The judge scheduled closing arguments. The prosecutor would have to explain why the signed instruction and the source mismatch proved knowing misrepresentation, not just an ill-advised administrative edit. The defense would have to address why the instruction should not be read as a deliberate change to a student-facing label. Neither side could rely on an unidentified recipient to fill the gap.

Before the session, the clerk circulated a list of accepted and disputed facts. Both sides agreed Ren’s signature appeared on the school request and that the front-office ledger linked its delivery to his school identity. They agreed the exercise combined source records from different dates and that the cited case number pointed to a weather-station drill. They did not agree that Ren knew those facts when he submitted the request.

They also agreed that the retained key listed the separate sources, but no record showed Ren had consulted it. The distribution register recorded four sets issued from storage, but no individual receipt. The control sheet had three legible marks and an unverified date, with no recipient. The blank stub did not identify a transaction. Nia’s file and the sealed code index were not evidence.

A reporter asked Ishikawa if the case had weakened. He said he was not the prosecutor and would not predict the judge’s decision. The archive’s work was to authenticate the source record; the school’s work was to preserve its files. The court would decide the count under its own standard.

At the end of the day, Ren entered the courtroom with counsel and took his place. He did not look at the gallery or at the control sheet displayed on the screen. The clerk announced that the next session would hear final arguments. The candidate names remained sealed, and the question of what Ren knew remained open.

The judge’s written order on the index was distributed before the hearing ended. It explained that the state’s request was denied without prejudice, not because the index had been shown to be irrelevant, but because the proposed review lacked a sufficiently narrow evidentiary need. The state could not use a search to see whether a name might strengthen a case after resting.

Any later application would have to identify the field to be examined and the proposition it was offered to prove. It would also have to explain why the existing register and testimony did not answer that question. A neutral privacy officer could report only whether the field existed and whether the record was complete; names and unrelated placement details would remain redacted unless the court found a separate, specific basis to disclose them.

The defense asked that the order make clear that the court had not found the index empty or incomplete. The judge added one sentence: no factual finding was made about what the sealed index contained. The ruling concerned the request and the protection owed to private records, not the truth of either side’s theory.

Nia read the order and closed the public file. She had not seen the index and would not ask the privacy officer what was inside it. Endo offered to keep the group’s access log with the next update. Nia agreed, provided it showed only the redacted materials used and the dates they were published. Their process could be audited without drawing her personal file into the public chronology.

At the next status conference, the privacy officer reported that the index remained sealed and that no additional candidate data had been accessed. The report did not list names or disclose whether any row was complete. The judge accepted it as a preservation update only, not as evidence of the index’s contents. The prosecutor renewed no request. The defense asked that the return be docketed publicly in redacted form, and the court agreed.

A member of the press asked whether the officer’s report meant there had been nothing to find. Nia said the order authorized a preservation check, not a substantive search. The public note used that distinction. She would not let a blank public report be mistaken for a blank private record. Endo added a link to the court’s limits, then archived the reporter’s question separately from the evidentiary chronology.

# Chapter 179: The Arguments

---

The prosecutor began with the cover, not the case number.

The signed instruction told the placement office to remove “simulation,” present the chronology as a “public-source case study,” and keep the source key from the candidate. The prosecution did not claim Ren wrote the exercise or selected Nia. It argued that he knowingly changed the information a candidate would receive in a process that could lead to a supervised placement.

The archive’s certification established that the chronology was not one event in the cited file. It was assembled from a maintenance drill and a public bulletin from another date. The instructor key listed those distinct sources. The office’s practice treated the simulation label as a notice that the sequence had been constructed. By directing its removal while withholding the material that identified the separate dates, the state argued, Ren intended the placement decision to be made on a misleading presentation.

The prosecutor acknowledged the limits. There was no access record placing the key in Ren’s hands. The distribution register did not identify a recipient. The control sheet was unverified and would not be treated as proof of delivery. No evidence showed that Nia received any particular version. The case, the prosecutor said, depended on the signed instruction and the surrounding facts, not on the disputed paper or an assumed handoff.

Defense counsel stood with the request enlarged on the courtroom screen. The signed words did not say “tell the student this is a true disappearance.” “Public-source case study” was a phrase used on other school templates. Keeping a source key from a candidate was normal testing practice. The office’s own witness could not define what the writer meant by “key,” and no evidence showed Ren knew the instructor packet listed incompatible dates.

The defense argued that the state had proved an administrative decision, not a fraudulent purpose. It had shown the effect of the instruction, but not knowledge of the source mismatch. A bad label could be careless, strategic, or a routine edit. The court could not select the most damaging explanation just because the correction had been signed by Ren.

The prosecutor replied that direct proof of a person’s purpose was rare. A fact-finder could infer intent from the words chosen and the timing of an instruction. The instruction did not ask to keep all answer materials from the candidate; it singled out the source key, immediately alongside the request to remove “simulation.” The office was told to make the constructed chronology eligible for a supervised placement after an independent score. The combination, the state argued, showed more than routine testing.

The judge asked both sides whether the code-sheet discovery altered their arguments. The prosecutor said no. The unverified table could not supply the missing recipient or access evidence. The defense said it reinforced the need for caution: even a sheet found inside the register had no reliable date or identified author. The judge accepted both limits and said she would not treat physical proximity as proof of a transaction.

The defense added that the front-desk clerk’s identification had been made in court after she saw Ren at the table and had read his name in the ledger. Her recollection supported the contemporaneous entry, but the identification was not independent. The ledger, in turn, showed a school card was checked; the card itself was not retained. The court had admitted the records, but the defense argued their combined weight should not be exaggerated.

The prosecutor said the case did not rest on the clerk recognizing Ren’s face. The signed request, matching student number, and contemporaneous delivery entry supplied the identity link. The clerk’s uncertain recollection could be discounted without erasing the other records.

Nia waited in the corridor during the arguments. She was not a witness, and neither party mentioned her as the candidate. A reporter asked whether she agreed with the prosecutor. She said the court had heard the same source-bound summary she had published: the instruction was signed; the exercise combined records; knowledge and recipient remained disputed. She would not announce a verdict before the judge did.

The judge allowed each side a brief reply. The prosecutor said the court need not decide who designed the underlying exercise to find that Ren knowingly altered its placement presentation. Defense counsel answered that the prosecution had to prove the mental element of its charge, not merely that Ren had signed a document with consequences. The phrase “remove simulation” could show awareness of the label, but not necessarily awareness that the cited record was false.

The judge closed the hearing and said she would issue written findings. She would decide only the charged placement-fraud count. No conclusion about Hara, Lantern House, the anonymous receipt, the exercise’s original author, or Nia’s selection would appear in the judgment unless supported by admitted evidence and within the charge.

Before closing the record, she reviewed the agreed and disputed propositions aloud. The signature and the front-office identity entry were admitted. The source mismatch was certified. The key listed separate records. What Ren knew when he signed the instruction remained disputed. The parties agreed the register contained four issue entries but no named recipients. The table from the sleeve had no established date or author. The blank stub contained no completed receipt. The judge said those were not clerical details; they defined the limits of the question she had to decide.

The prosecutor asked the court to consider the source-key wording as circumstantial evidence of knowledge. Defense counsel asked the court not to treat the word “source” as proof that Ren had seen the list of source dates. The judge said she would consider what the request itself showed and would not assume unseen access. The prosecution could argue that the instruction’s phrasing supported an inference; it could not state that Ren read a document for which there was no access record.

The judge also confirmed the standard. The fact that an inference was reasonable did not automatically make it the only reasonable inference. The burden remained on the state to prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt. Ren was not required to identify the table’s writer, explain a blank stub, open Nia’s file, or offer an account of his own. The court would evaluate the evidence that had been properly presented.

The prosecutor asked for one day to submit a proposed findings outline. The defense would receive the same opportunity to respond, but neither filing could add facts beyond the admitted record. The court reporter would circulate the final transcript, including the examiner’s qualifications and the limitations placed on the newly discovered sheet.

The proposed findings outline could organize the parties’ arguments, the judge added, but it could not quietly replace the exhibits with a more certain story. If either outline described an inference as an established event, the opposing side could flag the sentence and cite the record. The clerk would preserve both versions with any correction. The prosecutor agreed; defense counsel asked that the same rule apply to public summaries. The judge said the court controlled its own docket, while each party remained responsible for accurately describing the decision once issued.

Ren stood with counsel. The court clerk gathered the exhibits, keeping the control sheet and blank stub in their own sleeves. The judge’s written decision would identify which facts she accepted, which inferences she drew, and where the evidence stopped.

# Chapter 180: The Finding

---

The written decision began with what it did not decide.

It did not identify the author of the three-clock exercise. It did not find that Nia had been selected before applying to Aster, that she received a specific student set, or that Ren had ever read the instructor key. It did not treat the control sheet or blank receipt stub as proof of delivery. It made no finding about Hara’s death, the Lantern House inquiry, or the anonymous receipt.

The judge then addressed the charge. The school’s signed request and front-office ledger established that Ren, using his verified student identity, personally submitted an instruction to remove “simulation” from the student cover and to withhold the source key. The judge credited the record’s identity link, while treating the front-desk clerk’s in-court identification as limited corroboration rather than a blind identification.

The archive’s certified source records established that the exercise’s supposed single chronology combined material from a weather-station maintenance drill and a later public bulletin. The instructor key listed those sources separately. The court did not find that Ren authored the exercise or had read the key. It considered those limits alongside the instruction he signed.

The question was whether the instruction knowingly misrepresented the student exercise in the placement process. The judge found that the cover change and the direction to withhold the source key were linked in the same signed request. The use of “simulation” on the original student-facing form was not decorative: the placement officer had explained that it distinguished a constructed chronology from one presented as a single event. Removing that notice while directing staff to withhold the source table altered what the candidate could understand about the task.

The defense’s ordinary-testing explanation was considered. The judge accepted that answer keys were commonly withheld. But the request named the “source key,” rather than only an answer sheet, and paired that instruction with removal of the simulation label in a placement process conditioned on an independent score. The court found that the combined wording and context proved beyond reasonable doubt that Ren knowingly submitted a misleading placement instruction.

The finding was deliberately narrow. It did not mean that Ren knew every mismatch in the source archive, designed the false chronology, selected Nia, or intended any harm to a candidate. It did not make the unverified control sheet authentic. It did not establish that any student received the altered cover. It found that his own signed instruction knowingly changed how the placement office was to present the constructed exercise.

The judge entered a finding of guilty on the single placement-fraud count. She stated that Ren’s silence and his decision not to testify played no part in the decision. The prosecution had the burden throughout. The ruling on the dismissal motion had not shifted it, and the verdict did not rely on the livestream or on public reaction.

Sentencing was set for a later date so both sides could file statements and address the statutory range. The order did not authorize any new search, and it did not convert the placement count into a finding about other deaths or investigations. Ren remained in custody under the existing court process, with counsel access unchanged.

Outside the courtroom, the district issued a summary. Its first draft called the verdict proof that Ren had created the exercise. The clerk removed that line before publication and linked the written findings. The final statement said only that the court found him guilty of knowingly submitting the cover instruction in the placement process; authorship of the exercise and candidate selection remained unestablished.

Nia read the decision with Endo at the archive. She did not call the result vindication. The judgment supported the limited claim that Ren had submitted the instruction and that the judge found its combined effect knowingly misleading. It did not answer the remaining questions in her chronology. She asked the board to attach the full findings, the prosecution’s evidence list, and the judge’s explicit limits together, so no summary could stand alone.

The board accepted the amendment. Nia kept her placement file sealed and renewed her consent to chair the public chronology group for another month. Her work would remain a record of sources and claims, not an extension of the criminal case.

The control sheet and blank stub remained in the evidence room. The court’s judgment did not rely on them. Their date, author, and connection to the register were still unknown. The next proceeding would address punishment for the count that had been proved, not the theories the public had attached to Ren’s name.

The court clerk distributed the written findings before posting the public version. Each page carried the redaction code used for the sealed destination index. No candidate name had been removed from the findings because no candidate name appeared in them. The distinction was recorded so readers would not infer that an identity had been disclosed or withheld as part of the verdict.

The prosecution filed notice that it would seek a sentence within the statutory range for the placement-fraud count. It did not request an enhancement based on Hara, Lantern House, or the disputed control sheet. Defense counsel requested the presentence materials and asked that the court prohibit access to unrelated student records. The judge directed the clerk to collect submissions through the docket and keep any protected attachments under seal.

Neither filing changed the finding. Sentencing would address only the consequences authorized for the count, with both parties permitted to contest relevant factors. Any fact offered for a greater penalty would require proper notice and an opportunity to respond. The judge specified that sentencing was not a second opportunity to introduce theories excluded from trial.

Ren’s counsel spoke briefly outside the courtroom. The defense was reviewing the findings and would decide whether to seek reconsideration or appeal after consulting Ren. Counsel did not characterize Ren’s silence as acceptance or protest. The decision not to testify remained protected and had not been used against him.

Nia’s group prepared a public chronology entry with two linked documents: the judgment and the court’s exhibit ruling on the sleeve contents. The first stated the narrow finding; the second explained why the control sheet did not establish delivery. Endo asked whether those links belonged beside the archive’s source-mismatch record. Nia said yes, if the chronology showed the court’s claim and its limits together rather than presenting one as a correction to the other.

The group logged the revision history, preserving the earlier wording, the date of change, and the citations used. Nia declined to write a personal response. The count concerned an instruction submitted by Ren; it did not settle who designed the exercise, what she herself had received, or why her name had circulated in public. She would not let the verdict answer questions it had not reached.

The district’s final summary repeated the distinction. It named the count and finding, then stated that the judgment did not establish exercise authorship or any particular candidate’s receipt. The board appended the order refusing to open the destination index on the prosecutor’s initial request.
