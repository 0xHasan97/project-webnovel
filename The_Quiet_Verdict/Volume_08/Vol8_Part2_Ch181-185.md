**Current-State Recap: The Collapse**

The trial has ended with a guilty finding on one placement-fraud count, based on Ren’s signed instruction to remove “simulation” and withhold the source key. The court did not find that he authored the exercise, read the key, selected Nia, or that any student set reached an identified recipient. Ren remained silent throughout trial; no inference was drawn. The control sheet and blank receipt stub found in the register sleeve remain unresolved and did not support the finding. Sentencing has not yet occurred, and no appeal has been filed. Nia’s private placement file and the sealed destination index remain separate.

# Chapter 181: The Term

---

The court gave Ren a term, not an explanation.

The sentencing hearing opened with the judge confirming the one count before her. The state had proved that Ren submitted the instruction to remove “simulation” from the student cover and withhold the source key. It had not proved that he wrote the exercise, chose Nia, or read the instructor packet. The court would not sentence him for those unproved matters, or for Hara’s death, the Lantern House inquiry, or the theories attached to the register sleeve.

The prosecutor requested the upper end of the custodial range. The signed instruction had altered a placement process, the prosecutor said, and the court had found the combined wording knowingly misleading. The state did not claim a student had been harmed or that a particular candidate received the altered cover. It argued that the placement opportunity itself was treated as a benefit tied to an independent score, and that the instruction interfered with a fair assessment.

Defense counsel asked for a noncustodial educational order. There was no prior adjudication in the file before the court, no proven recipient, no measured loss, and no evidence that Ren had designed the exercise. The defense acknowledged the finding and challenged the state’s description of its reach. The signed page could justify a consequence, counsel argued, but it could not become a substitute for every missing link in the case.

The judge reviewed the written factors one by one. The court considered the deliberate wording of the instruction and the trust placed in the placement office. It also considered Ren’s age, the limits of the count, the absence of a proven handoff, and the lack of evidence about any candidate’s response. No victim-impact statement had been offered by Nia, whose private file and participation remained separate. The prosecutor did not ask the court to treat her silence as evidence of harm.

The sentence was eighteen months in secure youth placement, with credit for time already held, followed by supervised return to education if the placement review found it appropriate. The judge said the term reflected the knowing alteration proved at trial. It was not a sentence for exercise authorship, for a death, or for an uncharged act. The judgment repeated that Ren’s silence and decision not to testify had played no role in the finding or the penalty.

The defense asked whether the custodial order would begin immediately. The judge said it would take effect upon the receiving center’s acceptance, subject to the ordinary administrative transfer. Counsel reserved all appellate rights. A notice of appeal could be filed within the time set by law; the court made no ruling on any future application for release.

Ren stood beside counsel as the clerk read the order. He did not speak. The judge asked whether counsel understood the filing deadline, and counsel answered for the defense. No one addressed Ren directly after that. The court did not invite a statement from him, and his silence remained exactly what the record said it was: no testimony.

Outside, the district’s first headline read “Ren Sentenced for the Exercise.” The clerk’s correction request cited the judgment’s opening paragraph. The official summary was amended to say “sentenced for knowingly submitting the placement cover instruction.” It did not mention the unknown exercise author or suggest a candidate had received a particular version.

Nia read the published order at the archive. She did not join the sentencing debate. Endo asked whether the group should add the term to its chronology. Nia said yes, with a citation and the judge’s stated limits. Their chronology was not a measure of whether eighteen months was enough. It recorded what the court had done and what evidence the sentence relied on.

The public version of the judgment contained no destination for Ren, only the effective date and the receiving authority. A separate transfer record would be filed under juvenile privacy rules. The court clerk stamped the order and closed the hearing.

The sentence had a length. Its route into custody remained on another form.

The clerk read the appeal deadline again after the judge left. Defense counsel requested a certified copy of the oral findings and the final sentencing worksheet. The prosecutor asked that the worksheet’s sealed juvenile fields remain outside the public docket. The judge’s assistant confirmed that the public copy would contain the statutory factors and the custodial term, while protected educational and health information would stay in a restricted file.

The court reporter corrected one phrase in the draft transcript. The judge had said the instruction altered the placement presentation; the first transcription read “altered the placement determination.” The correction was made before certification, with the audio reference noted on the errata sheet. Neither party objected. The difference mattered: the court had not found a specific candidate was admitted, rejected, or scored differently.

The prosecutor’s sentencing memorandum cited the placement process’s reliance on fair instructions, but it did not claim a measured loss. It asked the court to treat the deliberate removal of a disclosure as more serious than an ordinary paperwork error. The defense memorandum answered that the trial finding already accounted for the signed instruction. Repeating it as proof of a proven victim or completed handoff would enlarge the record beyond what the court had found.

The judge asked whether either party wanted to call a witness. Both declined. No candidate appeared, and no one offered Nia’s private file. The court did not ask the youth authority to speculate about how a hypothetical student would have interpreted the cover. The sentence rested on the count and the admitted evidence, not on an imagined impact statement.

A court officer delivered the sealed transfer packet to the clerk after the hearing. It contained the order, a subject number, and the receiving authority’s acknowledgment form. The officer logged the packet’s arrival at the clerk’s desk; he could not confirm that Ren had been moved from the courthouse. The form’s next field was blank pending acceptance by the facility. The clerk stored it in the restricted cabinet and did not describe its contents to reporters.

Nia’s group added a link to the judge’s complete public findings and removed a draft note that called the order “the end of the trial.” The verdict had ended the fact-finding stage, but the appeal and sentence remained active. Endo added two separate entries: “finding entered” and “sentence imposed.” The dates were close; the legal acts were not the same.

At the courthouse steps, a camera operator asked the district spokesperson to confirm whether the sentence was a conviction for “creating a fake disappearance.” The spokesperson read from the corrected summary: the judge found a knowing misrepresentation in the placement instruction. The case-number mismatch supported the context, but the court made no finding that Ren created the exercise or the underlying chronology. The operator asked the question again with different wording. The spokesperson repeated the same boundary.

The judge’s written order was posted after the clerk confirmed every public attachment matched the signed version. The first paragraph identified one count. The final paragraph denied any inference from Ren’s silence. Between them, the court listed the limits it had maintained. The public could disagree with the result, but the official document did not license a broader story.

The transfer packet remained behind the restricted cabinet door. Its acceptance line was still empty.

# Chapter 182: The Notice
