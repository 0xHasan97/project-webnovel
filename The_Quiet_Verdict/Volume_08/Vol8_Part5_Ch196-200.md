# Chapter 197: On Record

---

The record said Kuze Minato was dead.

That was not a sentence I could write from memory. It came from the hospital notice copied to Aster, from the headmistress’s announcement, from the public statements that followed, and from years of people repeating one another. The notice’s source chain ended at a liaison. The liaison’s basis was not in the surviving file. I knew the report had been made. I did not know that it described me.

I am Kuze Minato. I am alive. I am the narrator of this account.

Until this page, the story has kept outside me as well as outside Ren. I have not entered his thoughts because I never had them. I have written what I witnessed, what people later told me, and what records allowed me to reconstruct. Those categories are not interchangeable. Where a transcript exists, dialogue can be checked. Where it does not, a conversation is a reconstruction of the account, not a recording of every word.

I was at the banquet. I did not see the person at the bottom of the stairs after I left the school. I did not see the ambulance take that person away. I did not see the hospital liaison, the body, or the page that became the death notice. The earlier sentence, “Kuze lay at the foot of the final flight,” was written from the story the witnesses believed and the name the record supplied. It should have said that a person the room took for Kuze lay there. I cannot identify that person now.

I left through the service side while the catering gate was being held for a delivery. I remember a cart between me and the road, a worker’s hand on the latch, and the kitchen door closing behind me. I did not ask the worker’s name. I did not see who held the gate from the outside. The school’s later account book gives an eleven-minute interval, but its clock was not synchronized with the hall or camera. It is consistent with my memory; it does not verify it.

I had no permission to take the extra place card or the meal sheet. I did not know who had set either aside. I carried no copy of the guest ledger. The notebook that appeared on the stairs was not in my hands when I reached the road. Whether it had been carried there earlier, or whether the person found below had moved it, I cannot tell from firsthand knowledge.

I had left my jacket. The person on the stairs wore a dark school jacket, and from the hall the witnesses could not see a face. I did not arrange a substitute body. I did not know who had been found until I read the record afterward. The school’s report treated the figure, the patient, and my name as one chain. The surviving public documents do not establish how those links were made.

I did not go to the hospital. I did not hear the two calls to Aster. The timing of those calls came from the event log and the school’s later statements. I learned that the first call came at 11:14, then that the headmistress was told of a death at 11:38, and that the notice carried 11:31 as the recorded time. Those numbers describe different records and different events. They do not tell me who was on the stairs.

I went first to a room above a closed repair shop, where I had arranged to stay for one night. The key had been left behind a loose drain cover at the rear entrance. That arrangement was temporary, and I had no route from there to the hospital file or the police. I had food, a coat without the school crest, and cash I had saved from tutoring. I had not planned a life in hiding. I had planned a way to leave the banquet.

The next morning I used a public telephone to call the number on the envelope I had given Himeno. No one answered. I did not leave a message. I could not tell whether the line was busy, disconnected, or simply unattended. I did not call again. I had written “if I cannot return” because I understood that the letter might be read as a goodbye. I had not written that I was dead.

The city believed the notice. So did I, for a time, in the limited sense that I believed an official had reported it. I did not know what person had been examined or what source the liaison had consulted. The absence of a name on the underlying intake sheet was not proof that no examination had occurred. It was a gap. A gap can conceal a mistake, an ordinary administrative shortcut, or a deliberate act. I had no right to choose among them without a source.

The story before this point has used public records, statements, and reconstructions. I watched some events directly: the banquet, the doors, the hall, the people who stood near me. I learned other events through documents: the appellate opinion, the public agency bulletins, the correction notices, and the archive chronology. I did not hear the private conversations at the youth center. I did not know what was inside the sealed appendix. Any line that appears to place me in those rooms is an account assembled from what was later published, not my memory of being there.

The public chronology was a way to keep sources beside claims. I read it after Nia’s group opened the relevant entries to the public. It recorded her corrections and the documents behind them. The group did not give me her private placement file, the sealed student index, or any protected youth record. I have not seen them. I do not know whether my absence from those pages was deliberate or simply proper.

I kept no complete diary of the years after the banquet. I kept notes, dates, a few receipts and copies of public notices. Some pages were written on the day; others were copied later and labeled as copies. If I cannot distinguish them, neither can the reader. I will not turn recollection into a timestamp because a timestamp would make it look certain.

I did not know Ren’s real name then. I do not know it now. The name used in the school records was enough to identify the person everyone meant. I have never seen an unabraded source that supplies the other one. The line in the registry remains blank. I will not fill it with a guess, a rumor, or a sound someone says he almost heard.

The public report said Ren was dead. I had no evidence to challenge it. I had only a memory of him alive at the banquet, an unverified route out of the school, and a record chain that did not reach a face. I did not know whether the reported body belonged to him. I did not know whether he had escaped, been killed, or been moved somewhere I could not see.

The first complete clue I could add was my own: I left the school through the service gate. It gave the old open-door entry a use, but it did not explain who held it, what the camera recorded, or how the hospital acquired the name. I am telling you this now because the surviving public record cannot tell the part I witnessed.

I was outside the gate before the announcement. I heard no one call my name. Behind me, the kitchen door shut. The cart kept moving.

I was not close enough to follow without being seen. I did not try. My first task had been to learn whether the records could be obtained; I had not planned to confront anyone. My own notes did not appear in it.

I could have sent Nia the sighting and asked her to publish it. I did not. She had not asked me to identify anyone, and a public accusation could put her under pressure to open her private file or investigate the placement system on my behalf. Her consent had been specific to the chronology she chose to lead. I had no right to turn that work into my instrument.

The next morning, the board posted its annual review of access requests. It showed how many records had been released, withheld, or corrected, but not the names of requesters. The school gate ledger was listed among records newly scanned. The listing did not identify who had supplied it or who had viewed the image. It did not mention Kuze.

The public account of the banquet remained the same: a student was reported dead; the hospital notice reached the school; the school announced it; later archives could not establish the liaison’s basis or the identity procedure from the surviving files. My firsthand account added that I had left through the service gate while a delivery was being unloaded. It did not explain the body on the stairs or the phone call from the hospital.

There was no single moment when I became free. The school stopped searching because it believed me dead. The city continued to file my name in past tense. I rented a room under another name, paid for food, went to work, and learned how to leave a public place without being remembered. None of that made the death notice true. None of it proved the man I saw was Ren.

The distinction mattered to me because there was no safe way to approach him. If I was wrong, I could expose an innocent stranger. If I was right, he had already been declared dead, and every official route to ask about him ran through the same record that said he was gone. The name the clerk used was not his school name. I did not record it in the manuscript.

At the end of the month, the city hosted a public celebration of the appellate decision and the end of the placement case. A banner hung across the courthouse steps: “THE VERDICT IS FINAL.” The legal sentence was accurate in that the appeal had been affirmed and the mandate issued. The crowd read the banner as a declaration that the wider mysteries had ended too.

The district’s own statement corrected that interpretation. The ruling concerned one count and did not resolve the Lantern House inquiry, the identity of the exercise author, or the delivery of student sets. The correction was posted beneath the event photographs. It did not stop the celebration, but it gave the record a place beside the slogan.

I watched from across the street. The man with the new name was not among the speakers. I saw him at the far end of the crowd, still in a plain coat, watching the courthouse doors. He did not approach the podium or Nia. A person near him called the surname I had heard at the registry office. He turned at the sound.

A public event photograph captured the courthouse steps, but the man stood beyond its sharp focus. The image could not verify my view. I did not submit my notes to the city or ask for the camera footage from the street. A public camera might have shown who stood there, but its existence and retention schedule were unknown to me. My account remained a witness statement I had written for myself, not an authenticated record.

I could not tell whether the turn was recognition, habit, or simply the response of someone called. I did not cross the street. The crowd cheered when the banner was lowered, and the man walked the other way, carrying no sign and speaking to no one. The public called it a release. The record called it closure. I had only a face I thought I knew, moving under a name I had not earned the right to repeat.
