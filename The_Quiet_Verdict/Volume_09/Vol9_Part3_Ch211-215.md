# Chapter 211: The Source Page

---

The source page looked smaller than its transcription.

I was not at this viewing. The public condition note and archive record let me reconstruct the scene, but not what anyone privately thought.

The municipal clerk placed the partial circulation volume in its cradle and checked the binding before turning to the relevant leaf. Nia and Endo sat at the permitted table. A conservator remained by the door. The page had a crease near the gutter and a pale water mark, but no loose section. The clerk had not opened the binding or flattened it for imaging.

The line read “Temporary review—north wing; return pending.” The word “north” was legible. The date field was damaged at the first digit, and the borrower field had a smear across its center. The item field was blank, not torn away. At the far right, a faint bracket sat beside the entry. No initials appeared next to it.

Nia asked the clerk to read the whole entry, including the heading above the column. The heading was “Review status.” The words “return pending” therefore described a status in that column. They did not appear in a field titled “destination” or “custodian.” Endo asked the clerk to note that distinction exactly.

The clerk confirmed that the code at the top of the page was only partly visible. The first two digits matched the consultation index; the last remained uncertain. The date could not be reconstructed from the opposite page, which covered a different department and had no cross-reference. The original ink had faded unevenly. The conservator did not consider it safe to apply additional light or chemical treatment during the viewing.

Nia looked at the bracket and asked whether the city had a guide to marks used in that register. The clerk brought a separate procedure card from the same records series. It showed three examples: a bracket for a line carried forward to a later review, a circle for a checked entry, and a slash for a closed item. The card was not dated. It did not say every clerk used the marks identically.

The bracket beside the north-wing line resembled the carry-forward example. The clerk said that was a possible reading, not an authenticated interpretation of this particular mark. The procedure card came from the same general series but had no direct link to the page or the person who wrote it. Nia asked that the viewing note say “resembles” and not “means.”

Endo examined the other entries on the same page without copying their contents. Several had brackets in the review-status column. One had a circle, and two were blank. The clerk confirmed that the marks appeared across multiple departments. She would not identify the entries or borrowers because they were outside the requested code and contained unrelated information.

Hoshina waited in the public reading area. Nia told her that the line did not establish the item, date, or borrower and that the mark resembled a carry-forward bracket. Hoshina asked if it could mean that someone expected the plan back. Nia said the procedure card made that possible, but the entry itself still did not name a plan. Hoshina said the distinction was frustrating; it was also fair.

The clerk completed a condition note. It stated that the entry was physically present, the item field was blank, the date and borrower fields were damaged, the final code digit was not readable, and a bracket-like mark appeared in the review-status column. It described the procedure card separately. The clerk did not certify a transaction or an identification of the mark.

Nia read the note twice before signing. Endo asked her to add that the viewing confirmed the transcription’s visible words but did not establish the missing fields. The clerk made the addition and signed the revised note. Nia and Endo left with a certified observation, not an image of the page.

The city’s own archivist offered a short public statement for the catalog: the register entry could not be linked to a specific plan or borrower. The statement would not identify the adjacent entries. Nia accepted it. She did not ask the clerk to infer what the blank item field had once contained.

I read the statement after it appeared on the archive’s public page. I had not been inside the room and had not seen the leaf. The clerk’s signed note and the city’s statement were the sources available to me. They supported that the line existed and that its fields were incomplete. They did not support a story about someone carrying the drawing out of the building.

A local post claimed the bracket proved an overdue return. The post cropped away the column heading and placed the procedure card beside the register transcription. Nia asked Endo to respond with the full heading, the damaged fields, and the city’s qualification. She did not ask the platform to remove the post. The archive page linked the complete source description so readers could compare it themselves.

Hoshina’s editor asked if she wanted to run the bracket as a new clue. She said it was a possible reading of a common administrative mark, not a person’s signature. She published the city’s note with the headline “Register Entry Cannot Identify Plan or Borrower.” The article was shorter than the first draft and carried its correction history.

The group’s request log showed that the city had answered the question it could answer: the line’s visible wording and current condition. It had not recovered the date or the object. On the next line of the chronology, Nia wrote “status mark uncertain.” Beneath it, she wrote “no custodian named.”

The city clerk asked whether Nia wanted a second inspection after the conservator completed the condition report. The report would not restore the damaged text, but it could establish whether the page had changed since the first viewing. Nia accepted a follow-up viewing and asked that the report describe the book’s condition without proposing a cause for the damage.

Two weeks later, the conservator’s note arrived. The crease and water mark predated the current transfer to the municipal records room, based on the condition of the old backing paper. The conservator could not date them more closely. No fragment was found under the repaired hinge. The note said the missing characters could not be recovered without a level of treatment the archive did not consider justified for this volume.

The note also confirmed that the blank item field was blank in the visible writing area. There was no evidence of scraping or erasure there. The clerk cautioned that the line might have been completed on a continuation slip that did not survive, or the item might never have been specified. The physical page alone could not decide between those possibilities.

Nia asked the archive to add the condition note to the public catalog, while keeping the adjacent entries masked. The clerk agreed. The catalog now showed the transcription, the viewing note, and the conservator’s statement. A reader could see that some fields were damaged and another field was simply empty. Neither condition identified a missing plan.

Hoshina drafted an article saying the city’s register “lost the date but never recorded the object.” Nia asked her to reconsider “never.” The page did not show an item in its visible field; the continuation slip was not available. Hoshina changed the sentence to “the visible item field is blank, and no continuation slip has been located.” The correction preserved the possibility without implying that one existed.

Kagami said the difference between a missing field and an unfilled field was crucial. The date and borrower were damaged; the item field was blank. The code was partly obscured. Those were three different conditions, and each limited the conclusion differently. Endo added a legend to the source map: “damaged,” “blank,” and “unreadable” were not interchangeable labels.

The legend prompted a review of the Foundation’s dispatch file. Its recipient signature was blank, not obscured. The courier receipt had expired, not been located. The city consultation code was partly unreadable. The group had previously used the general term “missing record” for all three. Nia asked Endo to separate the statuses and note the distinction in the public index.

The updated source map showed: “recipient signature: blank on surviving copy”; “courier delivery record: unavailable under retention schedule”; “municipal index code: final digit unreadable”; “register date and borrower: damaged”; “item field: blank”; “continuation slip: not located.” The list did not make the mystery easier. It made each absence precise.

A reader wrote that a blank field was more suspicious than a damaged one. Nia replied that the record provided no basis for assigning intent to either condition. The blank could reflect routine shorthand or a separate record; the damage could have occurred in storage. The group could preserve both facts without explaining them.

The city clerk confirmed that no new search for a continuation slip had been made. The original request covered the partial register and its transfer manifest, not every filing box from the department. Nia filed a scoped request for any continuation slip indexed to the north-wing code, if such slips had their own register. She did not ask for all employee notes or every adjacent paper.

The office answered that continuation slips were usually attached to the page, but local practice varied. No separate index had been found for that year. The clerk would not search unindexed personal work files. The response clarified that a separate slip was possible, not that one existed or had been removed.

Hoshina asked whether the request should remain open. Nia said it could remain pending for the office’s limited search, but the chronology should not keep the whole site-plan question in a pending state. The group’s next step would be to wait for this response and the contractor closeout inquiry. Other work could proceed without resolving the register.

The archive’s second viewing note was posted. It said the page’s visible wording had not changed between examinations and that the conservator found no recoverable text. It did not say the city had exhausted every possible record source. Nia linked it beneath the first note and closed the page-level inspection task.

The source did not identify an object, a borrower, or a final destination. It did identify what the group had actually seen, what the page had lost, and what it had never stated. Nia’s public summary ended with the sentence she had written in the margin: no custodian named.

# Chapter 212: The Duplicate

---

The same index card appeared in three places on the public page.

I reconstructed the review from the public audit log, posted revisions, and approved minutes; I was not in the room.

Endo noticed it while preparing a source audit. One copy came from the municipal archive’s spreadsheet. Another was embedded in Hoshina’s six-year-old article. The third appeared in an online discussion that cited Hoshina’s article as corroboration. The three links looked like independent sources until Endo followed the citation chain back to the original card.

The card itself was one record. Hoshina had quoted it years earlier, and the discussion had quoted her. Neither the article nor the post independently verified the card’s description. Endo placed the links side by side and asked Nia whether the public chronology should count them as three citations or one source with two repetitions.

“One source, two repetitions,” Nia said. She asked Endo to preserve the old links but connect them to the archive card. The site would display a note explaining that the article and discussion derived from the same catalog entry and were not separate confirmation. Endo could not alter Hoshina’s original article, so Hoshina would have to add the correction there herself.

Hoshina read the source map in silence. She had once described the repeated references as “multiple records,” though she had meant multiple publications. The phrase had encouraged readers to think the card had been independently confirmed. She asked the editor to change it to “multiple references to one record.” The editor agreed and kept the old phrase in the revision history.

The correction was not an admission that the card was false. It remained a municipal record, and the viewing had confirmed its visible words. It meant only that three appearances of the same description did not add three layers of corroboration. Nia added that explanation under the chronology rather than deleting the earlier references.

Kagami reviewed the source map and asked whether the contractor’s invoice and the city’s consultation card were independent. They came from separate custodians and described different stages, but both used the same parcel code supplied in the original request. The invoice supported a paid order for a measured plan; the city index supported a consultation code. Neither record said the plan reached the city. Their separate origins did not prove the events were connected.

Hoshina said a newspaper article based on her notes was not a new source either. The group had cited it as a record of what she had reported, not as confirmation of the underlying events. Nia asked Endo to label it as a publication and link the documents behind it where available. A source map should reveal dependencies, not inflate them.

The public page’s old chart had used solid lines between the contractor invoice, the Foundation’s dispatch instruction, and the city consultation card. Endo changed them to separate source boxes with dotted connectors labeled “possible relationship.” The line from the invoice to the prepared-copy entry remained dotted because both came from a contractor file but did not include a delivery receipt. The archive card sat apart from them.

Wataru worried that dotted lines looked like a claim the group had not made. Kagami said a dotted line was still a visual suggestion. They considered removing the connectors entirely. Nia decided to retain them only where there was a shared parcel number or date range, and to write the basis next to each. The chart would not use arrows unless a record showed direction.

A reader sent a correction about the old article’s date. Hoshina had written that the Foundation requested the plan in May; the public record showed the request was filed in June. The error came from a draft date in her notebook, not from the archive. Hoshina checked the original publication and found the line in two versions. She corrected both and stated that the public record—not her note—was the source for the June date.

The chronology now had a visible change log: index card transcribed, article repetition identified, date corrected, and chart connectors revised. The corrections did not produce a new custodian. They made the record of how claims had grown easier to inspect.

I followed the source map from the public page. The city card led to Hoshina’s article, then back to the same card. The loop had looked like a chorus until the references were opened. It was one voice repeated by people who believed they were passing along something confirmed.

At the next meeting, Hoshina said she had spent years gathering documents and had sometimes confused the number of times a statement appeared with the number of sources behind it. She did not withdraw the whole investigation. She corrected the places where repetition had looked like corroboration and kept the records that stood on their own.

Nia asked each participant to check one source chain and report any dependency they found. Ishikawa checked the public transport notices and confirmed that the passenger-count correction and the examiner’s identification came from different offices but both relied on a protected underlying record not available to the group. He added that the two notices could not independently establish the identification method.

Himeno declined the placement-chain review. She said she did not want to revisit the student file or the trial records. Nia thanked her and assigned the task elsewhere without asking why. The refusal remained in the minutes only as a declined assignment; no private file or explanation was requested.

Endo’s audit checklist asked three questions for every entry: what is the source, what did the source directly establish, and what other citations depend on it? Nia added a fourth: who has permission to make it public? The group adopted the checklist by agreement. No vote made the old chart true or false; the source map made its construction visible.

The checklist required one more review before it went online. Nia wanted someone outside the group to try to break the source map—find a loop, a missing qualification, or a claim that lacked a public source. Endo proposed the library’s volunteer records clinic. The clinic accepted public-history projects but had no connection to the archive or the police.

The clinic coordinator replied with a date and a condition: they would review citations, not investigate the mystery. Nia accepted. The source audit had not found the plan. It had found a way to stop echoes from being mistaken for witnesses.

The review also found that the city’s “copy consulted” entry had appeared in a local newsletter before Hoshina’s article. The newsletter cited a meeting agenda, not the archive card. Endo retrieved the agenda from the library’s public collection. It listed the north-wing consultation as a discussion topic and attached the same city code, but did not say a drawing was present. Hoshina’s article had cited the newsletter, then later versions added the archive card. The earliest published reference was not independent proof of a consultation with a physical plan.

Hoshina checked her notes from the time. She found a clipping with a handwritten “copy?” beside the agenda item. Her article had treated the code as a copy reference because the archive index later used the phrase “copy consulted.” The newsletter itself had not made that claim. She said the two descriptions had merged in her memory over years of reporting.

The correction touched the start of her public story. She asked the editor to preserve the original article but add a notice at the top: the newsletter recorded a consultation topic and code; the archive card separately used “copy consulted”; neither source confirmed a physical drawing or recipient. The earlier wording had overstated the link.

The editor asked whether the correction should apply to the headline, which said “The Plan at City Hall.” Hoshina said it depended on the same inference. She changed it to “A City Code and an Unresolved Plan Request.” The archived headline remained visible in the revision log.

Nia did not direct Hoshina’s editorial choices. She reviewed the chronology entry and confirmed that it linked to the two sources separately. Hoshina retained control of her article. The public index could describe the correction, not approve her publication.

A reader accused Hoshina of retracting the investigation after years of pressure. She replied that she was narrowing one claim, not abandoning the records. The contractor invoice still documented a charge for a measured plan. The Foundation’s correspondence still included a dispatch instruction. The city index still recorded a consultation code. What fell away was the assumption that the city entry confirmed a copy of that specific plan.

Kagami asked Endo to show the source dependencies. The newsletter agenda connected to the code; the archive card connected to “copy consulted”; the article had borrowed from both at different times. The source map now showed two branches and a dashed relation between them. No branch ended at a recipient.

The library volunteer who audited the map found a blog post quoting the newsletter and citing Hoshina’s article as a second source. Both citations traced back to the agenda and later card. The auditor marked the blog as derivative, not independent confirmation. Nia recorded the citation path without linking the post on the public summary.

Hoshina wrote to the blog author with the source map. The author replied that separate links had looked like separate sources. They changed the post to say “two references, one underlying agenda and a later archive card,” preserving the correction history.

The source audit had a cost. Hoshina’s article was less decisive, and readers could no longer count repeated descriptions as corroboration. But the contractor records remained intact. The city consultation had not vanished; its meaning had narrowed. No one had found a document saying the measured plan reached the city.

At the meeting’s end, Nia proposed that every repeated citation carry a lineage note. The participants agreed. Hoshina asked that the rule apply to her own publications too. Endo ran the audit across the other public entries and found that three news outlets quoted the same transport notice. They were separate publications of one official statement, not three independent confirmations.

The source map went online with the corrected article, agenda, and archive card. It said the two municipal references did not prove a specific drawing. The public could still follow the inquiry, but could no longer mistake an echo for a second witness.

# Chapter 213: The Volunteer

The former records aide asked to remain unnamed before she gave a statement.

I learned this through the library clinic’s public statement and approved minutes; I did not attend the private interview. The scene is reconstructed only to the extent those records support.

Her message arrived through the library’s volunteer records clinic, not through Ishikawa or the archive. She said she had worked temporary shifts in municipal engineering records many years earlier and believed the phrase “return to originating series” was a filing instruction. She did not claim to remember the north-wing file. She wanted to correct the group’s public explanation but did not want her employment history posted online.

The clinic coordinator asked Nia to respond through the clinic’s neutral address. Nia wrote that the group would accept a general statement if the speaker wished to give one, but would not use it to identify the person who handled the north-wing record. She asked the volunteer to say whether she had seen the specific card, the procedure card, or only similar forms in other files.

The aide agreed to a recorded interview at the library, with the recording kept private unless she later approved a transcript. She chose a seat near the open doorway and asked Endo to take notes instead of recording. Nia agreed. The aide could stop at any point, decline a question, or review the wording before anything was shared.

She said she had worked at the municipal records desk for two summers, filing requests and returning folders to the series from which they had been pulled. “Return to originating series” was a phrase she remembered seeing on routing slips. She could not tell whether the north-wing index card used it in that ordinary sense. She had not handled that card and did not recognize the code.

Nia asked whether the phrase ever referred to a physical drawing. The aide said it could have been written on a folder containing a drawing, but the phrase described where the folder belonged afterward. It did not say whether the drawing had been loaned, copied, or shown. She said clerks sometimes used the same words in different contexts, and the form’s column headings mattered.

The statement fit the series guide’s definition, but it did not authenticate the specific entry. The aide had no logbook, note, or work roster from those shifts. Her memory came from repeated office practice, not from the north-wing transaction. Nia asked Endo to record that distinction as part of the response, not as a footnote.

She changed “the phrase meant a folder was filed back” to “the phrase was often used for filing folders back.” She added that she could not exclude other uses. Nia accepted the change. The final statement was in the aide’s words, signed with a pseudonym, and accompanied by a note that the clinic had privately checked her employment dates.

Hoshina asked whether the employment verification could be summarized publicly. The aide consented to the clinic confirming only that she had worked in the records unit during the relevant decade, not the exact months or job title. The group would not publish her full name or personnel file. The clinic’s confirmation supported her general opportunity to learn the practice; it did not prove that her memory of the phrase was accurate in this instance.

Kagami asked why the group should publish an account that could not identify the specific transaction. The aide said she was not offering a transaction account. She wanted readers to know that the words did not necessarily mean a drawing had been returned to a person. If the group found the source form, its columns would matter more than her recollection.

Nia said the statement could be useful if presented as a limit on interpretation rather than proof of filing. The aide approved that description. She did not ask the group to name the archive clerk or speculate about who wrote the pencil note. She asked that her pseudonym be used consistently and that no one attempt to identify her from the wording.

The library clinic’s coordinator reviewed the consent form. It permitted a written summary, not the private interview notes. The aide could withdraw the summary until publication. She read the final draft once more and initialed each page. Endo sealed his notes in the clinic envelope and returned it to the coordinator, who would retain it under the library’s ordinary schedule.

The public statement said a former municipal records aide, identity privately checked by the volunteer clinic, remembered the phrase as commonly used for filing folders back into a series. She had not handled the north-wing card and could not identify the item or transaction. The statement did not say the plan had been returned or remained at the city.

I read the statement on the public chronology. It was the first account in the current review that came from a person rather than a catalog, but it did not fill the blank field. The aide had given the group a distinction she remembered from work. The card still lacked a borrower and item.

A commentator asked whether anonymity made the account impossible to verify. The clinic replied that it had checked the aide’s relevant employment history but could not independently verify her memory of routine wording. Nia linked the clinic’s consent note and stated that readers could weigh the limits themselves. The group did not claim the statement was equivalent to a signed contemporaneous record.

The aide’s message had prompted a new question: whether “return pending” appeared on a routing slip or only in the register’s status column. The city had already shown the column heading. Nia asked the records office whether a blank form from the same series survived. The request concerned the form layout, not the identities of staff or users.

The archive replied that it held a blank template from the next revision of the system. It could show the headings but not the older form’s marks. Nia booked a public viewing. If the template differed, the group would say so. The aide’s memory had opened a procedural question, not supplied an answer to the old entry.

At the end of the meeting, the clinic coordinator asked the participants whether they wanted the volunteer program to continue. Hoshina said yes, provided contributors were not treated as leads to be chased. Ishikawa agreed that no one should convert a general memory into an accusation. Nia said every voluntary account would be scoped, attributed, and offered a chance to withdraw before publication.

The coordinator accepted the rule and asked for it in writing. Nia added it to the group’s consent page. The new procedure required the contributor to say what they had seen directly, what they remembered generally, and what they had learned after reading public coverage. The aide’s statement had prompted the safeguard before anyone else used the channel.

The clinic coordinator asked the aide whether she wanted to withdraw her statement after reading the public summary. She said no, but wanted the general-office context to remain clear. She had not recalled the north-wing file and did not want her routine work treated as memory of a particular transaction. Nia asked the coordinator to record that distinction, not to press her for more detail.

Nia said the group would not publish a person’s refusal as a clue. The public note could say the contributor declined further questions, without implying why. Hoshina removed a draft sentence calling her “the only witness to the old system.”

The city clerk and conservator remained the only people who had inspected that page, and neither attributed it to anyone. The aide’s account stayed in the general-procedure section.

A listener asked whether Nia would release the private interview notes to independent reviewers. Nia said the notes belonged to the library clinic under the participant’s consent. The clinic’s public summary stated that contact had remained through the coordinator and that the aide had declined follow-up. It did not disclose her private reasons. Nia asked that the public note stop there, and the group did not infer a motive from the boundary.

The city archive’s blank-template request arrived as Endo updated the consent log. The form would be available for supervised viewing the following week. Nia filed it under municipal records, not volunteer testimony. The contributor’s recollection did not prove what the form would show.
