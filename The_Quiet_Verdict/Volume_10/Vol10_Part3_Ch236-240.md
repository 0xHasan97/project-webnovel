# Chapter 236: The Correction

---

The first correction after the meeting was not about the source map. It was about a sentence in a headline. The local paper had written that “witnesses cleared the man calling himself Ren.” Hoshina objected in a note sent to the editor and copied to the chronology group. She said no one in the room had authority to clear anyone. Her own statement said only that she had seen no contact from where she stood. The editor published the correction online, below the original headline, and left the headline in place until the next print edition.

I read the corrected version in the paper’s public archive. It said the statements did not describe an observed attack; they did not determine cause, identity, or innocence. The difference was one line of type and a paragraph of explanation. The comments below it treated the correction as a retreat. Several readers claimed Hoshina had changed her account. She had not. The original wording had changed the scope of her account for her.

The group met by written exchange because the library had closed the room to public sessions while the emergency review remained active. The approved minutes said each contributor could revise or withdraw their own statement. No one could revise another person’s words to make the set sound consistent. Endo circulated the rule at the top of the page, then asked for objections before the custodian posted an update.

Wataru wanted one short paragraph explaining that the room had not cleared the attendee. He proposed: “No finding about identity or cause was made.” Kagami said that sentence was accurate but would be quoted without the rest. He recommended adding the limits of the sources: the public chronology had not received police material, and its witnesses were not asked to certify every moment. Wataru said longer language gave people more places to misread. Hoshina answered that a short sentence could mislead more efficiently.

No one won the argument by being louder. They approved two paragraphs: one listing what the group could say, one listing what it could not. The opening sentence said the attendee made a verbal claim to be Ren Aizawa. The next said it was not verified. A separate sentence described the statements as individual accounts, not a single continuous observation. The final line said the cause of Nia’s death was not stated in the public notices available to the group.

A reporter asked whether the group’s minutes could be read as an alibi. Endo declined to interpret them. He said the minutes were evidence of what the group recorded, not a certificate that the record exhausted what occurred. The sentence sounded dull in the audio transcript, but it survived the cut for length. Hoshina sent the reporter the link to the correction rather than an additional comment.

The temporary custodian received a request to remove one contributor’s statement from the public page. The contributor had approved the wording at first and changed their mind after seeing it excerpted online. The request did not require the group to decide whether the reason was good. The custodian removed the statement and replaced it with a note that an authorized account had been withdrawn at its author’s request. The page retained no copy of the words.

That change made the surviving record less complete and more honest. It also meant the public could no longer compare all the accounts that had first appeared. One journalist called the withdrawal suspicious. Hoshina’s response was that consent did not become irrevocable because strangers had begun reading. She did not identify the contributor or describe the withdrawn sentence.

The clinic sent its own notice about the card. The person who had authorized a limited description now withdrew permission for the sentence to remain on the public page. The clinic did not tell the group whether the person had written the card, sent it, or merely claimed to have done so. The custodian removed the sentence and replaced it with: “A card was briefly described with permission; the description is no longer authorized for publication.” The card itself stayed sealed under the preservation request.

Endo asked if the map should show the removed description as a deleted source. Nia had not specified what to do if consent changed after a meeting. The group did not pretend she had. Kagami suggested a generic note in the change log, without the card’s text or the person’s reason. Hoshina agreed, provided the note did not imply the group had ever authenticated the card. They added only that an authorized public description had been withdrawn.

The revision made the source map less satisfying to anyone searching for a single story. It also preserved the distinction between a source existing and the group having permission to publish it. The blank remained blank. The event log kept the attributed identity claim because the claim had been made in a public session and had been included in the approved minutes. The card’s wording disappeared because its permission had been withdrawn.

A message board soon reproduced the old sentence from a screenshot. The platform marked it as an image supplied by an unknown account; it could not confirm who had taken the screenshot or whether it showed the original public page. The clinic asked that the image not be circulated. The request did not erase what had already been copied, and the group did not ask the platform to certify the sender.

The custodian’s final revision note was brief: “One authorized description withdrawn; no replacement source received.” I read that phrase in the public log and thought of how quickly a missing line was turned into a clue. The log did not say why the permission changed. It did not say who had held the card, or whether the card and the meeting had been planned together. It said the group no longer had permission to repeat a sentence.

The next printed edition corrected the headline but kept the photograph of the library entrance. The caption did not name the attendee. Under it, the paper quoted Hoshina’s narrow statement and her refusal to certify what she could not see. By then, the correction had become the most widely shared part of the page, stripped of the paragraph that explained it. Accuracy traveled too, but not always with its boundary.

The group closed the comment channel for a day after threats began appearing under the witness statements. The library’s public notice said the pause applied to the comment feature only; no source or statement had been removed by the library. The chronology remained online. A note at the top asked readers not to contact contributors through private addresses.

Wataru asked if leaving the page open helped Nia’s work or simply provided a surface for people to argue over her death. Endo said the custodians could not answer for her, but they could preserve the limits she had approved. The vote was close. They kept the page available with comments disabled and the withdrawal log visible.

The page was not the only place her rules survived. Each public copy still showed the change history. The difference between a corrected entry and a withdrawn one stayed visible without restoring private text. The correction did not make the witnesses more certain. It made their uncertainty harder to misquote as a verdict.

At the bottom of the updated page, one line remained unchanged: “No further source was received.” The next morning, the library added a timestamp beneath it. Someone had submitted a new item overnight, but the public log named no sender and described no contents. It said only that the submission had been placed in a restricted review queue pending consent.

# Chapter 237: The Notice

---

The new submission was not an answer. The library’s public notice said an item had arrived through the chronology’s ordinary channel and had not been opened. Its sender had requested a restricted review, but the request did not itself give permission to publish the contents or identify the sender. The custodian logged the envelope’s receipt and stopped there. No one in the group was told what it contained.

Readers treated the entry as if a new witness had appeared. One account said the envelope proved the attendee had arranged the meeting. Another said it contained a note from Nia. Both descriptions were invented. The public notice did not say it was a letter, a record, or even paper. The clinic declined to confirm whether the submission had passed through its office. Its privacy notice said only that it did not discuss individual contributors.

I had no way to see the object or learn who sent it. The only information available to me was the notice and the custodian’s later public correction. The envelope was retained for a short period under the library’s submission policy, then returned unopened when the sender failed to complete the consent form. The return did not establish that the address was real or that the sender had received it.

The correction included no photograph, postmark, handwriting description, or identifying details. A reader asked why the group would list an envelope without proving it existed. The custodian replied that the intake register recorded the act of receipt; it did not certify what was inside. That distinction was familiar from the old counterfoil and the sealed maps. A label could describe a claim, not verify the contents.

It did not identify a cause, announce a charge, or confirm the attendee’s identity. It also did not refer to the chronology group’s documents. The public language was narrower than the headlines and no more satisfying.

Another asked whether the official identification of the transport occupant as Ren Aizawa had been withdrawn. The briefing did not claim they were the same person.

Wataru asked that the chronology page avoid copying the official statement into its map because the map concerned a separate historical source review. The submission queue remained empty after the envelope was returned. The clinic’s withdrawn card description remained withdrawn. The public incident page showed the meeting, the authorized accounts, the later correction, and the scope of the official notice. It did not include the sealed card, the unverified envelope, police interviews, or Nia’s medical records. The page was a record of permission as much as a record of events.

The next challenge came from an editor who wanted a single portrait of the attendee. The photograph taken at the library entrance showed a person at a distance, but the photographer’s metadata was missing. The event’s registration policy did not permit identification from an image. The person in the picture could not be compared to the official transport record, which was not public. The paper did not print the image after the clinic objected that no one in it had consented to identification.

A separate article used the phrase “the man who returned from the dead.” Hoshina declined a request to endorse it. In her public note she wrote that a claim made in a room did not undo a death identification made through an official process, and an official record could not verify the speaker who used the same name. She said the contradiction belonged to the authorities, not to her map. It was the clearest statement she had made since the meeting, and the one most often shortened to “Hoshina refuses to say.”

Kagami read that summary and sent the group a correction. He said Hoshina had not refused to speak; she had refused to answer a question outside her evidence. The distinction mattered to him because truth was not only what a person said. It also included the edge where that person stopped. No one asked him to take over Nia’s chair. He did not offer.

The group received no public finding about how Nia died. The notice’s silence was not evidence for one cause over another. It did not settle whether the event had been planned, whether the attendee had been involved, or whether the room’s accounts had missed something. The official inquiry remained separate from the chronology, and the group had no authority to fill the gap with an interpretation.

A short correction was added after someone claimed the room had been continuously recorded. The library’s policy showed that recording was prohibited; no public record established a recording had been made. The moderator’s approved minutes said there was none. The revised page attributed that statement to the moderator rather than treating it as an independent technical audit. Even a negative could have a source and a limit.

That correction changed a popular timeline. A graphic had marked the exact second Nia stopped speaking, the moment the attendee stood, and the time help arrived. The source materials did not support those precise intervals. The graphic was removed by its maker after the moderator asked that the meeting not be reconstructed as a second-by-second transcript. Copies remained elsewhere, detached from the correction.

Hoshina’s editor requested that she write a final column on the unresolved death. She said she would write about the pressure to turn an unfinished record into a satisfying one. The editor asked for a conclusion. She said the conclusion was that the sources did not support one. The column was published under her name, with no confidential details and no claim to speak for Nia.

She described how each witness had been asked to speak only for their own view, and how the internet had compressed those accounts into a single witness. She did not repeat the card’s withdrawn sentence. She did not call the attendee Ren. She named the official identification as a public fact and the session claim as an attributed statement. Where the two met, she used a question mark.

The column received more attention than the source map. Readers praised Hoshina for proving the man innocent, then criticized her for refusing to prove him guilty. Neither reading matched the piece. The correction stayed linked beneath the article. It was not enough to stop the argument, but it gave the next reader a way to find the source boundary.

The authorities later confirmed only that the inquiry remained active. No public explanation accompanied the notice. The library kept its own page open, with a banner stating that it was not an investigation. The envelope was no longer in the queue; its contents remained unknown, and its sender remained unidentified. Its brief appearance added no fact to the map.

At month’s end, a local station asked whether the group would reopen the source session. The temporary custodian said there was no plan to do so. The group’s mandate had been voluntary and its chair was gone. The station asked who had authority to end it. The answer was that no one had authority to continue in Nia’s name. The page remained available, but the meeting room would not be booked again.

The decision was posted on a Friday. On Monday, the library’s calendar showed the room reserved for an Aster Institute public event. No title or speaker was listed, only a time and a capacity limit. The reservation was not made by the chronology group, and the library would not disclose the organizer before the event’s public notice.
