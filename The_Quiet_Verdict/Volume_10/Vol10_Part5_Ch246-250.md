# Chapter 246: The Seat

---

The first public report after the transfer student began classes did not give a name. It gave a date, an aggregate count, and a correction to the term’s opening roster. The school said the student had joined under the same privacy rules as every other student. The notice did not say that the student had returned.

Readers made the word anyway. They fitted the new count to the library registration and the old transport notice. Each fit required a different assumption: that the two appearances belonged to one person, that the public identification after the collision had been wrong, and that a school would not have a reason to protect an ordinary transfer student’s name. None of those assumptions appeared in the enrollment notice.

I had no roster. My sources were the public calendar and the school’s versioned updates. I could follow the term, but not the student through it. The official page showed where the class met and which policy applied. It did not show the desks or the people sitting in them.

Aster’s student council published the first class review after the handbook revisions took effect. Totals were grouped by class; individual scores remained protected. Students could ask for their own calculations and appeal an error before the posted deadline. The notice included the rule version, the date of the count, and the person responsible for receiving corrections. It was a small system, but it left fewer places for a number to pretend it had no author.

The first appeal changed one class total by a point. The review log said a duplicate line had been removed. It did not name the student who spotted it. Another class asked that its own figures be checked by a second group. The school recorded the request, the review, and the correction together. No one had to take another student’s word for which version had been used.

Hoshina’s old article about the ranking system was linked from the handbook’s history page. The school did not describe the link as an apology. A reader added a comment saying the new method proved the past had been repaired. The editor rejected the sentence as unsupported. The method could keep future totals open to review. It could not change the years in which students had been ranked without seeing the rules.

The transfer review appeared at the bottom of the published count as a completed administrative entry. It still gave no class, name, or previous enrollment. A question submitted to the school asked whether the student had sat in the same room before. The registrar replied that the published record did not identify the student and the school would not disclose an individual schedule.

That answer did not deny the return. It did not confirm it. I left the question and answer beside each other in my notes, without drawing a line between them.

At the end of the term, the public minutes recorded a quiet study room reserved for graduates. The room had one table, several chairs, and no posted attendee list. The reservation was private, but the policy was ordinary. Aster no longer published every name merely because a form had collected one.

The record ended with the room’s closing time and the staff member who returned its key. It did not record who had used the quiet seat. A booking was not a person. A count was not a face. Still, the room had been prepared for someone to sit there.

The published version did not name the graduate who had made the reservation. The archive’s custodian confirmed the room had been opened at the scheduled time and locked after it. A public log showed the key returned; it did not keep the visitor’s name. That was enough to correct one rumor, which had claimed the booking was never used. It was not enough to say who had used it. The correction changed a sentence, not the range of what could be known.

The building’s ordinary records contained more details than the public notice: times for opening and closing, a maintenance check, a chair count. None of those fields described the people who sat down. When a reader requested the full room log, the library released the retention policy instead. It said visitor information was not collected for unreserved study use. There was no hidden attendance list to request. The absence came from how the room worked, not from a clerk deciding to withhold a name later.

Aster’s term-end announcement called the transfer student’s progress satisfactory. The phrase was printed beside the same protected-record notice used for every individual student. I checked the previous year’s calendar and found the template unchanged. A person had joined the class; the document did not say when that person had first attended the school, which credits had transferred, or whether there had been any interruption. Each of those details stayed in the registrar’s file.

An alumnus wrote to the paper asking whether the returning student had graduated. The question assumed both that the student had attended before and that the unnamed graduate was the same person. Hoshina’s successor published the inquiry as an example of what the public record could not answer, not as a lead. The registrar repeated the policy. The reporter linked the school’s reply and did not ask the office to make an exception.

I added the correction to the source note. The reservation had been used; the visitor remained unidentified. It was the smallest possible change to my account, and one that kept a rumor from becoming an apparent fact.

The library confirmed the distinction in its annual report. Reservations were counted to plan staff hours; visitors were not asked to sign in. A booking could show that the space had been requested and used. It could not, by itself, tell the next reader who had entered. The report did not call the blank an omission. It described a deliberate choice not to collect names for an ordinary study room.

In the class review, a student asked whether the new privacy policy also made mistakes harder to challenge. The board’s reply linked the individual appeal route and the public version history. A student could inspect their own record, request a correction, and receive a dated explanation. The public would see that the policy had been used without seeing the private file. The separation was not perfect knowledge. It was a way to let a person challenge a decision without turning their name into a public exhibit.

I used the words “the room had been used” because the library’s log said it had. I did not write “the attendee had returned.” Those phrases sounded close when read quickly. They were not supported by the same source, and they did not carry the same claim.

A short feature in the alumni bulletin described the opening of the study space. It thanked the staff for keeping the booking process simple and noted that graduates could use the room without joining an event. There was no photograph. The bulletin explained that the school did not publish attendance because visitors had not agreed to be identified. The policy was not written for the one reservation people had noticed; it had applied before anyone connected the room to the old case.

The registrar’s office also corrected a public count after a student withdrew. The original total remained available with a dated annotation explaining the change. The correction did not say which person had left or why. A reader asked whether that meant the unnamed transfer student was no longer enrolled. The office replied that the count represented the whole school and could not be used to infer an individual’s status. I added the public answer, then removed a sentence that had treated the two notices as if they referred to the same person.

# Chapter 247: The Rule Version

---

The school published the examination schedule three weeks before the first paper was set. Alongside it appeared a table of the rules that would apply: how absences were counted, how a question could be challenged, and which version of the assessment guide controlled if a correction was made. Students could download the current guide and the one it replaced.

The notice had a revision history. The first entry added the version number. The second corrected a date. The third recorded that the appeal period began after the results were posted, not after the last examination. Each change had an office attached to it. No person was named when the account belonged to a shared staff login.

One anonymous comment asked why Aster kept the older rules. The registrar answered that a correction needed a comparison. The old version showed what had been available when a decision was made; the new version showed what would apply next. The school did not say that keeping a record made every decision fair. It said a student should be able to ask which rule had been used.

The examinations proceeded without a public incident. The school issued one correction to a room assignment and one to a printed start time. Both changes remained visible in the public log. A student who had traveled to the wrong building received an extra sitting under the published accommodation rule. The announcement did not identify the student or ask the public to judge whether the accommodation was deserved.

After the papers were marked, the registrar released a summary. It gave the number of students who had completed each requirement and the number of appeals received. It did not publish the students’ names or the scores. One review remained open when the summary first appeared. A revised copy later showed that it had been closed; a note explained that the review had ended but did not publish its subject.

I could not tell whether the transfer student had appealed, taken the same exams, or been counted in the summary. The school’s total had changed for ordinary reasons across the year. It had admitted students, recorded departures, and corrected its arithmetic. A single difference could not be assigned to the person the rumors wanted.

Kagami posted a short comment after a reporter asked whether the revised total proved someone had returned. He said the number supported one fact: the number had changed. It did not say who had been counted or why. The reporter quoted that sentence and removed the one that followed, where Kagami said that the school’s own notice should be read with it.

Hoshina’s replacement restored the context in a correction. She did not add a guess about the unnamed student. The correction remained attached to the article instead of replacing it. Readers complained that the old wording was still visible. The editor answered that hiding the mistake would make it harder to see what had been corrected.

Aster’s board adopted the practice for the next academic year. Public forms would carry a version number, and material corrections would include a date and a source. Individual records would remain private. The rule was not a window into the student files. It was a way to mark the boundary between the record the public could inspect and the record it could not.

The final summary used the same language as the earlier notice: one transfer review had been completed. No name followed it. A hundred readers sent the registrar a version of the same question. The answer stayed the same because the source had not changed.

For the first time, the public could compare the unanswered question with the exact wording of the rule that protected it. Neither one supplied a name.

Before the examinations, the student board held a public demonstration of the new appeal form. A teacher entered a sample score, showed the version of the rubric, and asked a student representative to check the calculation. The representative found that one line had been counted twice. The teacher did not delete the original entry. She marked the duplicate, linked the correction, and printed a clean copy whose history remained available beside it.

The demonstration was not an endorsement of every decision Aster had made. One student asked why only a student could inspect their own detailed score, while class totals could be compared publicly. The principal said privacy and review served different purposes. Another student replied that the distinction could still leave a class unable to challenge a pattern. The minutes preserved both statements and listed a further review date. The policy was not settled by the fact that it had been published.

A reporter asked if the new procedure came from the unnamed transfer student. The school did not answer questions about individual students. The public minutes attributed the proposal to the board committee and identified the meeting in which it passed. No private request, author’s note, or student file supported the reporter’s guess. I left the question out of my account. Repetition would have given it a weight it had not earned.

During the term, the committee changed a form to add the date beside the rule version. The minutes showed that the change came after a student found two versions online with identical filenames. The school labeled both documents with distinct dates, then updated its links. This was the kind of repair a reader could verify. It did not require the reader to know who first noticed the problem.

At the end of the year the board issued a short evaluation. The public tables showed the number of appeals, how many resulted in a correction, and how many were still under review. They did not claim that a low appeal count meant the system had become fair. The board said that students should have more than one route to question a result. The statement included the committee’s unresolved objection and promised another review the next year.

The old rule remained in the archive, exactly as it had been used at the time. The new rule sat beside it. A student could read both and decide whether the change answered the earlier problem. I could show the two pages, the dates, and the recorded vote. I could not tell a reader what judgment to make between them.

The final examination notice listed the office that would receive appeals and the date on which the review window closed. A student could challenge an arithmetic error without making another student’s score public. The board’s posted procedure said the reply had to identify the rule version used and state whether the correction changed the result. A denied appeal also required a reason. The public could see that the process existed; it could not see the protected student’s submission.

The first review used the process exactly as written. A student challenged a duplicated line. The teacher confirmed the error, the registrar changed the total, and the original remained in the history. The student’s name was not posted. The record described the correction and its effect. No one had to infer that a number changed because a particular person had secretly returned.

Aster’s annual report called the test a successful first year. The word “successful” belonged to the board’s evaluation, not to a universal finding. The report listed the number of requests, corrections, and decisions still open. An appendix noted that two appeals had been withdrawn before review. It did not describe why. I kept the label with its source and left the claim open to readers.

The committee reviewed the appeal log before the term ended. Several students had asked for arithmetic checks; fewer had challenged the rule itself. The board did not take the difference as proof that the policy was accepted. A student representative said that some disagreements were settled privately, some students lacked time to appeal, and others did not believe a written request would change anything. The minutes attributed the statement to the representative and recorded that the board had asked for another review after the next term.

The public evaluation included a response from the registrar. The office said the form could make a decision traceable, but could not make the decision correct by itself. An appeal still depended on someone checking the source material and explaining the result. The board retained the comment beside the success measure rather than smoothing the two into a single conclusion.

Aster’s committee also approved a procedure for students who could not meet the standard appeal deadline. The procedure named the circumstances under which an extension could be requested and who would decide it. It did not publish examples involving individual students. A notice said the first review would occur after one year, when the school could compare how the exception had been used against the reasons students had requested it.

# Chapter 248: The Last Bell

---

The commencement program listed the speakers, the order of the ceremony, and the graduates who had chosen to be named. Several lines were omitted under the school’s ordinary privacy policy. The printed copy said so. It did not explain which omission belonged to which graduate, or whether a person had chosen privacy, missed the deadline, or changed their mind after the list had gone to press.

The school’s public stream showed the stage. It did not show the student seats as they filled. The principal read a statement about the appeal rules and the duty to correct an error without erasing the earlier record. The text was prepared in advance. Aster’s archive showed the same paragraph in a draft from the previous year, before the transfer notice existed.

A graduate received a certificate after the main procession. The published correction said the graduate had asked for that arrangement. No name was spoken in the public stream. The school confirmed the request, then declined to identify the graduate. A fan account called it a secret graduation. The registrar said it was a private handoff under the same process offered to any student who asked.

The ceremony’s seating plan circulated online the next morning. A narrow gap at the end of one row was marked as the place where a chair could be turned for an accessible route. Users circled it and called it the quiet seat. The fire plan showed the gap had been there from the first approved version. The school published both copies. The later photograph did not show a person leaving it.

Aster’s revised handbook was handed to the new class. Its first page showed the version number and the date of adoption. The page also linked to the former versions. No signature appeared on the policy. The board had approved it; the school’s staff had put it into practice. The public index gave both facts and did not assign the change to an unnamed student.

The class totals were posted after the ceremony. They were aggregated, dated, and accompanied by an appeal route. The final calculation showed one correction made before publication. The original and revised copies were both available. The school had not made a flawless system. It had made one in which the public could see that a correction had happened without learning the private name behind it.

Kagami’s last note on the matter said that a record with a blank field did not tell him who had been withheld. It told him only what the author had decided not to publish. He did not name the graduate. He did not say he recognized anyone on the stage. His statement was linked to the same policy page as the program.

The archive’s temporary custodian asked whether the ceremony should be cross-referenced with Nia’s chronology. The answer was no. The old map concerned a plan’s source and custody. The graduation file concerned certificates and consent. No shared identifier joined them. The custodian recorded the decision and why the two records stayed separate.

After the stream ended, the public page showed the time the broadcast stopped. The camera had not followed the graduate who received the private certificate. The school’s final photograph showed rows of empty chairs facing the stage. The caption said the room had been cleared. It did not identify the last person to leave.

I could report that a graduate chose not to receive a certificate in the public procession. I could not call that choice an escape. I could report that the school’s transfer review was complete. I could not call it a return. Those events sat beside each other in the calendar, but proximity was not a source.

The last bell rang on the public schedule. Aster closed the ceremony page at the end of the day and left the corrected program in its archive. The next term would begin with the new rules in force. The record did not say who had first needed those rules, or who would take the quiet seat when the room opened again.

The private certificate handoff took place after the stream ended. The school later confirmed that the graduate had agreed to the arrangement and that the record had been filed under the usual graduation procedure. It did not say whether the graduate had walked in alone, whether anyone had been waiting outside, or what words had been exchanged. The notice concerned consent and delivery, not identity. A second request to publish the graduate’s name was declined under the same policy.

The student committee’s last report carried the names of members who had consented to publication. Two contributors remained listed only as reviewers. An online thread tried to compare their writing styles with the anonymous comment in the handbook history. The comparison had no original message to test against, only a line preserved under a shared account. The school said the account had been used by several staff. The committee did not invite guesses about which student had contributed.

At graduation, the principal thanked the students who had challenged the ranking process and the staff who had recorded its changes. The sentence had appeared in the prepared remarks a year earlier, in a shorter form. The revision log showed the later wording was approved by the communications office. Neither version named Nia or the unnamed transfer student. The applause in the stream came from a room full of people, not an identifiable individual.

After the ceremony, the archive deposited the handbook revision history with the public records. Its new index retained both versions and linked each correction to a date. The school’s archivist described that choice as a routine retention decision. It was not called a memorial. Aster’s process could hold a history without claiming that the people involved wanted to be remembered by it.

I read the graduate’s private-handoff notice beside the ceremony schedule. The schedule showed the main procession; the notice showed that a request had been honored. It did not say the graduate was missing, absent, or hiding. A private choice was not a clue simply because the public had not been invited to witness it.

The quiet study reservation returned to the library calendar for the summer. This time the notice explained that graduates could reserve the space for reading or completing records. The calendar had no attendee field. No journalist asked the library to provide a list. The reservation was no longer presented as a potential sighting; it was a room available under a published rule.

Aster’s next academic year began with the versioned handbook already on every public form. The change had survived a full cycle: request, revision, use, appeal, and correction. There was no final author to name. The credit remained with the committee and the institution that kept the policy in force.

The private ceremony request was processed before the final program went to print. The coordinator’s public correction said only that the graduate preferred a separate handoff. A reporter asked whether the graduate had rejected the school or the audience. The school declined to speculate about a student’s reasons. The graduate had asked for a different arrangement; the record established that the request was honored.

An old photo circulated beside the graduation image. Its caption claimed the same chair had appeared in both years, but the original files showed two different rooms. The images had looked similar after the captions joined them. Their sources did not.

The school’s archive attached the versions together so a reader could see that the wording had changed before publication. The older draft was not left as an unsourced rumor; it was marked as superseded and linked to the final copy.

The graduate did not ask the paper to disclose any name. The editor revised the figure and explained the counting method. The school’s next-year calendar showed the same quiet-study hours for graduates. No special booking was listed. The room remained available under the ordinary policy, with the same privacy notice that had applied the year before. A place can be kept open without being reserved for a particular person.

# Chapter 249: Natural Causes

---

Dr. Kreutz was found dead at home. The institute’s notice named him, stated that the authorities had determined the death was from natural causes, and gave the date on which the notice had been issued. No medical file or private account accompanied it.

The first report called him the man who had made the Monster. The headline did not appear in the institute notice. It came from an old description repeated so often that readers had begun to treat it as a finding. Kreutz had been the medical director at Lantern House. The public record had never established that he created every practice carried out there, or that every fragment later found belonged to him.

I had not seen his home or spoken to the people who were with him. The notice was the limit of what I could confirm. I copied its words into the source list with the authoring office and the date, then left the medical details blank. A cause stated by an authority was not permission to invent a final hour.

Hoshina’s earlier reporting remained online. She had distinguished Kreutz’s recollection from the clinic schedule, the schedule from a destruction certificate, and both from the missing records nobody had found. The new notice did not close those questions. It added one public fact about his death.

A reader asked the library whether the death had anything to do with the old transfer files. The library replied that it had no source linking them. Another asked whether the notice proved that the Mechanism had not been used. The library did not answer that question. Neither conclusion followed from the published medical line.

The archive staff entered the notice under public institutional statements. It was not added to Nia’s historical-plan map. Her group had ended its sessions, and the map’s remaining search paths had not been resolved. The staff did not reopen the map merely because a death made the old mystery feel unfinished.

At the publisher, the editor asked whether the book needed a new paragraph. It had already gone through its final proof. The book named Kreutz only in the public sources readers could identify and did not present a private death scene. The notice could be added to a later source index, but it could not change what the manuscript had known when it was written.

I declined to add a scene to the final chapter. No witness had authorized one. The public notice did not describe a final conversation, a last letter, or the contents of his desk. A silence in the record was not a document waiting for me to fill it.

The obituary listed his work, his appointments, and the people who had requested that their names not be printed. It did not describe him as a maker or a monster. A correction added that the institute had not released details beyond its notice. The obituary and the correction remained linked.

Readers still built endings around him. Some said his death settled the Lantern House case; some said it proved there had been a cover-up. Neither group had a source that moved the notice beyond the fact it stated. Kreutz could no longer give a new account, but the records that survived him did not become more certain.

I filed the notice, checked that the date was correct, and closed the page. In the public index, his name now sat beside a line that said what the authorities had reported. Nothing on the page said what he had known, what he had done, or what he might have explained.

Kreutz’s name had been absent from the public schedule for months. The institute notice said only that he had died and that the determination was natural causes. It did not publish whether he had been under medical care or who had notified the institute. A reporter asked for the examiner’s name. The institute declined to release any detail beyond its statement. The refusal did not make the notice false; it marked the limit of what had been offered to the public.

The first obituary described his appointments and his work with the clinic. A former colleague asked the paper to correct a phrase that suggested Kreutz had founded Lantern House. The record showed he had served there as medical director, not that he had established the institution. The editor changed the line and linked the correction. One careful distinction changed the story more than the dramatic headline had.

The school’s archivist checked the old transfer index for Kreutz’s name because a reader had submitted a question about his files. The search was limited to the public index. It found a mention of his role in a clinic retention schedule, but no record connecting his death to the missing plan. The archivist published the scope of the search and did not open restricted material. The result meant that one bounded search found no link; it did not prove no link existed elsewhere.

A second question asked whether Kreutz had told anyone the missing registry name before his death. The public record contained his claim that he recognized the abraded entry, but the protected comparison had been withdrawn before he spoke a name. No neutral officer had authenticated the claim. The institute’s notice did not change that. The name remained unknown in the public archive.

I had once written that the unanswered name had followed him. I removed the line. It made the absence sound like an object he had carried, when I had no source for his thoughts or private knowledge. The revised paragraph said only that a comparison had been proposed, then withdrawn. It ended at the point where the record ended.

The correction log listed the obituary change, the bounded search, and the unanswered question as three different entries. Readers could see which came from the paper, which from the archive, and which remained a question. No line turned the public notice into an explanation of Lantern House.

The institute placed Kreutz’s published works in a memorial catalog. The catalog gave dates and titles, not annotations or private correspondence. A reader suggested that the last book should be read as a confession. The publisher’s note said the text had been released years before the current inquiry and did not refer to the missing records. The catalog kept the book available without asserting that interpretation.

I copied the notice into the source index and stopped there. The man who might have recognized a name could no longer answer the proposed question. The surviving documents could not answer it for him.

The institute’s notice did not mention an investigation or a criminal inquiry. A reporter asked the local office whether anyone had requested a formal review. The office said it would not discuss private medical records. That answer did not establish that a review had happened or that it had not. The obituary’s editor removed the sentence “no inquiry followed” and replaced it with “the institute released no further detail.”

A second correction concerned Kreutz’s title. The first memorial draft called him the founder of Lantern House. The school archive named him medical director at a later date. The paper revised the line and linked the appointment notice. The correction narrowed his documented role without resolving what he might have known about the people who passed through the house.

The old notebook remained an object of rumor. No public inventory identified it among Kreutz’s effects, and no archive had announced its receipt. I wrote neither that he possessed it nor that he did not. A page that could not be checked could not settle the count of uses.

The library’s index manager declined to merge the obituary with the Lantern House entry. A shared name was not a shared source path. Kreutz’s public appointment record belonged to the institution’s history; the notice of his death belonged to its current announcements. The old case materials remained under their separate access conditions. The manager recorded the rejected cross-reference so the next researcher would know the question had been asked and why the records stayed apart.

A memorial page invited former colleagues to submit an approved recollection. Several did. Their memories did not agree on what Kreutz had been like in private. The editor published each account with its author and date, and did not combine them into one definitive portrait. A colleague who chose not to write was not listed by name. The choice not to contribute was not treated as evidence of guilt or silence.

One recollection mentioned that Kreutz kept careful notes. It did not identify a notebook, describe any page, or say that the notes had survived him. I did not use the sentence as a link to the Mechanism. The word “notebook” was common; resemblance alone could not authenticate an object.

# Chapter 250: The Thriller

---

Kuze Minato’s manuscript appeared under a publisher’s label that read “psychological thriller.” The cover showed a table, an empty chair, and a closed notebook. It did not show a face or reproduce Nia’s map.

The publisher’s note said the book drew on public records and statements shared with permission. It also said that reconstructed scenes were not transcripts. No private placement file, confidential intake, medical record, sealed student index, or owner-restricted paper appeared in the edition. The note had been read by the people whose statements it quoted. It did not claim that every event in the story had been independently verified.

Readers looked for the missing plan. They compared the fictional town with the old municipal register and the school’s north wing. The book changed the parcel codes and combined several dates. One review said the changes were a cipher. Another said they proved the author had seen the original map. The publisher replied that the novel was fiction and that no original map had been reproduced.

I watched the launch discussion from my desk. The moderator asked whether I had been present at the library meeting where Nia died. I said I had not. I had used approved minutes, public notices, and statements authorized by their authors. The story did not give me access to an interview or a medical file that the public had never seen.

A second question asked whether the attendee who claimed to be Ren had killed Nia. I said the public record did not establish a cause of death, and the attendee’s identity had not been authenticated. The room’s witnesses had reported what they saw from their own positions. Their accounts were not a continuous recording of every movement or an expert finding about cause.

The moderator asked whether that made the novel incomplete. I said a source can be incomplete and still be worth preserving. The attendee’s claim was part of the record. So was the official identification after the transport collision. Neither document erased the other, and the novel could not choose between them without evidence.

The last section of the book printed the stated rules of the Mechanism as they had appeared in the earlier account. A reader asked if I believed those rules were real. I said they were a claim in the manuscript. I had never seen the ledger. The text’s exact intervals did not give me a way to test them.

The moderator moved to another question. The stream retained my answer in its transcript. A clipping account reposted the question without the words that followed and titled it “Kuze refuses to deny the notebook.” The publisher linked the full exchange and did not ask the account to remove its post.

That evening I checked the source note one last time. The book had made events easier to read by putting them in order. It had not made the gaps disappear. I understood how a reader could mistake an orderly page for an answer. The page did not correct them on its own.

The first printing sold through its planned run. A second edition was announced with the same cover and an expanded source note. The note named the public documents, separated reported claims from verified records, and said that the story did not investigate Nia’s death. Nothing in the revision claimed a source the first edition lacked.

The title remained the same. I did not write an author’s afterword. The people who had agreed to speak had given statements with limits; the people who had kept their names private had not given me permission to turn that choice into a clue.

Before the second edition closed, the publisher received a request to stream a school records demonstration. The public program said it concerned the handbook’s version history. The school’s notice described the format, the camera frame, and the rules for identifying participants. It did not announce a guest.

The first review said the story had “solved the academy.” The review’s summary confused the placement conviction with the old source-plan inquiry. The publisher sent a correction request with links to the two sections of the book. The reviewer amended the passage, saying the court had decided the narrow charge about Ren’s signed instruction, not who created the exercise or whether a student received a set.

A second review quoted Nia’s public minutes without the sentence that limited what she had witnessed. The editor contacted the paper, and the correction restored the full quote. Nia had not authorized the book to speak on her behalf. Her words appeared only in the form she had approved before her death. The publisher left the correction beside the review, not hidden in a later edition.

The source index at the end of the book divided entries into public records, attributed statements, and materials that had been described but not independently seen. It did not print the private counterfoil serial. It did not reproduce the sealed card. Each category explained what the manuscript could and could not say. Several readers called the index an invitation to investigate. The publisher clarified that access remained subject to the owners’ permission and the institutions’ rules.

At the launch, I was asked why the narrative sometimes described rooms where I had never been. I explained that the scenes were reconstructions, not eyewitness testimony. The notes identified the public minutes or authorized statements that supported them. Where accounts disagreed, the text kept the disagreement. The form of the book did not grant me new evidence.

One reader asked if I had met the man who used Ren Aizawa’s name. I said I had not. I had read the authorized statements and the published notices. The story showed an attendee claim a name in a public room, and later reported the school’s separate enrollment update. It did not establish that the signer and the transfer student were the same person.

A moderator asked whether the novel’s final chapter was intended to point toward a sequel. The publisher said the contract described a complete story. I said that an unanswered record did not automatically mean a plot remained unfinished. Some questions belonged to people who had not given permission to answer them in public.

Before the second printing, the art team proposed replacing the closed notebook on the cover with a face. The publisher rejected the change. The book did not verify who had held the notebook, or whether the attendee’s claim was true. A faceless cover was not a clue; it was an accurate account of what the public image could show.

The new edition added the corrections and a clearer note about source limits. It did not add a name to the library registration or a cause to Nia’s death. The story had reached its printed end. The record had not reached a verdict on every question.

The publisher’s source editor tested every quotation against the permission log. One statement had been approved for paraphrase but not direct quotation. We replaced the sentence and added the change to the revision history. The speaker had not withdrawn the account; the manuscript had exceeded the scope of permission. The correction belonged to the book’s process, not to a hidden clue.

The note also explained that public documents did not share one chain of custody. A court judgment, a school calendar, a library minute, and an attributed recollection could each be accurate within its own scope without establishing a connection to the next. Readers could compare them, but a matching date or repeated name did not make them a single file.

A review described that distinction as “the book refusing to choose.” The publisher linked the court’s narrow finding and the still-open death inquiry. Neither record decided the archive’s missing-plan question. The choice not to join them was not uncertainty about the wording. It was fidelity to the sources.

A copy of the book reached the school library. The librarian cataloged it as fiction and placed the source note in the record. A student asked whether the academy in the story was Aster. The catalog description said that names and institutions had been changed and that the novel did not document the school’s actual private records. The librarian did not remove the book or present it as a case file.

The public discussion generated a list of suggested sources. Some were links to documents already in the book; others led to closed databases or private accounts. The publisher’s editor checked the list and removed links that required access the readers had not been granted. The remaining references led to public pages, not to private witnesses. An open web address was not permission to publish someone else’s information.

The moderator read one last question: whether a story could be honest if its ending still left identities unresolved. I said that the story could disclose what its sources established and preserve what they did not. It could not turn a reader’s wish for certainty into a new record. The moderator thanked the audience, and the stream ended on the title page.
