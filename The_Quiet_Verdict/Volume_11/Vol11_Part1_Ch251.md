# Chapter 251: The Signature

---

The stream began with the school’s current handbook and the version that had preceded it. A clerk placed both copies on a table and read their publication dates. The camera showed the papers, the clerk, and the chair across from her. It did not show the doors or the people watching from outside the frame.

The demonstration was ordinary. The clerk pointed to a correction in the newer edition, then opened the public revision history. The earlier wording remained visible. A change note named the school office that had approved the revision. The note did not name the person who had first asked the school to preserve its prior version.

A participant sat down after the clerk invited questions. The person asked for the blank field on the demonstration form. The clerk explained that a signer could use a public name or decline to sign. The form was not a student record, and the stream would show only the parts the signer agreed to publish.

The person said the school had once asked students to accept a number without showing them the rule. The clerk replied that the old system was part of the archived record and that the new process did not erase it. Neither spoke about the unnamed transfer student. The stream did not identify the participant beyond the name he chose to give.

“Ren Aizawa,” he said.

The caption read: “The participant gives the name Ren Aizawa.” It did not call him the person officially identified after the transport collision. It did not authenticate the claim made at the library. The school’s moderator said the broadcast could preserve a statement and a signature, but not decide a question that belonged to another record.

The participant turned the demonstration form toward the camera. He said the rules of the Mechanism had been written by him. The intervals, the required face, the handwriting, the limits on copying: he had made them precise because people trusted a rule that looked precise. He said the audience had read the rules as evidence, when the page had shown only that someone had written them down.

He said the Mechanism had been used exactly three times. One use was the death in the early account. A second was the collapse that readers had timed from records of Hara’s last day. The third had not appeared in the story. He said he had arranged every other death without the notebook.

The clerk did not confirm the statement. No ledger was on the table. The stream displayed no page that could verify how many times the Mechanism had been used, and no source in my possession established the speaker’s identity. I watched the public broadcast. I could record what he claimed and what the camera showed. I could not certify what was outside its frame.

The moderator asked whether the speaker wanted to sign the form. He said yes. The pen touched the blank line. The camera held its position while he wrote, then the clerk asked him to read the name aloud so the transcript would match the page.

He looked at the camera and spelled it once. The captioner checked the spelling against the visible signature. The clerk entered the approved name into the public version history. No one added a birthplace, a student number, or a family record. The demonstration ended with the two handbook versions still open side by side.

In my notes, I kept three lines separate: the participant’s name claim; his statements about the rules and their use; and the signature shown on the school’s live record. The broadcast connected them to one act of speaking and signing. It did not open the private files that could connect them to anything else.

The clerk turned the signature page toward the camera. The moderator said the stream was live, that the participant could withdraw permission before the page entered the public archive, and that the school would not use the form to alter a protected student file. The participant listened, then asked that the signature be shown as written. The clerk repeated the terms in the same words used for every public records demonstration.

The participant gave the name Ren Aizawa. It was the same name that had appeared on the public library register and in the reports of the transport collision. The school’s moderator did not reconcile those records. No document on the table identified the participant with a school number or a protected file. The claim was spoken on camera; the source of the claim was still the speaker.

He explained that the notebook’s rules had been his. The intervals had been chosen to make a reader count. The condition about seeing a face had been written so a reader would treat an ordinary name as insufficient. The ban on copying made the ledger seem singular. The details were instructions in the story he had told, not independent proof that an object or power existed as described.

He said that only three uses had occurred. The first had been described early. The second was the timing around Hara’s collapse. The third was absent from every published chapter. He said the other deaths had been produced by people, accidents, and choices that did not require the Mechanism. The camera showed him speaking. It did not show a page listing the three uses, or a record of the deaths he said he had arranged.

A question appeared on the stream’s public caption feed: if he had written the rules, why had he never corrected the account? He answered that a correction would have made readers look for an author. He had wanted them to look at the numbers. The moderator said the question period was not an interrogation and that the school could not verify a claim about a private history. The answer remained in the public recording as a statement, not as a finding.

The signature field required a name. He asked whether the school would accept a name not previously on its public forms. The clerk said the records demonstration accepted a participant’s chosen public signature. It did not certify a legal identity. He said that was enough. He placed the pen on the line, lifted it, and began again when the first stroke caught in the paper.

When the final letters were complete, the clerk waited for permission to display them. The participant nodded. The camera zoomed only as far as the table allowed. The moderator read each letter slowly for the captioner. The archive’s public copy would retain the form and the broadcast link under the participant’s chosen name. The school would not add the signer to a student roster on the basis of the demonstration.

The record included the date, the video’s public checksum, the school office that hosted it, and the sentence authorizing publication. It did not include any of the claims about the notebook as verified facts. A separate caption note identified them as statements made by the participant. The version history preserved the caption edits, including the change from “Ren Aizawa signs” to “participant gives the name Ren Aizawa and signs.”

A reporter asked if the signature proved the man had survived the collision. The school said the demonstration had no role in that inquiry. The reporter asked if it proved the attendee at the library had been Ren. The school said it could not authenticate that link. The answers appeared beneath the stream in the same order as the questions. No one deleted them after the broadcast ended.

I watched the archive copy again, not for a hidden image but to check the caption against the recording. The participant said he had written the rules. The clerk read the name the signer had given. The signature was visible. Everything beyond that belonged to records I did not have.

The name at the end of the page was not a deduction from the blanks. It was the name the signer had chosen to place where the public could read it. The archive kept his claim, his permission, and its own limits together.

The school’s notice beneath the demonstration described what the public archive retained: the submitted form, its authorized display, a transcript, and a correction history. A participant could request that a future republication be limited, but the original notice of consent would remain in the audit record. The procedure did not create a legal identity file. It documented a choice to speak in that room and to let the chosen signature be seen.

A viewer later wrote that the letters matched a name in an old school flower list. The list was not part of the broadcast and did not identify the signer. Another viewer found the school’s emblem and compared its petals with the title card. Those comparisons were readers’ interpretations. The official stream offered one connection: the signer said the name had been given before the institute adopted the flower. The record showed that he made that assertion. It did not show a birth certificate or a founding document that could settle its direction.

The moderator asked why the signer wanted the name entered now. He replied that the book had left a blank where readers could place their own answer. He wanted to change the page without changing the archive. He did not ask the school to erase the transport notice or alter the library’s account. The moderator read the scope back to him, and he confirmed it. The form would contain the chosen signature, and nothing else about the protected records would be updated.

A copy editor asked whether the last line should call him by the name he had signed. I retained the transcript’s attribution in the final paragraph, then let the visible letters carry the reveal. The book did not certify the participant. It showed the reader the name he chose to place before them.

The public record retained the earlier editions of the handbook, the demonstration form, and the transcript. Its final revision note said only that a participant’s chosen signature had been added with permission. The page did not label the event a confession or an identification. It recorded a person’s public act, exactly as the school had promised. You were given the name at last: SHION.
